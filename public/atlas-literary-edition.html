HOW TO ENTER

This is not a test of belief.

Begin anywhere. Listen before reading when you can. The literary passages offer images, arguments, and questions; they are not claims that KLAAT has already produced measurable neurological or therapeutic effects. Every field links to its corresponding research chapter, where the evidence and its limits can be examined.

The drawings are conceptual scores for attention—not spectrograms, physiological traces, or records of experiments. If an image suggests an experience you have never had, you are not required to have it.

There is no prescribed response. There is only the next interval.

TERRITORY I / 09

THE HELD FIELD

INTERVAL 01 / NINE

A ROOM BEFORE A NOTE

Before the first breath, the room is already making decisions. It has dimensions, surfaces, places where bass will gather and places where it will almost disappear. Nothing here is mystical. Nothing here is neutral.

The player enters carrying two instruments. The listener enters carrying memory. When the first sound begins, all three histories become audible in different ways.

FIELD NOTE / 01

The room has no center until someone begins to listen. A surface becomes a boundary; a boundary becomes an echo; an echo becomes evidence that the sound has traveled somewhere the player cannot reach.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 01 OF 28

The Object That Answers

Before the breath, the agave is only a hollow object. When air enters, the instrument answers in many voices: fundamental, harmonics, friction, and the irregular effort of a human mouth. The ear gathers those movements and calls them one note.

Resonance describes physical response, not a universal healing code. C-sharp belongs to Amastacia's identity; it does not possess a demonstrated neurological privilege. A room reinforces some tones and diminishes others. The mystery is not a secret frequency. It is the encounter.

A listener may call a sound resonant because it feels personally meaningful. That is a valuable description of the listener's experience, but it does not identify a resonant frequency in the listener's body. The same word crosses two domains; the Atlas must mark the crossing rather than pretend there was none.

This distinction is the beginning of a language. One mark for what was played. Another for what was measured. A third for what was felt. Their correspondence is a question, not an assumption.

FIELD NOTE / 02

A single note seems to offer certainty. Look closer and it fractures into relationships: pressure and resistance, vibration and reflection, a fundamental carrying a changing company of overtones.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 02 OF 28

The False Singular

A drone has harmonics; a drum has a transient; a beat has a rate. These are different kinds of measurements. Calling all of them frequencies conceals the distinctions that make the music intelligible.

A number may be correct and still answer the wrong question. KLAAT begins by listening for the whole event, not worshipping a single value.

The didgeridoo's audible pitch is not an inventory of everything it emits. Formants can emphasize groups of harmonics; roughness can complicate the surface; the room can redistribute what survives. To describe the result as one number is to discard the relationships that make it recognizable.

A diagram of the sound should therefore look less like a pin driven through a page and more like a field of unequal lines. The lines belong together, but none can stand in for all the others.

FIELD NOTE / 03

Stand in one place and call the sound enormous. Move a few steps and find that part of it has vanished. The walls have not changed their minds; the geometry has simply revealed another answer.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 03 OF 28

The Walls Are Listening

Walk across a room while the drone continues. A tone swells, then thins. The player may have changed nothing. The air has arranged itself differently around the listener.

The room is neither an empty container nor a single acoustic position. It returns, absorbs, and redistributes sound. A corner, a chair, a body, and a microphone do not inhabit identical versions of the performance.

In a room, sound reflects and interferes. A sustained low component may be strong in one position and weak a short distance away. The listener moving through that field is not merely changing their viewpoint; they are changing the signal reaching their ears.

The Atlas needs multiple listening positions because no single microphone can certify what an entire audience heard. The room is distributed, and so is the evidence.

TERRITORY II / 09

THE LIVING LINE

INTERVAL 02 / NINE

THE THREAD THAT HOLDS

If the first territory asks where sound exists, the second asks how something can remain itself while constantly changing.

Amastacia does not maintain continuity by freezing the world. The breath renews the field again and again. Its apparent stillness is an achievement of motion.

FIELD NOTE / 04

The player holds the drone, but no moment of the drone is exactly held. The breath works in secret; the harmonics travel across a surface the listener cannot see.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 04 OF 28

A Sound That Refuses to Stand Still

Amastacia holds a note long enough for the ear to mistake it for a background. Inside that steadiness, breath, lips, tongue, and throat keep changing its harmonic balance.

Continuity is not the absence of motion. It is the thread that makes motion perceptible. The drone becomes a place to return to without ever becoming the same place twice.

Listen to a sustained drone for the instant at which you stop hearing its beginning. That transition is difficult to locate. The tone has become a condition under which later sounds occur, even as its harmonics keep shifting.

A continuous sound can carry a history without repeating a fixed state. The living line is not a straight line. It is a line whose identity survives its motion.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 4 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 05

At the edge of every sustained sound is a body working. The slight rasp, the uneven breath, the almost-hidden correction: these are not interruptions to expression but some of its most legible marks.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 05 OF 28

The Human Edge

A machine can sustain a tone. The player negotiates with the instrument through breath, muscular effort, and learned coordination. A slight roughness can expose the labor inside the sound.

Intensity is not the same as volume, and imperfection is not always an error. The human edge gives the listener evidence that someone is there.

The lips and cheeks are not invisible machinery. Their fatigue, control, and adjustments shape the signal. The body is not merely producing an acoustic specimen; it is performing a negotiated act that cannot be wholly separated from its technique.

Yet the audience cannot reliably infer the player's effort from timbre alone. A rough edge may be intentional, incidental, or caused by recording. A good atlas marks what it cannot know from listening.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 5 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 06

Something disappears beneath a strike. Something else returns when the strike has faded. The ear carries continuity across gaps the microphone may plainly record.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 06 OF 28

The Sound That Survives

A drum strike may briefly hide the drone. A small speaker may remove its lowest energy. Yet the listener may still recognize the field as continuous.

Perception can preserve identity when parts of the signal are missing. Recognition, however, is not proof that the same bodily experience survives. KLAAT must learn what can disappear without taking the whole with it.

An interruption in the physical signal is not always heard as a break in the musical field. The ear can group events across masking and silence. That grouping is a perceptual achievement, not proof that the missing energy remained present.

To investigate what survives, compare the same performance across listening conditions and ask for descriptions before naming the features you expect listeners to notice.

TERRITORY III / 09

THE UNEQUAL CLOCKS

INTERVAL 03 / NINE

A MARK IN THE FIELD

Ankora arrives without asking permission from the drone. Its first strike makes a before and an after; its return gives the ear something to anticipate.

Here the Atlas acquires a second dimension. Sound has occupied space. Now it begins to divide time.

FIELD NOTE / 07

One impact makes a mark. Two create a distance. Three begin to establish a rule the player never announced and the listener may never consciously name.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 07 OF 28

The Count Between Strikes

The first strike divides the drone. The second gives that division a distance. By the third, the listener may begin waiting for the fourth.

The brain organizes events into beats and groupings, drawing on expectation rather than merely counting impacts. Ankora does not only mark time; it gives the listener material from which to build it.

Tempo names one rate; subdivision introduces events within it; meter organizes relative emphasis. The apparent density of a passage can increase without any corresponding change to its underlying beat.

When Ankora accelerates the surface while preserving a recognizable anchor, the listener may hear urgency without hearing a new tempo. That possibility should be tested, not declared a universal effect.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 7 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 08

Silence becomes articulate only after the ear has learned what ought to fill it. An absent strike can divide a performance more sharply than a louder one.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 08 OF 28

The Event That Did Not Occur

A missing drum strike can become an event in the listener's mind. The hand remains raised. The expected impact never comes. The silence has acquired a shape.

Surprise depends on what repetition has taught us to expect. A new sound need not surprise; an absent sound need not be empty. Sometimes the strongest event is the one that did not happen.

A repeated figure gives the mind a model of what might follow. Remove an expected impact and the model becomes briefly visible: the listener knows what did not happen.

But absence is not equally striking to everyone. Someone who never formed the expectation may hear only a pause. The Atlas must ask who learned the pattern, and when.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 8 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 09

The body answers before it has formulated an opinion. Not always, not in everyone, and not for a reason that can be reduced to a single rhythmic number.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 09 OF 28

The Invitation to Move

A pattern invites a foot to tap. A shoulder makes a small agreement with the pulse. Yet no rhythm compels every body in the same way.

Groove depends on timing, complexity, context, and the listener. There is no magic amount of syncopation or human imperfection. Ankora offers movement; the listener decides whether to follow.

The body's tendency to move with a beat is not a mechanical switch. Familiarity, context, complexity, and personal history shape the invitation. A listener may experience a strong sense of groove without moving at all.

The difference between wanting to move and actually moving belongs in any careful account of rhythmic response.

TERRITORY IV / 09

THE BODY AS WITNESS

INTERVAL 04 / NINE

THE LISTENER HAS A SURFACE

The room is not the only thing that receives sound. The listener has ears, skin, contact with the ground, and a mind willing to bind unlike sensations into one event.

A sensation is not less real because its cause is uncertain. Nor is it more scientifically specific because the language used to describe it is vivid.

FIELD NOTE / 10

A sensation may arrive through the ear or through the soles of the feet. The mind does not label every pathway before assembling an experience.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 10 OF 28

Two Routes Into One Sensation

Low sound may reach the ears through air, while mechanical vibration reaches the body through floors and seats. These routes are related but not interchangeable.

A sensation in the chest is real as an experience. It does not establish that an organ is resonating at a particular frequency. To honor a sensation is not to invent its mechanism.

The low frequencies that reach the ear through air and the mechanical motions that reach skin or bone can coexist. They should not be conflated merely because the listener calls both vibration.

A controlled comparison of speakers, floor contact, and tactile sources would reveal more than asking whether the bass 'went into the body.'

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 10 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 11

The same floor carries different messages to different bodies. Contact is not an incidental detail; it is one of the conditions under which a vibration becomes perceptible.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 11 OF 28

The Floor Has Its Own Opinion

One patch of floor trembles; another hardly moves. Shoes, posture, contact, and structure change what reaches a listener.

The phrase 'the room vibrates' conceals a landscape of different physical interfaces. There is no single floor and no single body in the audience.

A seat can transmit mechanical motion; a shoe can damp it; a platform can emphasize it. Even within one venue, physical contact differs from listener to listener.

A field diagram that includes the body must include its contact points. Otherwise it mistakes a distributed interface for a universal sensation.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 11 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 12

Two signals meet in the listener and acquire a shared identity. Their unity is not located wholly in the speaker, the floor, or the nervous system, but in their encounter.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 12 OF 28

The Map Drawn From Within

When a drum strike and a bodily vibration arrive together, the mind may bind them into one event. When they disagree in time, the unity can dissolve.

Perception constructs coherence from different senses. That construction is not a fraud. The body does not merely receive a map of the sound; it helps draw the map.

The mind can combine sound and touch when their timing and character suggest a shared source. This binding helps create the sense that music occupies the body rather than merely arriving at it.

The Atlas should distinguish an observed correspondence between signals from a listener's felt unity. Both matter; they answer different questions.

TERRITORY V / 09

THE LISTENING MIND

INTERVAL 05 / NINE

THE INNER WEATHER

The ear can follow a repeated sound long after the mind stops counting it. Some people find a passage opening; others find a closed door.

The Atlas cannot tell a listener what to feel. It can describe conditions, record differences, and preserve the possibility of surprise.

FIELD NOTE / 13

Attention cannot be ordered into existence. A repeated sound offers a place to return, yet the listener may stay, drift, or leave without moving.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 13 OF 28

A Place to Put Attention

The drone stays. The drum returns. Between them, attention finds something to follow and somewhere to rest.

Absorption is not automatically trance, hypnosis, or neurological entrainment. These are distinct claims requiring distinct evidence. A listener who loses track of time has reported something important, but not its complete cause.

Repetition can provide an attentional anchor, but the anchor does not guarantee absorption. Someone may listen inwardly, become bored, notice the room, or lose the thread entirely.

Instead of asking whether the music induces trance, ask listeners where attention traveled, what they noticed, and whether their sense of time changed. The narrower questions are more illuminating.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 13 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 14

Familiarity has two faces. It can make a passage disappear through repetition, or make its smallest variations impossible to ignore.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 14 OF 28

The Familiar Becomes Strange

Repetition may make a pattern familiar enough that quieter details emerge: a changing overtone, a skin's decay, the difference between two nearly identical strikes.

Familiarity can deepen listening or exhaust it. The same passage may open for one listener and close for another. Duration is an opportunity, not a guarantee.

Novelty draws attention; familiarity can release attention to subtler details. Yet extended repetition may also reduce engagement. These possibilities coexist.

Duration is therefore a variable to document, not a credential that makes an experience profound. Longer is not automatically deeper.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 14 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 15

No score divides the performance into chapters. Nevertheless, the listener remembers a before and an after, and begins drawing borders in time.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 15 OF 28

The Chapter No One Composed

An improvised performance may contain no planned movements, yet the listener hears an opening, a turning point, and a return.

Memory and contrast give sound a shape. This does not mean the performer secretly composed that shape. Sometimes the audience discovers a chapter no one wrote.

A listener may retrospectively divide a performance into movements. The performer may remember no such sections. Neither perspective needs to be corrected by the other.

Compare their accounts without showing either person a proposed map. Where the maps overlap, we have a question about shared cues. Where they diverge, we have evidence of distinct experiences.

TERRITORY VI / 09

THE PLAYER INSIDE THE SOUND

INTERVAL 06 / NINE

THE ONE WHO MAKES THE SOUND

From the audience, the drone can seem to have no beginning and no end. From inside the instrument, it is made of muscles, small corrections, and breath exchanged under pressure.

The player knows a version of the work that no listener can directly occupy. This is not a hierarchy of truth. It is a distinction the inquiry must keep.

FIELD NOTE / 16

The apparent impossibility of endless breath is a practiced exchange of tasks. Technique is a chain of physical decisions made fluent enough to vanish from the listener's attention.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 16 OF 28

The Breath That Returns

Circular breathing is a coordination skill, not a metaphysical claim. The player manages air stored in the cheeks while inhaling through the nose, then returns to ordinary breath-driven airflow.

Practice can make this sequence more reliable and less demanding of attention. Freedom arrives not by escaping the body but by learning what it can do without constant supervision.

Circular breathing involves maintaining outward airflow while briefly inhaling. It is a technique acquired through repetition, coordination, and error correction.

Its significance for KLAAT is practical and aesthetic: it allows the sustained field to remain available while the player chooses how to change it. It does not imply altered consciousness by itself.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 16 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 17

Inside the performance, effort and hearing are joined. Outside it, the audience receives the consequence without sharing the labor.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 17 OF 28

Two Different Interiors

The performer feels the resistance of the instrument and the drum answering the hand. The listener hears the result without carrying those same motor demands.

Flow in the player does not establish trance in the audience. Both experiences matter; neither should be used as evidence for the other.

The performer receives tactile and motor information unavailable to the listener. Effort, timing decisions, and anticipations belong to the player's interior experience.

A performer feeling absorbed cannot be used as a proxy for an audience feeling absorbed. Two reports must remain two reports, even when they describe the same performance.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 17 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 18

Amastacia and Ankora are not two arguments competing for the same conclusion. Each changes what the other can mean.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 18 OF 28

One Living System

A drumbeat changes how a continuing drone is heard. The drone changes the weight of the drumbeat by providing an unbroken background.

Perhaps the most revealing unit of KLAAT is not a single instrument or sound but the evolving relationship between them. Two objects produce sound. Their meeting produces the field.

The drum changes the temporal frame around the drone; the drone changes how the drum's decay is heard. Their combination is not adequately described by adding two isolated acoustic profiles.

A useful experiment may preserve one instrument while varying the other, then ask what the listener believes changed. Relationships, not just ingredients, become the object of study.

TERRITORY VII / 09

THE THIRD PARTICIPANT

INTERVAL 07 / NINE

AFTER THE ROOM

At some point the instruments stop and a recording begins. What leaves the venue is not the venue itself. It is a constructed path back toward an event.

The portable work must decide what it cannot afford to lose. That decision is artistic before it becomes technical.

FIELD NOTE / 19

The venue enters the work without announcing itself. Every reflection is a delayed participant; every absorption is a decision the walls make without intention.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 19 OF 28

The Venue Is Not One Room

The performance begins in the shape of the space that will receive it. A narrow room, hard floor, soft crowd, and distant wall change what becomes audible.

There is no ideal venue independent of the desired experience. Choosing a room is part of choosing the instrument.

Every venue distributes sound unevenly. A reflective surface can extend a strike; a crowded room can absorb portions of the field. Placement matters.

Rather than searching for the single best room, document the characteristics that support the particular kind of attention and bodily experience the performance seeks to invite.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 19 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 20

The recording has no duty to pretend it is the room. It has a different obligation: to reveal the work without inventing a false equivalence.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 20 OF 28

The Small Speaker Problem

A phone cannot reproduce every part of a large acoustic event. The lowest energy may vanish while upper harmonics and the drum's timing remain.

A recording creates another encounter, not a perfect duplicate. The question is which relationships must survive the journey.

A microphone chooses a position; a recording chain chooses what to preserve; playback equipment chooses what can be reproduced. The recorded work is a translation with physical constraints.

The aim is not to counterfeit the room. It is to make the translation legible and to acknowledge what it cannot carry.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 20 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 21

A small speaker removes weight from the sound. What remains may still contain its skeleton, its breath, and its peculiar way of counting.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 21 OF 28

The Portable Field

A person walking with headphones may hear KLAAT without feeling the floor move. That is not necessarily a failed version of a live performance.

Continuity, timbral identity, and rhythmic structure may survive better than bodily pressure. This is a hypothesis to test, not a result already proven.

If the low fundamental weakens on a small device, upper harmonics may still allow recognition. The drum's pattern may remain even as its physical weight disappears.

This suggests two separate questions: does the listener recognize KLAAT, and does the listener report a comparable experience? The answers may differ.

TERRITORY VIII / 09

THE MEASURE AND THE WITNESS

INTERVAL 08 / NINE

THE QUESTION AFTER THE STRIKE

A person describes a passage as opening. A waveform shows a changing overtone. The descriptions may refer to the same interval, but neither is a translation of the other.

The work of research is to build a bridge between such accounts without erasing the distance that makes them useful.

FIELD NOTE / 22

Some changes can be counted. Others become important only because a listener remembers what preceded them.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 22 OF 28

What Counts as an Event?

A recording contains measurable onsets, silences, levels, and spectral changes. A listener may identify a turning point where no graph shows a dramatic peak.

Acoustic description tells us what the signal did. Listener description tells us what someone experienced. An event is not always a spike on a graph.

An acoustic onset is measurable; a perceived turning point is reportable. Their relation may be close, loose, or absent.

The researcher must resist selecting only the moments that fit a favored story. Unexpected descriptions and disagreements are part of the territory.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 22 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 23

To measure an improvisation is to approach a living thing after it has passed. The record can clarify what happened; it must not become the command for what happens next.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 23 OF 28

A Measurement That Leaves the Music Alive

Study need not dictate performance. Record the improvisation, then examine its structure. Ask listeners what they heard before telling them what they were supposed to notice.

A null result belongs in the archive as surely as a discovery. The laboratory is not the author of the music; it is one way of asking a careful question.

Recordings can be annotated after the fact for onset timing, spectral motion, and structural change. These measures can support comparisons without prescribing the next performance.

A useful protocol should preserve spontaneous creation while making later questions answerable. Science follows the music rather than writing its instructions.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 23 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 24

The listener speaks in images before speaking in units. A careful inquiry must make room for those images without confusing them with measurements.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 24 OF 28

The Listener Is Not a Sensor

A participant might report pressure, warmth, waiting, an opening passage, or nothing remarkable. These statements cannot all be reduced to one number.

Open description should precede imposed categories where possible. Individual differences are evidence to understand, not mistakes to erase.

A listener may report a 'wave' or a 'threshold.' Those terms may be metaphors, sensory descriptions, or personally meaningful images. Their ambiguity is not a defect to erase immediately.

Ask what the listener meant before converting the phrase into a physiological claim. Description should precede interpretation.

TERRITORY IX / 09

THE UNFINISHED RETURN

INTERVAL 09 / NINE

WHAT REMAINS UNPROVEN

The Atlas has reached the point where its language must become more exact, not more grandiose. A compelling idea is not a demonstrated result.

KLAAT can stand on the strength of its music while its scientific questions remain open. The open questions are not an embarrassment. They are the next territory.

FIELD NOTE / 25

Evidence is a narrow bridge over a wide experience. It supports only what has actually crossed it.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 25 OF 28

The Narrow Ground

Rooms shape sound. Harmonics complicate a note. Repeated rhythms invite prediction. Hearing and touch can interact. These foundations are real; none proves a unique KLAAT effect.

The ground is narrower than a manifesto might wish. It is also firmer.

Established acoustic and perceptual findings provide a strong foundation, but KLAAT-specific outcomes remain to be tested. The distinction should be visible on every page.

A confident statement about a room mode and a tentative statement about absorption must not be printed in the same voice of certainty.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 25 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 26

A possibility becomes useful when it can fail. A hypothesis that cannot be contradicted is not yet a research question.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 26 OF 28

The Provisional Bridge

Perhaps Amastacia creates continuity without stasis. Perhaps Ankora anchors time inside evolving density. Perhaps expectation creates the strongest moments without any increase in force.

Each perhaps is a bridge toward a test, not a result disguised as poetry. Some bridges will hold. Others must be dismantled.

A hypothesis is useful because it proposes a relationship that can be challenged. For example, does rhythmic density alter perceived urgency when the underlying beat stays constant?

The Atlas should keep its propositions close to the experiments that might disconfirm them. A possibility is not diminished by the risk of failure.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 26 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 27

The easiest myth is the one that promises to explain everything at once. The harder discipline is to leave some experiences unnamed until the evidence catches up.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 27 OF 28

The Beautiful Wrong Answer

It would be easy to claim that C-sharp heals, a tempo induces trance, or a vibrating chest proves a vibrating organ. Such answers end the inquiry quickly.

The Atlas refuses them as established conclusions. The experience deserves explanations capable of surviving scrutiny.

Some appealing claims are not supported: that one pitch heals, that one tempo guarantees trance, or that a felt bodily vibration proves a particular organ is resonating.

The refusal to repeat these claims is not a refusal of wonder. It is a refusal to confuse wonder with a conclusion.

THE UNDERLYING RESEARCH / CHAPTER 27 ↗
FIELD NOTE / 28

At the end of the interval, the sound withdraws. The questions do not. A field remains open precisely because the last word belongs to the next encounter.

FIELD OBSERVATION / 28 OF 28

The Work Remains Open

Record the performance. Let listeners describe it without prompting. Compare their perceived transitions with the signal. Change the playback and ask what remains. Compare the player's experience with the audience's.

The final strike ends before the room becomes silent. A discipline does not endure by declaring itself complete. The sound came first. It should always be permitted the last word.

The next encounter may contradict this edition. A listener may hear a passage differently, a recording may reveal an overlooked change, or an experiment may find no effect where one was expected.

The Atlas should be built to accept such revisions. Its authority comes not from finality, but from the discipline of remaining answerable to what happens.
