SESSION 137 — THE DRUM SOUND IS NOT A FINGERPRINT
Why Ankora's recorded spectrum cannot uniquely reveal its hide, tension, or the movement that produced it
Published: Saturday, 2026-10-31, 08:15 CDT (America/Chicago)
Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


CENTRAL QUESTION


If one Ankora strike has a deep, resonant body and another sounds sharper, what, precisely, changed? Was it the elk hide, a local tension difference, where Paul's hand landed, how long the hand contacted the skin, the way he supported the frame, the microphone's angle, or the presence of Amastacia's drone? A recording can preserve the audible difference while leaving the physical cause unresolved. The crucial scientific distinction is between recognizing a sound and identifying the mechanism that made it.


This is an INVERSE PROBLEM. The forward problem asks: given a membrane, tension field, geometry, striking force, support, surrounding air, and microphone, what sound should be produced? The inverse problem asks: given the sound, which of those conditions was present? Several different causes can produce similar observations. The existence of a measurable spectral peak does not mean there is one uniquely recoverable material property behind it.


KLAAT's artistry is not the problem to solve. Paul's spontaneous Ankora gestures already generate a meaningful vocabulary of weight, attack, roughness, decay, and pressure beneath Amastacia's continuous C# drone. The research problem is how to describe that vocabulary without mistaking the recording for an anatomical scan of the instrument, or mistaking a listener's response for proof of one physical cause.


1. THE EVIDENCE CHAIN HAS MORE LINKS THAN THE WAVEFORM


A useful causal sketch is:


Mounted drum state (hide, hydration, tension distribution, frame and support)
  + actual excitation (strike position, contact area, force-time shape, damping by hand)
  -> membrane and frame motion
  -> radiated sound field, mixed with Amastacia and room sound
  -> microphone and recording system
  -> analyzed waveform and spectrum
  -> listener's perceived timbre, energy, rhythm and bodily sensation.


Each arrow introduces information loss or confounding. A microphone senses pressure at its location, not the entire vibrating surface. A Fourier transform describes frequency components of that captured pressure, not the exact spatial displacement pattern of the hide. An ear judges a complex, contextual event, not the values of an isolated material parameter.


This distinction is especially important for a frame drum. Ankora is not merely an ideal circular sheet. The hide is a natural, heterogeneous membrane under mounting stress; its edge is attached to a frame; Paul's supporting hand and striking hand can affect boundary conditions; the air around it loads the moving surface. None of those interactions is reliably recoverable from one phone recording of a full two-instrument improvisation.


ESTABLISHED PHYSICS: an ideal, uniform circular membrane with radius a, uniform tension per unit length T, and mass per unit area mu has modal frequencies of the form f_mn = (alpha_mn / 2*pi*a) * sqrt(T/mu), where alpha_mn is an appropriate Bessel-function zero. For the lowest mode alpha_01 is approximately 2.4048. This is a model, not an equation for all of Ankora's audible peaks. It already shows a fundamental ambiguity: a higher T and a higher mu can offset one another. The same modeled fundamental frequency can occur with different tension and surface density. Changes in radius also change the frequency. Knowing only one peak does not uniquely determine T, mu, or hide identity.


For illustration, holding a and mu fixed while multiplying T by four doubles the ideal membrane's modal frequencies. Holding a and T fixed while multiplying mu by four halves them. These are exact consequences of the simple model, not measured Ankora adjustments. In a real drum, tension, moisture, stiffness, damping, frame behavior and the player's hand are not necessarily independent.


2. WHAT A STRIKE EXCITES IS NOT WHAT THE DRUM COULD EXCITE


An ideal membrane possesses many possible vibration modes. A particular strike excites them unequally. If the force is applied near a mode's nodal region, that mode may contribute little to the resulting motion. If it is applied near an antinode, that mode may be prominent. A different contact duration changes the force spectrum; a broad, soft contact and a brief, hard contact are not equivalent inputs. A supporting hand may absorb some motion. The same mounted drum can therefore generate quite different recorded spectra without its hide or overall tuning changing.


The distinction is not merely theoretical. Skrodzka, Hojan and Proksza (2006) measured vibration modes and radiated spectra of a snare drum. Their measured spectral maxima did not simply coincide with all the membrane's modal frequencies. The lowest mode was the strongest radiating mode only for central excitation, and off-center excitation produced substantially different relationships between radiated spectral peaks and measured modal frequencies. The shell, snare system and other components also mattered. The experiment was on a snare drum, not Ankora, but it directly falsifies the general shortcut 'one recorded peak = one uniquely identified membrane mode.'


A second experimental example comes from Bader, Fischer, Münster and Kontopidis (2019). They built a modified 40-cm frame drum with added small masses arranged in rings and examined its vibration using microphone arrays and high-speed optical methods. The strike's location relative to the ring affected where vibration was concentrated. Their modified instrument developed a frequency band with substantially suppressed response, with the location of that band depending on ring configuration. The experiment is a deliberately modified synthetic-head drum, NOT an account of how elk hide behaves. Its transferable lesson is narrower and stronger: excitation location and spatial distribution of mass can change which parts of a membrane participate, and the microphone's spectral picture alone is not a complete map of those motions.


This suggests a more precise way to hear Ankora: a brighter strike may indicate a different EXCITATION of the same instrument rather than a newly altered instrument. A duller strike may reflect damping, not necessarily a lower-tension patch. The sound is real; the material diagnosis is uncertain.


3. THE ELK-HIDE QUESTION: A CONTRADICTION WORTH KEEPING


Natural animal hide contains oriented collagen and can have direction-dependent mechanical properties. It is tempting to hear two nearby frequencies or uneven decay and conclude that the hide's grain direction caused them. That is one possible hypothesis, but not a demonstrated Ankora mechanism.


Li, Gower, Destrade and Yun (2022) measured waves in a mounted goatskin bodhrán. Although the material was intrinsically anisotropic, the observed local wavefronts were nearly circular under the tested conditions. The researchers interpreted this in relation to the drum's approximately equibiaxial mounting stress. In the same experiment, hydration was associated with substantial changes: the reported dry fundamental was about 84 Hz and the hydrated fundamental about 36 Hz, with estimated radial stress changing from 3.79 to 1.31 MPa. Their head was goatskin, approximately 360 +/- 30 micrometers thick, and their measurements were made in a particular region and frequency regime. None of those numbers is a measurement of Ankora.


The lesson is not 'animal skin is isotropic.' It is that intrinsic material anisotropy, wave propagation in the MOUNTED skin, global drum modes and peaks in airborne sound are four different observables. One cannot substitute for another. Circular local wavefronts do not guarantee perfectly degenerate global modes, and anisotropic collagen does not guarantee a particular audible spectral splitting.


Worland (2010) studied another competing cause: nonuniform tension around the rim can split modes that would be degenerate in an ideal symmetric membrane. This offers a mechanism for paired resonances without requiring the hide's collagen direction to be the explanation. Equally, observing a close pair of spectral peaks does not prove that they are split membrane modes at all: they might involve the frame, room, Amastacia harmonics, a changing strike, or analysis artifacts. A physical explanation must survive comparisons designed to rule out alternatives.


This is the new synthesis beyond the recent hourly hide-anisotropy note. The narrow contradiction about nearly circular waves is one part of a broader inverse-identification problem: even when one proposed material mechanism is weakened, a recorded timbral difference still does not uniquely select the replacement explanation.


4. THE NUMERICAL MODEL THAT SEEMED TO IDENTIFY A DRUM


An especially useful counterpoint comes from Alexandraki, Starakis, Zervas and Bader (2023). They generated 11,114 synthesized drum sounds using a finite-difference membrane model with several simulated distributions of added mass. Their machine-learning approach identified the modeled damping-pattern class with a reported 97.4% accuracy and estimated added mass with a reported error of approximately +/-11% of the original membrane mass. This is impressive within the constructed dataset. But the authors emphasized the central limitation: the training and evaluation sounds were generated by the same computational modeling framework, and they had not validated those results as an inference system for arbitrary recordings of real drums.


This is a textbook example of why prediction accuracy and causal identification are not identical. If a system is trained on a restricted universe of six modeled patterns, it can become good at identifying which modeled pattern produced a modeled waveform. That does not establish that every real elk-hide sound was produced by one of those patterns, that the model includes all relevant sources of variability, or that a new microphone recording would preserve the same discriminating cues. The algorithm's high score is conditional on its model world.


A KLAAT-specific classifier might someday recognize natural differences among Paul's own Ankora strikes. But 'recognizes strike A versus strike B' would not yet mean 'measures elk-hide stiffness' or 'knows where the hand contacted the skin.' Those are different scientific claims requiring different validation. A model that can predict the performer’s own labels might still rely on room noise, recording position, or take identity unless testing explicitly blocks those shortcuts.


The productive discovery is not that computational inference is worthless. It is that an honest model must specify its inference target, its training conditions, its independent test set, and the alternatives it cannot distinguish. Good science here will likely begin with modest descriptive claims and advance only when new kinds of measurement are available.


5. HOW THIS INTERACTS WITH AMASTACIA


The two-instrument KLAAT performance makes the inverse problem harder, not because it is flawed, but because it is genuinely interactive. Ankora's strike spectrum may overlap with Amastacia's drone harmonics and breath noise. If a particular peak appears during a drum attack, a mixed microphone channel cannot by itself tell whether that energy came from Ankora, Amastacia, room resonance, or a nonlinear processing artifact. The ongoing drone can mask part of the drum's decay; a hand movement can alter the drum while a breath articulation alters the drone; microphone automatic gain control may change the apparent envelope of both.


Suppose an ordinary recording contains an Ankora strike whose high-frequency energy appears weaker than the previous strike. Four very different descriptions could all be consistent with that observation: the hand made a broader contact; the strike landed in a different region; Amastacia became louder and masked some of the strike; or the recording system adjusted gain or filtering. The measured statement 'this window contains less captured energy above a specified frequency' may be defensible. The causal statement 'the elk hide became more damped' is not defensible without more information.


This matters for KLAAT's artistic identity. Its distinctive pressure, grain, pulse and organic imperfection can be discussed as heard properties of the PERFORMANCE without assigning every audible quality to one piece of material. The performance remains the primary artifact. Scientific refinement should make the descriptions more exact, not make the improvisation answer to an acoustic model.


6. FOUR LEVELS OF CLAIM, KEPT SEPARATE


LEVEL A — ACOUSTICALLY MEASURED. With an original file and a defined method, one could measure attack timing, local RMS, spectral peaks, relative band energy, decay constants, or changes in spectral shape at the recording microphone. These describe the recording under stated conditions. Without the file, even these are not KLAAT measurements.


LEVEL B — PERCEPTUALLY REPORTED. A listener might describe a strike as heavy, dry, booming, sharp, vibrating, urgent, or grounding. Those reports are legitimate subjective data if collected faithfully. They do not by themselves establish where the hand struck, which membrane mode dominated, or whether an autonomic response occurred. The performer may experience physical effort, tactile contact and flow that a passive listener does not.


LEVEL C — PHYSICALLY PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE. Different strike positions, contact shapes, support pressure, moisture states, boundary tension and microphone geometries can change the recorded sound. The cited research supports those mechanisms in other systems or ideal models. Which ones dominate Ankora in a particular performance remains unknown. An actual test would need enough independent observations to distinguish competing causes.


LEVEL D — SPECULATIVE OR UNSUPPORTED. Claims that elk hide necessarily causes a particular trance state, that its collagen orientation produces a specific neural rhythm, that one close spectral pair proves directional skin stiffness, that a model trained on simulated membranes can diagnose Ankora from a phone video, or that C# makes Ankora's membrane physiologically special are unsupported. KLAAT has not yet demonstrated trance, neural entrainment, autonomic regulation, therapeutic outcomes, or listener activation in a KLAAT-specific controlled study.


The distinction is protective. It allows a strong, authentic artistic vocabulary while preventing the research program from claiming an unmeasured mechanism as fact.


7. WHAT A REAL ANKORA BASELINE WOULD REQUIRE


A useful future baseline need not resemble a composition. It can be a brief, optional instrument check outside the improvisation, preserving all existing performances untouched. The minimal scientific gain comes from holding some variables approximately stable while observing others. If a few naturally comfortable taps are recorded with a fixed microphone and support arrangement, one can compare within-position repeatability with between-position differences. That helps answer whether the instrument can generate different spectra merely because the hand lands elsewhere. It does not identify hide stiffness, collagen direction, or tension distribution.


For stronger inference, one would need information beyond a single audio channel: close and room microphones; documented strike location or video; consistent geometry; room and moisture conditions; possibly noncontact surface-motion measurements; and a way to distinguish hand force from instrument response. The optical-sensor study by Sokolovskis and McPherson (2014) estimated strike locations within about 2 cm in its instrument and experimental arrangement, illustrating that strike position can be independently measured. This is not a guarantee that a camera or a generic audio algorithm can locate Paul's hand to 2 cm. It is evidence that an independent measurement channel can make an otherwise ambiguous inference more answerable.


A cautious analysis would separate at least three questions: (i) Can we repeatedly recognize a recorded timbral difference? (ii) Does the difference covary with an independently observed gesture or instrument condition? (iii) Does changing that condition, while reasonably controlling alternatives, cause the difference? The first is description, the second association, the third causal inference. No one recording can leap across all three.


An especially important null result would be that several strike positions sound nearly indistinguishable in a given room and microphone position. That would not imply the instrument lacks expressive nuance. It would identify a limitation of that capture setup or of the chosen feature, and it might help preserve the original sonic identity more faithfully when recording later.

Session 136 separated Ankora's produced actions from delayed room and electronic returns. The 2026-10-31 02:19 hourly note introduced the mounted-hide anisotropy counterexample. This session asks a different, more general question: when multiple drum causes can produce similar recorded effects, what level of evidence is required to identify a cause? Its specific additions are the mode-versus-radiation distinction; strike-dependent excitation; forward versus inverse identification; and the warning that even 97.4% classification accuracy on synthetic sounds does not validate a real-instrument diagnosis.


That distinction also protects future listener work. If someone says Ankora feels 'more physical' at one moment, the next task is not to infer hide tension from that sentence. The next task is to preserve the timestamp, exact report, playback context and unedited sound; then, if useful, compare independently measured acoustic properties. Felt bodily energy, liking, absorption and acoustic modal structure remain separate outcomes until evidence connects them.


The deeper point is compatible with KLAAT's archaeosonic aesthetic: an instrument can have a distinctive material history and an unmistakable artistic identity without its material origin providing a complete explanation of every sound. The hide matters. The hand matters. The frame, air, room, microphone and listener matter. Science gains precision by identifying which link it has actually measured.


OPTIONAL KRX-137 — ONE DRUM, MORE THAN ONE VOICE


At any convenient moment, outside a normal KLAAT improvisation, record a handful of relaxed Ankora taps that naturally fall at slightly different comfortable locations while leaving the microphone, room and support position unchanged. Do not force identical strikes, set a tempo, or alter the drum. Simply listen back and note whether the differences are obvious, subtle or absent; if you wish, mark the rough locations. Keep the raw file. This is a demonstration of within-instrument variability, not a test of elk-hide chemistry or a mandate to change how you play. If the exercise feels intrusive, skip it and preserve the existing recordings instead.
