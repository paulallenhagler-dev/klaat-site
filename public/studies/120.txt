KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 120
THE SKILL THAT CHANGED WHILE THE INSTRUMENTS WERE QUIET
Sleep, brief rest, motor-memory consolidation, and the difference between feeling fluent and becoming fluent


Published: October 25, 2026 — 15:17 CDT (America/Chicago)
Governing principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH STATUS
This is a critical synthesis of controlled human motor-learning experiments, including musician-specific studies and recent contradictory results. No Amastacia or Ankora skill acquisition, sleep-dependent change, neural replay, listener absorption, or listener physiology has been measured in KLAAT. The purpose is to better interpret Paul's naturally evolving technique, not to schedule his art, prescribe a score, or convert improvisation into a training protocol.


CENTRAL QUESTION


Paul sometimes discovers a coordination while playing: an Ankora accent happens as Amastacia's airflow shifts; a circular-breathing changeover becomes less effortful; a transition between pulse textures emerges without conscious calculation. The next time he plays, a movement that had been awkward may seem available almost immediately. Did he improve while practicing, during a pause, during sleep, or simply because fatigue and self-conscious monitoring receded?


The question sounds simple but reaches an active scientific dispute. A musician's performance is not identical to the motor skill that supports it. An excellent moment can reflect a genuinely strengthened skill, but also temporary alertness, attention, warming up, favorable instrument conditions, or chance. Conversely, a rough moment can conceal learning that will become visible later. KLAAT's improvisational freedom makes this distinction especially valuable: we want to understand how fluency grows without deciding in advance what the music must become.

2. THREE CLOCKS THAT CAN MAKE ONE GESTURE LOOK BETTER


CLOCK A: ONLINE ACQUISITION. During actual playing, the nervous system receives sensory feedback and updates motor control. This can happen while the performer remains deeply engaged and is not verbally analyzing what is learned. Observable change may include more reliable lip/cheek/soft-palate coordination, a smoother return to the drone after nasal inspiration, or fewer unintentional Ankora interruptions. These are possible indicators, not required artistic goals.


CLOCK B: BETWEEN-BOUT PERFORMANCE. A brief pause may allow motor slowing, local fatigue, or reactive inhibition to dissipate. It may also provide an opportunity for planning or memory stabilization. If the first gesture after a pause is better, the difference alone does not tell us which process occurred. This is a measurement problem: 'after the pause' does not automatically mean 'learned during the pause.'


CLOCK C: DELAYED RETENTION. After several hours or a night, a motor pattern may be more stable, equally available, or less available. Sleep may contribute to some tasks, but circadian timing, fatigue, interference from other learning, motivation, and how the test is constructed can also affect performance. Retention means a capacity survives a delay; improvement means its measured expression exceeds a relevant earlier baseline. They are not synonyms.


There is a fourth layer, specific to KLAAT: ARTISTIC USE. A newly fluent breath transition might never appear in a particular improvisation because the music goes elsewhere. The absence of that gesture from a recording does not mean the ability was lost. A spontaneous decision not to use a technique is not a motor-learning failure.


3. THE CLASSIC SLEEP RESULT—REAL, BUT NOT UNIVERSAL


Walker and colleagues (2002) tested a trained finger-sequence task and reported approximately 20% improvement in motor speed after a night of sleep without loss of accuracy, whereas a comparable waking interval produced no significant benefit under their conditions [1]. In a follow-up sequence-learning investigation, Walker and colleagues (2003) found that most of the reported delayed gains appeared after the first sleep period, with some further improvements across subsequent nights; doubling initial training did not simply double the later gain [2]. These experiments are important evidence that offline change is possible. They do not show that sleep grants a fixed 20% gain to musicians or to complex improvisation.


The closest early music-specific evidence is more interesting than a finger-keyboard slogan. Simmons and Duke (2006) taught experienced learners a 12-note piano melody, then compared recall after 12- or 24-hour intervals that included or excluded sleep [3]. They reported significant sleep-associated improvement in accuracy but not consistent sleep-related improvement in speed; temporal evenness improved at a 24-hour retest without further practice. This matters because musical skill has multiple endpoints. A technique can become more reliable without becoming faster. And a smoother sound can reflect a change in timing precision without a change in nominal tempo.


Duke and Davis (2006) also studied brief digital-piano keyboard sequences across days, finding improvements after nights of sleep, but showing that the pattern could be affected by intervening learning of another sequence [4]. These studies used explicitly learned and repeated material. KLAAT's spontaneous hand–breath coordination may involve overlapping but nonidentical forms of motor memory. We cannot assume that an unscored improvised gesture follows the same retention curve as a memorized piano sequence.


4. A CONTRADICTION: SOME CAREFUL EXPERIMENTS DID NOT FIND SLEEP-ENHANCED SKILL


Rickard, Cai, Rieth, Jones, and Ard (2008) argued that apparent overnight improvement in motor-sequence experiments can be inflated by analytical choices and performance-state confounds [5]. They identified four hazards: learning hidden by averaging, reactive inhibition hidden by averaging, time-of-day and time-since-sleep differences, and fatigue accumulating during concentrated practice. In two experiments designed to reduce those problems, the expected special enhancement from sleep was eliminated. Their conclusion allowed that sleep might protect a learned skill from forgetting without necessarily adding a new performance gain.


This is not proof that the 2002 and 2006 findings were fabricated or that sleep is irrelevant. Different protocols, outcome measures, training amounts, test timing, and skill demands can yield different results. It is, however, decisive against the blanket statement 'every motor skill improves overnight because sleep consolidates it.' A listener cannot infer Paul's neural consolidation from hearing a more fluent Ankora passage the next morning. Nor can Paul infer it from how easy the gesture feels.


In a 2023 controlled study, Truong and colleagues randomized 36 right-handed adults to learn a finger-tapping task at 10 a.m., 3 p.m., or 8 p.m. and retested them 24 hours later [6]. Immediate acquisition was similar across groups. The evening-trained group improved at the delayed test; the morning group deteriorated; the afternoon group was approximately stable. Two additional control experiments supported a contribution of intervening sleep in the evening condition. This is a compelling demonstration that acquisition and retention can separate. But it does not establish that 8 p.m. is the ideal time for KLAAT practice. The study used a specific non-dominant-hand sequence, a defined test, and controlled schedules; it was not a test of Paul's natural circadian preference or free musical improvisation.


5. THE TEN-SECOND REST FINDING—AND ITS STRONGEST CHALLENGE


The most relevant controversy for casual, instinctive technique development concerns very short breaks. Bönstrup and colleagues (2019) studied a five-element finger sequence practiced in alternating 10-second practice and 10-second rest periods [7]. They observed that much of the early measured improvement appeared across the rests rather than within the brief performance bouts. Magnetoencephalography recorded beta-band changes associated with those between-bout gains. A 2020 follow-up used crowdsourced experiments with a cumulative sample of 951 people across conditions; its authors argued that seconds-scale gains survived controls for fatigue and predictive slowing and that interfering immediately after practice reduced subsequent improvement [8].


If those results generalize, the nervous system may do meaningful work when the fingers are not moving. That possibility is attractive for a musician who wants technique to become more available without endless forced repetition. But the evidence cannot be treated as settled.


Gupta and Rickard (2024) constructed quantitative models that explicitly included reactive inhibition—the temporary slowing that accumulates during repeated execution and dissipates during breaks [9]. Across four groups with different practice/break structures, their best overall fits came from a HYBRID model that included both online and offline learning; a pure offline model that ignored reactive inhibition fit worst. Importantly, model superiority depends on the candidate model family and assumptions. It does not measure neural replay directly.


A still sharper counterexample arrived in a five-experiment 2025 PNAS paper by Das and colleagues [10]. In their in-lab experiment (N=85) and online experiment (N=358), 10-second practice bouts with 10-second breaks produced familiar 'micro-offline gains' during training, but did not produce a durable skill advantage over continuous practice when both groups were tested under comparable conditions. In the break group, the authors reported significant within-training micro-offline gains (in-lab t(43)=3.92, p<.001, d=0.59; online t(178)=3.53, p<.001, d=0.26). Yet the advantage disappeared at immediate standardized tests and after five-minute rests. They also observed micro-offline gains with NONREPEATING movement sequences and showed that limiting advance planning reduced the gains. This challenges the claim that every improvement across a pause represents newly consolidated sequence-specific learning.


The key disagreement is not whether a break can make performance feel or look better; both sides observe that it can. The disagreement is what that improvement MEANS. One interpretation emphasizes rapid memory stabilization and replay; another emphasizes relief from transient slowing and the opportunity to preplan. The 2024 hybrid model suggests both online learning and offline processes may contribute. It would be premature to announce a single universal mechanism.


6. WHY THIS DISPUTE IS USEFUL FOR AMASTACIA AND ANKORA


KLAAT makes the problem unusually visible. Consider a difficult circular-breathing handoff while the supporting hand continues to position Ankora. During several minutes of playing, the lips, cheeks, jaw, tongue, soft palate, respiratory system, arm, and auditory attention all work together. A later smoother handoff could reflect better coordination learned during the earlier attempt. It could also reflect relief from local muscular strain, a different breath volume, a change in Amastacia's bore temperature, or the fact that Paul begins the next phrase with a clearer motor plan. Those explanations can coexist.


Likewise, a rhythmic transition that suddenly 'clicks' after setting the drum down may not be evidence that a whole musical phrase was consolidated in silence. The next attempt may simply occur when Paul is less tired or less busy correcting the previous one. Conversely, a real improvement in coordination could be hidden by a drier Ankora membrane, a changed microphone angle, or a more exploratory musical choice. Session 117 already established that animal-skin membrane state can vary; Session 118 established that files from one take are not independent evidence of a general trend.


The correct artistic response is not to impose a drill, score, or required break. It is to stop using the immediate smoothness of one attempt as a complete account of technical development. KLAAT can preserve spontaneous emergence and still ask careful questions about what becomes easier across days.


7. PERFORMANCE, CAPACITY, AND EXPRESSION MUST BE MEASURED SEPARATELY


A practical research vocabulary has three levels:


OBSERVED PERFORMANCE: what the recording actually contains—an audible drone interruption, timing relationship, breath noise, harmonic change, unintended strike, or uninterrupted transition. Even these require caution about microphone processing, time alignment, and instrument state.


TECHNICAL CAPACITY: what Paul can reliably do under a range of ordinary circumstances. Capacity cannot be inferred from one favored moment or one rough moment. Repeated observations across independent occasions are more informative, but they remain descriptive without controlled comparison.


SUBJECTIVE EFFORT/FLOW: whether the gesture felt automatic, effortful, awkward, expressive, or absorbed. Paul's own report is authoritative about his experience but is not a direct measurement of a neural learning mechanism. A passage can feel easy because it is well learned, because the musical demand was lower, or because attention was engaged elsewhere.


Listener response is a FOURTH and separate level. A listener might find a slightly unstable, breath-textured transition more compelling than a technically seamless one. We have no evidence that better motor retention automatically increases absorption, groove, bodily energy, or trance. A smoother technique is useful insofar as it expands Paul's freedom; it is not a guarantee of a stronger audience effect.


8. WHY SPEED IS A POOR SOLE OUTCOME FOR KLAAT


Much of the motor-consolidation literature scores the number of correct sequences per unit time, reaction time, or tapping speed. Those measures are well suited to a defined laboratory task. They are not universal measures of artistry.


A faster Ankora transition may be neither more expressive nor more controlled. A longer sustained Amastacia passage may be neither more musical nor more absorbing. Circular breathing that leaves no audible seam may be technically desirable in some moments, while an exposed, breath-shaped seam may be integral to KLAAT's organic aesthetic in others. A more relevant technical question is whether Paul can CHOOSE, spontaneously, among possibilities without being trapped by an unwanted mechanical limitation.


The strongest future indicators would therefore be Paul-identified, rather than externally imposed: 'I wanted to continue the drone but lost it,' 'I intended the drum to remain independent but the hand froze,' or 'this transition felt available without conscious rescue.' Even those labels should be documented after playing rather than turning every moment into self-surveillance.


9. A FAIR TEST OF DELAYED SKILL CHANGE IS HARDER THAN IT LOOKS


If KLAAT ever moves from casual observation to a formal technique study, the comparison must define a gesture and a criterion that Paul actually values, then separate measurement from the music itself. For example, an optional technique-only calibration could measure the frequency of unintentional drone interruptions during comfortable circular-breathing attempts, without requiring those attempts to appear in the finished art. Such a calibration is not a prescribed performance architecture.


The study would need to distinguish end-of-practice performance from start-of-next-session performance, account for warm-up and fatigue, document time since sleep, and avoid treating multiple adjacent attempts as independent days. It would also need to identify whether the skill tested is genuinely the same on both occasions. If Paul freely changes the coordination, then a different sound is not necessarily an error or an improvement. It may be a different artistic choice.


For the rest-versus-learning dispute, the crucial outcome is PERSISTENCE under comparable conditions, not merely a first post-break improvement. A brief recovery followed by an immediate return to the old difficulty supports a transient performance-state explanation more than durable skill acquisition. Stable availability on subsequent occasions is more consistent with learning, though still not proof that the learning occurred during sleep rather than during prior playing. No phone waveform can reveal replay, synaptic consolidation, or a particular sleep stage.


10. THE NEURAL CLAIM MUST STAY IN ITS LANE


Some studies record EEG or MEG activity during rest and sleep; others report behavior alone. A correlation between beta-band activity and a later keypress improvement does not establish that a specific brain rhythm caused the gain, nor that a particular acoustic drone frequency 'tuned' the brain. The neural frequencies in motor-learning studies are not Amastacia's nominal C# fundamental, Ankora's strike rate, or a therapeutic resonance target.


Similarly, sleep-dependent motor memory is a hypothesis about the PERFORMER'S skill, not about what happens in a passive KLAAT listener. It provides no evidence that KLAAT induces sleep, theta activity, trance, or motor rehabilitation. The science may help us understand Paul's developing fluency; it does not convert the music into a neurological intervention.


11. EVIDENCE LADDER


ESTABLISHED IN TESTED NON-KLAAT TASKS: Some finger-sequence and keyboard-melody experiments report improved delayed performance after sleep; the magnitude and affected dimensions differ by task. Short rest periods can improve immediate performance. Multiple experiments show that fatigue, reactive inhibition, motor planning, and time-of-day can materially affect what looks like a learning gain.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT / CONTRADICTORY: Walker et al. and music-keyboard studies support sleep-associated gains under specified designs. Rickard et al. found no special sleep enhancement after reducing key confounds. Bönstrup et al. interpret micro-offline gains as rapid consolidation; Gupta and Rickard favor a hybrid model with reactive inhibition; Das et al. show that micro-offline gains can disappear under standardized retention tests and can occur without repeating a learned sequence. These results do not share one endpoint or one theoretical assumption.


PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES: A difficult but personally valued Amastacia–Ankora coordination may become more consistently available across independent playing occasions, perhaps partly through offline stabilization. Some immediate post-rest fluency may instead reflect relief from transient strain or improved motor planning. Natural sleep and rest may matter, but their contributions and effect sizes in Paul's playing are unknown.


SPECULATION: A broadening repertoire of implicitly available hand–breath gestures might permit longer, more absorbing performer flow without requiring Paul to monitor mechanics. That is an artistically coherent possibility, not an established neurophysiological effect and not a listener claim.


UNSUPPORTED: 'KLAAT improves itself while Paul sleeps'; 'ten-second breaks consolidate every gesture'; 'a smoother drone proves motor-memory replay'; 'night practice is optimal for everyone'; 'more practice or more sleep guarantees stronger listener trance'; '137 BPM or C# activates consolidation'; or 'the musician's flow state can be read directly from a clean waveform.'


12. WHAT THIS CHANGES IN THE RESEARCH PROGRAM


First, future analyses of Paul's recordings should preserve playing occasion, time-of-day where trustworthy, approximate sleep/rest context only if voluntarily supplied, instrument conditions, and whether a technical event was intentional. Second, a single impressive next-day transition should be described as an observation, not as evidence for a particular consolidation mechanism. Third, listener studies should not treat the performer's technical smoothness as a validated proxy for listener activation or absorption. Finally, the research can support a valuable artistic aim: making more technique available without prescribing which technique must appear in a performance.


The larger insight is a separation between the living performance and the slowly changing abilities that make it possible. KLAAT is not a sequence to be memorized. It is an improvised acoustic world whose creator accumulates options over time. Rest and sleep may help preserve some options; practice develops them; fatigue and attention affect when they can be expressed. We should not confuse any one of these processes with the music's meaning.


13. OPEN QUESTIONS


Does Paul's circular-breathing coordination feel easier on a later day without becoming acoustically more uniform? Do naturally occurring Ankora transitions show fewer unwanted hesitations across independent occasions even as the rhythms themselves remain different? Does an immediate improvement after a pause persist after another few minutes of playing, or disappear as effort accumulates? Can a skill become more available while Paul's subjective flow stays unchanged? Could some deliberate imperfections be artistically valued and therefore intentionally retained? Can a future listener hear a difference in expressive continuity without being told which take came later? None of these has been tested in KLAAT.


14. ONE OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION


On an ordinary day when a particular Amastacia–Ankora coordination happens to feel difficult, make a brief AFTER-PLAYING note in your own words about what felt awkward. If you naturally encounter that same kind of movement again on another day, note whether it feels easier, unchanged, or harder, and whether you were fresh or tired. There is no requirement to repeat the gesture, count successes, set a tempo, film the attempt, or change the music. Two impressions cannot prove sleep consolidation; they can begin a useful record of how your personal technique evolves.


The conclusion: AN IMPROVED MOMENT AFTER REST IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY NEW LEARNING. Genuine motor skill can change across practice and delayed intervals, but the observable result also depends on fatigue, planning, sleep, context, and artistic choice. For KLAAT, the purpose of understanding those processes is to expand freedom inside the improvisation—not to regulate the improvisation.
