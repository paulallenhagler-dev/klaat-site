KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 151
The Bass That a Sound Meter Hid
Why A-weighted readings alone cannot characterize Amastacia's low-frequency acoustic presence
November 5, 2026 · 19:16 CST
Guiding principle: Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


RESEARCH QUESTION
If an acoustic measurement of a spontaneous Amastacia–Ankora performance reports a modest dBA value, does that establish that the drone contributes little low-frequency sound pressure? No. The answer depends on the frequency weighting, source spectrum, calibration, location, and measurement interval. This is an interpretation problem, not a suggestion to alter the music.

1. WHAT A SOUND LEVEL ACTUALLY MEASURES
A sound pressure level is a logarithmic expression of root-mean-square acoustic pressure relative to a reference pressure (20 micropascals in air). In simplified form Lp = 20 log10(prms / 20 µPa). The number depends on the measurement bandwidth and duration, even before any psychoacoustic interpretation. The microphone samples pressure at a position; it does not measure what a particular person feels in the chest, how emotionally intense the music is, or whether the listener becomes absorbed.


Frequency weighting is a defined filter applied to the measured signal before its level is calculated. A-weighting greatly attenuates low frequencies, C-weighting attenuates them less, and Z-weighting is approximately flat across a meter's specified operating band. Their suffixes are not interchangeable: 70 dBA, 70 dBC, and 70 dBZ are different statements about a sound. A-weighting is widely useful for standardized exposure and environmental measurements; it is not defective merely because it discounts bass. The error occurs when its reading is misrepresented as a complete description of low-frequency acoustic energy.


2. THE 63-HZ EXAMPLE: WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN
At the standardized 63-Hz nominal frequency, the A-weighting correction is approximately −26.2 dB; C-weighting is approximately −0.8 dB. Thus an ideal isolated 63-Hz tone at 80 dB unweighted would indicate approximately 53.8 dBA and 79.2 dBC, under matching measurement conditions. This is an illustration of filter transfer functions, not an observation of KLAAT. The apparent difference of roughly 25 dB does not mean the instrument's sound energy vanished, nor that C-weighting gives an exact measure of physical vibration. The same air-pressure waveform passes through different mathematical filters.


A nominal C#2 equal-tempered frequency is 69.30 Hz when A4 = 440 Hz. That places the musical label near the 63-Hz example, but it does not prove Amastacia's measured fundamental is exactly 69.30 Hz. Instrument construction, breath articulation, playing conditions, and actual spectral measurement determine the emitted signal. Nor does a C# drone consist only of its fundamental: didgeridoo timbre contains harmonics, broadband breath noise, formant-like resonances, and time-varying components. Ankora's attacks add broadband energy and resonant modes. A correction at 63 Hz therefore cannot be applied wholesale to an entire KLAAT performance.


3. WHY THE SINGLE NUMBER IS NOT ENOUGH
A measured difference LCeq − LAeq can suggest that low-frequency content materially affects the aggregate reading. But this difference is not a spectrum. Multiple different spectral distributions can produce the same two weighted totals. A quiet low-frequency fundamental with strong upper harmonics may sound perceptually prominent; conversely a high low-frequency SPL may be less audible in a room location near a cancellation or with inadequate headphones. The readings also depend on whether the same interval, meter, calibration, and location were used.


For future KLAAT recordings, a more defensible characterization would pair calibrated LAeq,T, LCeq,T and, if available, LZeq,T with a low-frequency spectrum or one-third-octave bands and a precise time interval. A sound-level meter with a compliant frequency response is preferable to an unvalidated phone microphone for absolute bass measurements. A consumer phone may be useful for comparative sketches but its microphone, filtering, automatic gain, and frequency response can severely compromise low-frequency accuracy. A nominally flat Z weighting is still constrained by the instrument's microphone and frequency range; it does not measure indefinitely toward zero hertz.


4. THREE DISTINCT CLAIMS THAT MUST STAY DISTINCT
ACOUSTIC: The recorded waveform contains a measurable low-frequency pressure component. This can be established with appropriately calibrated microphones and spectral analysis, subject to room and equipment limitations.


PERCEPTUAL: A listener reports bass weight, darkness, pressure, warmth, or an energetic pull. These are subjective observations, potentially influenced by harmonic structure, overall level, prior expectation, room acoustics, playback transducers, and individual hearing. They cannot be read directly from an SPL meter.


PHYSIOLOGICAL: A listener shows an autonomic, motor, respiratory, or other bodily response. This requires a separate measurement and careful interpretation. Audible bass and mechanical vibration are not identical: a person may feel structural vibration through floors or furniture, while headphones can create strong bass perception without comparable whole-body vibration. Neither a large C-minus-A difference nor a C# fundamental establishes a particular nervous-system response.


CAUSAL: A KLAAT-specific acoustic feature produced the response. This stronger claim requires comparative exposures, controlled playback levels, participant-level accounting, and outcomes specified before interpreting results. There is currently no KLAAT-specific evidence demonstrating trance, neural entrainment, therapeutic benefit, or consistent physiological activation.


5. THE ROOM AND THE RECORDING ARE PART OF THE QUESTION
At roughly 69 Hz, wavelength in air at 343 m/s is about 4.95 m. That scale is comparable to dimensions of ordinary rooms. Reflections and standing-wave patterns can therefore change the local pressure of a low-frequency component considerably. Moving a microphone or listener can change the measured bass without changing the performance. This is one reason a single microphone reading at one point should not be treated as a complete map of the acoustic field.


A phone recording can also exaggerate or suppress bass through high-pass filters, automatic gain control, compression, and microphone placement. Conversely a playback speaker may have insufficient extension near the drone's fundamental while reproducing upper harmonics that preserve an impression of pitch. The artistic source, the captured waveform, and the listener's delivered sound are separate stages. This extends the concerns of earlier sessions about Bluetooth microphone mode, performer-versus-listener sound, and recording-clock discrepancies, without repeating those studies.


6. WHAT SCIENCE ESTABLISHES AND WHAT REMAINS OPEN
ESTABLISHED: Standard A, C and Z weightings are distinct measurement filters; A-weighting substantially attenuates 63-Hz energy; acoustic SPL is location- and bandwidth-dependent; rooms and playback systems can reshape the delivered spectrum.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: The amount by which a real KLAAT performance's dBA value differs from dBC or dBZ depends on its complete spectrum, attack structure, room, distance, and meter settings. The 63-Hz example cannot predict that difference.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE: In some listening environments, the perceived bodily weight of Amastacia may correspond more closely to calibrated low-frequency spectral energy or C/Z-weighted readings than to a single dBA value. Whether that relationship is strong, weak, or absent must be established with listener reports and controlled comparisons.


SPECULATIVE: Low-frequency energy interacting with Ankora's pulse might increase bodily activation or sustained absorption for certain listeners. This is a research question, not a finding. Strong bass can also be unpleasant, fatiguing, or distracting; effects need not be universally positive.


UNSUPPORTED: Claims that a C# drone uniquely induces trance; that a dBC−dBA gap measures somatic intensity; that a phone app reliably measures sub-bass without validation; that a spectral peak proves neurological entrainment; or that KLAAT has already demonstrated therapeutic outcomes.


7. IMPLICATION FOR THE ART
The point is not to make Amastacia louder, tune Ankora to a frequency, chase a decibel target, or impose phases on improvisation. KLAAT is strongest as an experience-led artistic practice whose actual sound can be examined after the fact. A measured low-frequency profile can help explain why one recording sounds physically compelling on full-range speakers and less so on a phone, or why a live room differs from a headphone version. It can also protect the project from false negatives: a low dBA number is not sufficient grounds to dismiss the drone's bass contribution. Equally, a large bass measurement is not grounds to announce a listener effect that has not been observed.


8. ONE OPTIONAL OBSERVATION
On a day when suitable calibrated equipment is available, measure one ordinary spontaneous KLAAT excerpt from a fixed listener position, using the same time interval for LAeq, LCeq and LZeq and retaining one-third-octave or spectral data. Record room position and playback/capture conditions. Do not alter the performance to serve the meter. If such equipment is unavailable, simply preserve the rawest practical recording and note the microphone and room; no experiment is required.


DURABLE FINDING
A-weighted SPL is a valid standardized measurement but an incomplete descriptor of Amastacia's low-frequency acoustic content. Calibrated spectral measurements and explicitly labeled weightings are needed to characterize the physical signal; listener experience and physiology remain separate empirical questions.
