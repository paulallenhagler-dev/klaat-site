THE SOUNDTRACK THAT CANNOT DRIVE FOR YOU

Activation, divided attention, and the difference between feeling alert and behaving safely

SESSION 113 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

Published: October 23, 2026 — 08:20 CDT (America/Chicago) Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT. Evidence status: A critical synthesis of primary experiments conducted with other music, listeners, and driving tasks. No KLAAT driving study, listener test, physiological measurement, or recording analysis has been conducted.

THE QUESTION

KLAAT is not only an event to attend with eyes closed. Amastacia's sustained, breathing drone and Ankora's pulse can belong to daily life: walking, working with one's hands, drawing, traveling, and being alert in the ordinary world. This breadth matters artistically. An improvised performance can be absorbing without demanding that the listener abandon every other activity.

But “energizing” has an unresolved meaning. Does a soundtrack that makes someone feel more awake help them perform a demanding task? Can it simultaneously improve one measure of performance and impair another? Could an absorbing piece make monotonous driving feel less dull while making a rare, peripheral hazard harder to notice? The scientific answer is not a simple yes or no. It is that the endpoints must be separated, and the circumstances matter enormously.

This session uses driving as a stringent case study, not as a recommended KLAAT experiment on public roads. Driving combines sustained vigilance, visual search, vehicle control, rapid responses to unexpected events, and access to auditory warnings. It therefore exposes a distinction central to KLAAT's everyday-listening ambitions: felt activation is not equivalent to safer or better performance. The study does not propose changing Paul's music to suit a vehicle, choosing a scientifically approved beat, or testing intense listening during real driving.

1. A NEW VALIDITY GATE: ACTIVATION IS NOT PERFORMANCE

At least six different variables can hide behind the sentence “this music keeps me going.” They are (1) positive or negative mood, (2) felt arousal or wakefulness, (3) bodily activation such as movement or physiological response, (4) vigilance or sustained attention, (5) measured task accuracy and response speed, and (6) risk-taking or willingness to accept a smaller safety margin. These dimensions can move in different directions.

Someone may feel intensely awake, maintain a lane more steadily, and still scan the periphery less often. A faster response to a lead car's change in speed may coexist with closer following distance. A person may prefer the music while becoming less attentive to an external warning. None of these contradictions is logically impossible; they arise because performance is multidimensional.

For KLAAT, this gate prevents a tempting inference. If a listener says that Ankora makes a commute more energetic, the report establishes a subjective experience of energy. It does not demonstrate improved hazard perception, faster reaction, reduced fatigue, safer driving, or beneficial autonomic change. The listener may be sincere and the acoustic event may be powerful; the causal claim still requires independent evidence.

2. MONOTONOUS DRIVING: A REAL POSITIVE RESULT WITH NARROW BOUNDARIES

Ünal, de Waard, Epstude, and Steg (2013) examined music in a low-complexity car-following simulator. Forty-seven participants were assigned to moderate- or loud-music groups and completed drives with and without music. The researchers recorded driving behavior and heart rate, using physiological measures to examine arousal and effort.

Music did not reduce car-following accuracy in this particular setting. It was associated with favorable changes in response latency to the lead vehicle's speed changes and in lateral vehicle control. Arousal was higher with music than without it, but the loud condition was not more arousing than the moderate condition. The heart-rate-variability measure used to infer mental effort did not differ between the music and no-music conditions [1].

The finding is useful because it directly contradicts “any music must impair attention.” Under monotonous, relatively simple conditions, music may maintain engagement or arousal without degrading the measured task. Yet the experiment did not show that all music helps, that a driver is globally safer, or that increasing volume improves the effect. The task was car-following in a simulator, not an uncontrolled city street full of unpredictable conflicts. The physiological measures were proxies, not direct readouts of a unified mental state.

It also does not establish anything about Amastacia or Ankora. KLAAT's long drone, broadband attacks, dynamic texture, and improvised recurrence may differ substantially from the music selected in the experiment. That uncertainty is a reason to preserve the distinction, not to assign a safety rating to KLAAT.

3. THE OPPOSITE RESULT: ATTENTION CAN NARROW

Beh and Hirst (1999) studied tracking and vigilance tasks under silence and music at different intensities, with lower- and higher-demand conditions. Their results did not follow one simple good/bad pattern. The relatively straightforward tracking task was unaffected. Responses to centrally located visual signals improved under music conditions. Yet high-intensity music was associated with slower responses to peripheral signals when task demand was high [2].

This is one of the most important experimental contrasts in the session. Better central responses can coexist with worse peripheral responses. A single average reaction-time score would conceal that trade-off.

Imagine an unchanged Ankora passage in which several strikes form an absorbing, recognizable pulse. That pulse could make a listener feel engaged; it might even help sustain alertness in a monotonous setting. But if the listener is also responsible for monitoring unpredictable events outside the center of attention, that engagement is not automatically beneficial. The hypothetical KLAAT mechanism is attention allocation, not a specific pulse frequency or a special neural entrainment state. The driving study did not test KLAAT, and the controlled task did not establish real-world collision risk.

The key methodological lesson is broader than driving: a soundtrack's effect depends on which aspect of performance is measured. “Concentration” is not one outcome. Sustained vigilance, serial recall, visual scanning, peripheral detection, and motor control are different tasks with different vulnerabilities. Session 106 established that changing-state sound can disrupt serial-order memory. Session 113 extends the everyday-listening question to a safety-relevant trade-off among arousal, peripheral monitoring, and behavior.

4. AROUSAL IS NOT THE SAME AS TEMPO

It is tempting to classify music by beats per minute and infer the resulting behavior. Ankora's provisional pulse around 137 BPM, and later faster versions of some reference material, might appear to invite such classification. That would be premature. A performed event rate is not the whole experience of musical speed, and perceived activation depends on timbre, loudness, familiarity, preference, rhythm, task, and context.

Navarro, Osiurak, and Reynaud (2018) conducted three experiments manipulating background music during car-following. One experiment altered a driver's preferred track by plus or minus 30 percent tempo; another compared tracks of different tempos; a third contrasted arousing and relaxing tracks. Their conclusion was especially instructive: tempo itself did not consistently determine driving behavior. Arousing music improved responsiveness to changes in the lead vehicle, but it also shortened the intervehicle safety margin. Better adjustment did not amount to improved safety [3].

A separate counterbalanced simulator experiment with 46 participants examined fast, slow, and no music under higher and lower task load. Task load had the strongest overall effects. Under high load, reported arousal was higher with fast than slow music; the heart-rate-variability measure SDNN was lower in the fast condition than in both slow and no-music conditions. A traffic-light risk-rating measure was also highest in the fast-tempo condition [4].

These results need not be forced into a contradiction. They use different music manipulations, traffic scenarios, measures, and participants. One separates perceived arousal from nominal tempo; the other finds conditional effects of a tempo manipulation under particular loads. Neither provides a universally safe BPM. It would be scientifically indefensible to declare 137 BPM safe, 184 BPM unsafe, or any other number a biological threshold for KLAAT.

There is also a problem of ecological interpretation. A driver may unconsciously speed up, but a controlled change in simulator speed is not equivalent to a population estimate of crashes. A lower HRV index under one task condition is not a diagnosis of stress, nor evidence of trance. An outcome must retain its original operational definition.

5. WHEN THE MUSIC IS CHOSEN BY THE DRIVER

Preference adds another complication. People often select music precisely because it changes how they feel. Preferred music may sustain motivation or positive mood, but personal liking can increase engagement with the soundtrack itself. Enjoyment is not a universal safety benefit.

Brodsky and Slor (2013) tested 85 young novice drivers during six trips in an instrumented learner vehicle. Their within-person comparison included driver-preferred music and music selected to provide moderate perceptual complexity. In the study, preferred music increased positive mood and enjoyment, but also accompanied more severe driving errors, violations, and aggressive driving. The authors reported that 27 participants needed a verbal warning or command and 17 needed an instructor's steering or braking intervention at some point across the trips [5]. Those counts describe events across the study, not a claim that music caused each intervention or that every intervention occurred during the preferred-music condition.

This on-road design has an advantage over a computer simulation, but the sample is deliberately narrow: young novice drivers under instruction, not experienced adults in everyday conditions. The particular comparison music was researcher-designed. Its results cannot be translated into “complex music is bad,” “simple music is safe,” or “KLAAT should be less intense.” They show that subjective enjoyment and observable driving deficiencies can diverge, especially in a vulnerable task population.

The 2025 experiment by Morrongiello and colleagues offers a related but not identical picture. Adolescents aged 17–19 completed two 25-minute simulator drives, one with their favorite music. The study found reduced visual attention to the road with music for both male and female participants, yet no overall music effect on speed or hazard reaction time, and improved steering performance. Among male participants, music was associated with more one-handed driving; that behavior correlated with more hazard contacts [6]. A related thesis reports a sample of 56 (32 female, 24 male), but the published paper's abstract is the main basis for the outcome claims.

Again, mixed outcomes are the point. If one had measured only steering, the story would look favorable. If one measured visual attention, it would look unfavorable. If one measured hazard response time, one might conclude little changed. A well-designed KLAAT study must not select whichever outcome flatters the desired artistic narrative.

6. ANOTHER CONTRADICTION: MOOD MAY IMPROVE WITHOUT CARDIAC CHANGE

Van der Zwaag and colleagues (2012) used individually selected music in simulated drives with normal and high cognitive demand. Music helped maintain the intended mood. During music listening, respiration rate was lower than in the no-music conditions, while heart rate showed no music effect. Narrow lanes increased reported effort and swerving, demonstrating that task load remained an important determinant [7].

The physiology is a useful warning against reducing “activation” to a single bodily channel. Music-related mood regulation can occur without a detectable heart-rate difference in the measured setting. Lower respiration rate does not necessarily mean sedation, improved driving, vagal entrainment, or that the driver's breathing synchronized to the music. The experimental result is a group-level comparison in a particular simulator protocol.

KLAAT's breath-led acoustic identity makes respiratory interpretations especially tempting. A continuous Amastacia drone is produced by an active breathing musician, but it does not automatically entrain the listener's respiration. The performer, acoustic signal, and listener have distinct physiological clocks. A person might hear the breath texture and report calm or activation while their measured respiration does something else. Both measurements can be valid.

7. THE UNHEARD WARNING

Driving adds an outcome that ordinary music-listening questionnaires often ignore: hearing an external signal when it matters. Vehicle interiors contain engine, road, wind, speech, and entertainment sound. A warning tone can be acoustically present but masked or overlooked. Its detectability depends on frequency content, level, duration, background spectrum, spatial cues, and listener attention.

Antin, Lauretta, and Wolf (1991) tested 24 people with auditory warning tones under recorded vehicle noise and radio conditions. They measured intensity relative to masked threshold needed for consistent detection and listeners' preferred warning intensity. The preferred and detectable levels did not coincide in the same way across backgrounds; the radio condition was different enough that the authors recommended further research on automatically muting entertainment audio during critical warnings [8].

This does not mean Amastacia's low fundamental would necessarily mask a siren or that Ankora's attacks would necessarily obscure a horn. Actual overlap depends on the spectrum reaching the ear, not on the name of the instrument. But it demonstrates why loudness preference, enjoyment, and safety-critical signal detection cannot be collapsed into one score.

For everyday KLAAT listening, the implication is not to redesign the improvisation. It is to recognize that playback level and listening environment are part of the experiment. An unedited recording can be artistically intact while being unsuitable at a level that interferes with awareness of surroundings. That is a delivery and context issue, not a defect in the musical material.

8. WHAT ABOUT FAST MUSIC AND SPEEDING?

Brodsky's earlier 2001 simulator work reported faster simulated driving and more virtual traffic violations with faster background music [9]. That finding is often retold as though tempo alone controls driving speed. But the 2018 three-experiment study by Navarro and colleagues found no consistent tempo effect when tempo and arousal were more explicitly separated [3]. Both studies are primary evidence; neither erases the other.

The appropriate synthesis is conditional. Music can influence speed or risk measures in some designs, but a universal tempo-to-speed law is not supported. The driver's experience, task demand, music familiarity, manipulation method, and measurement endpoint may change the result. The causal question is not “does a 137-BPM beat make people drive at a corresponding speed?” There is no meaningful direct conversion from BPM to miles per hour. The question is whether the particular listening condition changes behavior relative to a relevant comparison.

KLAAT should therefore resist a second category error: equating a felt musical acceleration with a measured increase in physical action. Session 103 distinguished locomotor vigor from exact synchronization; this session distinguishes music-related arousal from task-specific safety and performance. The fact that Ankora can energize a listener is an artistic possibility worth exploring. It is not a license to claim improved driving.

9. WHY KLAAT MAY BE DIFFERENT—AND WHY WE DO NOT KNOW

Much of the driving literature uses familiar songs, participant-selected playlists, edited tempo versions, or genre categories. KLAAT's defining features are different: a continuous C#-centered agave drone, breath articulation, harmonic shifts, noise and texture, and a frame-drum pulse whose density and timing arise spontaneously. There may be no lyrics, conventional verse structure, or predictable recording edit. That makes the research relevant as a methodological warning, not as a ready-made forecast.

Three plausible, mutually compatible KLAAT hypotheses can be stated without pretending they are results.

HYPOTHESIS A — CONTINUOUS PRESENCE: Amastacia's drone could provide a stable, nonverbal acoustic backdrop that some listeners find less intrusive than changing-state, lyric-heavy music. Alternatively, its shifting harmonics and noise could draw attention or mask environmental cues. We cannot decide from the instrument name.

HYPOTHESIS B — TRANSIENT ORIENTING: Ankora's impacts could help maintain subjective alertness in an otherwise monotonous setting. The same impacts might distract from a concurrent warning or external event. A change in alertness is not a demonstrated gain in task accuracy.

HYPOTHESIS C — ABSORPTION TRADE-OFF: Some listeners may become absorbed in KLAAT's evolving perceptual field, which could be rewarding during dedicated listening or creative work. That same absorption might be unwelcome during tasks demanding immediate response to unpredictable hazards. Performer flow, listener absorption, and task performance must remain independent observations.

None of these hypotheses requires a tempo prescription, a new composition structure, or a modification to the sound Paul already makes. The next useful evidence would come from real KLAAT recordings and listener reports collected in safe, non-driving contexts, with acoustic and behavioral outcomes separated.

10. AN OUTCOME MAP FOR EVERYDAY KLAAT

A useful research vocabulary would record five distinct layers for an unchanged excerpt.

SOURCE: Which original performance, take, duration, instrument configuration, and recording chain produced the sound?

DELIVERY: What playback device, level, environment, and competing background sounds reached the listener?

EXPERIENCE: What did the listener freely describe—energy, tension, absorption, bodily sensation, pleasure, distraction, or nothing remarkable?

TASK: If a task was present, what exactly was measured—errors, response times, visual scanning, serial recall, persistence, movement, or a self-rated sense of productivity?

INFERENCE: Which claim is warranted by those observations, and which alternative explanations remain open?

This map also guards against “good music for driving” becoming an unexamined label. A soundtrack can support mood during a monotonous commute and still be inappropriate when the driver needs to hear an emergency vehicle or attend to complicated traffic. KLAAT does not have to be universally useful to be artistically successful.

11. THE PROBLEM OF TRANSLATING A SIMULATOR INTO A LIFE

The driving literature has methodological strengths: controlled within-person comparisons, defined hazards, heart-rate measures, and direct behavioral outcomes. It also has limitations. Simulator participants know they are not on a real road. The duration is often short. A high-demand condition is defined by the researcher's scenario. Some studies test young novices; others recruit adults with different experience. Self-selected playlists introduce familiarity and preference, while edited-tempo tracks may change timbre or other attributes. Listening level is not always acoustically comparable across experiments.

Most importantly, the studies measure specific tasks, not the metaphysical property of “being activated.” Improved steering in one simulator condition is not a demonstrated reduction in crashes. A heart-rate-variability difference is not proof of a distinct consciousness state. A subjective rating of alertness is not equivalent to measured vigilance. A hazard response in a controlled clip is not proof of safe performance in traffic.

These limitations do not make the research useless. They make its claims appropriately sized. The best scientific use for KLAAT is to learn from the contradictory pattern: under some conditions music may support mood or monotonous-task engagement, while under others it may narrow attention, alter risk, or mask warnings. The outcome depends on task and context as much as the music.

12. WHAT WE CAN SAY NOW

ESTABLISHED IN NON-KLAAT EXPERIMENTS: Music can affect mood, subjective arousal, selected physiological measures, visual attention, driving-related control, and risk-relevant behavior. These effects are not uniformly beneficial or harmful. Specific studies have found favorable car-following or steering outcomes alongside reduced visual attention, smaller following margins, or greater driving deficiencies. Background sound can affect warning-tone detectability.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Tempo effects vary across designs; preferred music can improve enjoyment without improving safety; task load, listening level, age, experience, and background sound matter. A simulator outcome should not be presented as a real-world crash rate. Respiratory or cardiac differences are not direct proof of a particular mental state.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: Amastacia's sustained acoustic continuity and Ankora's intermittent attacks may influence energy, absorption, distraction, and task engagement differently across listening contexts. Existing unedited recordings could support safe comparisons during ordinary, nonhazardous desk tasks or dedicated listening. There is no known effect direction or size.

SPECULATIVE: KLAAT's combination of stable drone and emerging pulse may produce a distinctive balance of alertness and immersion. This is a possible artistic-perceptual signature, not a validated property or a brain-state discovery.

UNSUPPORTED: KLAAT improves driving, reaction time, focus, productivity, road safety, or physiological regulation; 137 or 184 BPM is a safe/unsafe neurological setting; a felt bodily surge proves entrainment; an uninterrupted drone makes external warnings easier to hear; a participant's favorite track is always safer than silence; or an enjoyable performance must be cognitively beneficial.

13. THE UNRESOLVED QUESTION

KLAAT's larger scientific question is not whether it should become a driving soundtrack. It is whether its activation and absorption can be described precisely enough to know when those experiences support, coexist with, or compete against an external task. That question has a richer answer than “fast music energizes” or “repetitive music induces trance.”

The immediate next step is not to modify Amastacia's pitch or Ankora's pulse. It is to collect the recordings Paul already makes, document their acoustic character honestly, and ask listeners what actually happens. A soundtrack that feels strong may be a wonderful artistic achievement without becoming a performance-enhancement tool. Science can help preserve that distinction.

OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-113 (SAFE, NON-DRIVING)

Choose one existing, unchanged two- or three-minute KLAAT recording. On a quiet day, listen once at a comfortable level while doing an ordinary low-risk desk activity such as sorting a few objects; on another occasion, perform the same activity in quiet. Do not treat this as a controlled trial or draw productivity conclusions from one comparison. Simply record the time/context, whether the task felt easier or harder, whether the music was enjoyable or distracting, and one concrete task observation if available. Preserve “no difference” as a valid answer. Do not test with driving, cycling, traffic navigation, machinery, or any other safety-critical activity. Do not change the improvisation to fit the test.

THE RETURN

Amastacia can hold a world open. Ankora can make that world move. Neither instrument can steer a car, scan a blind corner, or guarantee a safer decision. KLAAT's ambition to be an energizing everyday soundtrack is artistically sound; the research task is to discover where its effects begin, where they end, and what remains uncertain. Activation is an experience. Performance is an observation. Safety is an outcome that must be earned by evidence.

Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.
