AUDITORY SCENE ANALYSIS: HOW THE BRAIN DECIDES WHETHER AMASTACIA AND ANKORA ARE TWO INSTRUMENTS—OR ONE SOUNDING ORGANISM

ORIGINAL SESSION TEXT PRESERVED FROM PROJECT CONVERSATION

SESSION 044 / 164Pulse & RhythmRESEARCH SESSION

Session 43 gave us roughness as a controllable dimension of activation. We now have a growing set of variables: amplitude, pulse rate, event density, predictability, masking, spectral density and roughness.

But there is a deeper question underneath all of them:

When Amastacia and Ankora are sounding simultaneously, what does the listener think they are hearing?

Not intellectually. Perceptually.

The acoustic world doesn't arrive at the ears conveniently labeled:

DIDGERIDOO

DRUM

ROOM REFLECTION

VOICE

TRAFFIC OUTSIDE

Instead, the ears receive two continuously changing pressure waveforms. The auditory system has to infer which pieces belong together.

That process is generally studied under auditory scene analysis.

And it gives KLAAT a completely new compositional target:

control over whether Drone and Pulse remain perceptually separate or fuse into a single auditory object.

The cocktail-party problem

Imagine entering a crowded restaurant.

Physically, sound from dozens of sources reaches your ears simultaneously:

voices,

silverware,

music,

HVAC,

footsteps,

glassware.

Yet you can often follow one person's voice.

Your auditory system has somehow grouped parts of the acoustic mixture into:

that person speaking.

This is called auditory streaming or auditory object formation.

The brain uses several clues to decide what probably came from the same source.

Among them are:

frequency relationships,

harmonicity,

common onset,

common amplitude changes,

spatial location,

timbre,

temporal continuity.

No single cue always determines the answer.

The nervous system combines evidence.

Common onset is powerful

Imagine several frequencies begin at exactly the same moment.

The auditory system has reason to infer:

these components probably came from one event.

That makes sense ecologically.

Strike an object and many of its resonant modes begin together.

So simultaneous onset is evidence of a common physical cause.

Now think about Ankora.

A strike produces many frequency components almost simultaneously:

THUMP.

The auditory system groups those frequencies into:

one drum event

rather than hearing dozens of unrelated tones.

Harmonicity provides another grouping cue

Suppose components occur at approximately:

100 Hz

200 Hz

300 Hz

400 Hz

500 Hz.

Their integer relationships suggest a shared periodic source.

The auditory system tends to group harmonically related components.

This is one reason a harmonically rich sound can still be perceived as one pitch-bearing object rather than many individual tones.

Amastacia depends heavily on this.

Its spectrum can be enormously complex while still being heard as:

one drone.

But vocalization and nonlinear playing can weaken or complicate that harmonic organization.

As you increase spectral instability and roughness, parts of Amastacia may begin feeling less like one unified tone and more like a collection of interacting textures.

That's a perceptual transition we can exploit.

Temporal coherence matters enormously

Suppose two spectral regions rise and fall together.

Every time one gets louder, the other gets louder.

Every time one weakens, the other weakens.

The auditory system has evidence that they belong to the same source.

This principle is sometimes described in terms of temporal coherence.

Now imagine Amastacia's spectral components fluctuate with your breath.

They move together.

The brain groups them.

But Ankora follows an independent amplitude envelope:

attack → decay

attack → decay

attack → decay.

Therefore Drone and Pulse initially provide strong evidence that they are separate auditory objects.

But KLAAT can deliberately weaken that separation

Imagine Ankora strikes exactly when Amastacia receives a strong breath-pressure accent.

Every time:

Drone swells + Drum strikes

Again:

Drone swells + Drum strikes

Again:

Drone swells + Drum strikes.

Now two previously independent sound sources share temporal structure.

The auditory system receives increasing evidence that they belong together.

Not physically—they obviously remain two instruments.

But perceptually, the boundary can become less distinct.

The listener may begin experiencing something closer to:

one composite event.

This is potentially huge for KLAAT.

Synchronization can create perceptual fusion

We've previously discussed synchronization mainly as rhythm.

Now consider synchronization as source identity.

Early performance:

Amastacia —————————

Ankora THUMP THUMP

Clearly separate behaviors.

Later:

Amastacia ———WAA———WAA———WAA——

Ankora THUMP THUMP THUMP

If those Amastacia articulations precisely coincide with Ankora, they begin forming repeating compound events:

WAA-THUMP

The auditory system learns that unit.

After enough repetition, the perceptual object may no longer be:

“didgeridoo plus drum.”

It becomes:

that KLAAT event.

That is exactly the kind of transformation worth pursuing.

Spatial position can strengthen or weaken separation

Auditory localization provides another grouping cue.

If Amastacia comes strongly from the listener's left and Ankora from the right, their identities remain relatively easy to separate.

Bring their apparent acoustic origins closer together and spatial evidence for separation decreases.

Room reflections complicate this further.

In a reverberant space, reflected energy surrounds the listener.

Source boundaries can become less precise.

This means room acoustics could affect whether KLAAT feels like:

two performers/instruments in a room

or:

one acoustic field occupying the room.

That is a much more interesting use of reverberation than simply wanting things to sound “big.”

Attention can reorganize the scene

Here's another fascinating part.

The acoustic signal can remain identical while perception changes depending on what the listener attends to.

Listen specifically for Ankora:

suddenly you notice membrane decay, tiny timing differences, high attack components.

Shift attention to Amastacia:

the drum may recede while internal harmonics become prominent.

Shift attention to the room:

reflections appear.

Nothing physically changed.

Perceptual organization changed.

This is another reason KLAAT shouldn't constantly demand attention through novelty.

Long repetition can allow listeners to explore different layers of the same acoustic environment.

One person may spend several minutes “inside” Ankora.

Then Amastacia suddenly becomes foreground.

Then breath.

Then room resonance.

The stimulus remains relatively stable while subjective experience moves.

That is valuable.

There is a famous streaming phenomenon

Present alternating tones:

HIGH – LOW – HIGH – LOW – HIGH – LOW

Slowly, listeners often hear:

HIGH-LOW-HIGH-LOW

as one alternating sequence.

Increase the presentation rate or frequency separation and perception can reorganize into:

HIGH HIGH HIGH HIGH

and

LOW LOW LOW LOW

as two simultaneous streams.

The physical sequence hasn't changed category.

The brain's organization of it has.

This demonstrates something central:

auditory objects are perceptual constructions.

There isn't always one objectively correct way to divide an acoustic scene.

KLAAT can compose that boundary

Now imagine the whole session architecture through this lens.

Ground Anchor

Amastacia establishes one dominant auditory object.

Threshold Gate

Ankora appears as a clearly separate object:

Drone + Pulse.

Rising Pulse

Timing becomes increasingly coordinated.

Amastacia articulations synchronize with Ankora.

Spectral density increases.

Masking increases.

Room energy increases.

Auditory and tactile events align.

The distinction between Drone and Pulse may gradually weaken.

At peak intensity, the listener could experience:

KLAAT as one multisensory object.

Then Return Path begins.

Ankora separates.

Density decreases.

Temporal coupling weakens.

The composite object decomposes:

KLAAT → Drone + Pulse → Drone → Room.

That gives the architecture another interpretation.

You're not only changing intensity.

You're performing:

assembly → fusion → disassembly.

This may be the strongest conceptual model we've developed yet

Because it connects several previous sessions.

Masking controls which components remain visible to hearing.

Roughness changes internal spectral texture.

Temporal prediction creates shared timing expectations.

Vibrotactile synchrony connects bodily and auditory events.

Adaptation establishes sensory baselines.

Auditory scene analysis determines whether all those components remain separate or become one perceptual object.

The pieces are starting to converge.

KLAAT can potentially be understood not as two instruments producing music but as a deliberately constructed multisensory perceptual system.

That's a much stronger research direction.

Evidence boundary

Established: the auditory system organizes complex acoustic mixtures into perceptual streams and auditory objects.

Established: harmonic relationships, common onset, temporal coherence, spatial location and spectral characteristics contribute to auditory grouping.

Established: identical acoustic sequences can sometimes produce different perceptual organizations.

Established: attention influences which components of an auditory scene become perceptually prominent.

Established: spatial separation can assist segregation of competing sound sources.

Highly plausible: Amastacia and Ankora can move between stronger perceptual separation and stronger perceptual integration depending on timing, spectral overlap and spatial presentation.

Testable: synchronizing Amastacia articulations precisely with Ankora should increase listener reports that the instruments feel perceptually unified.

Unknown: whether the particular KLAAT performance techniques you're currently using already produce a reliable fusion point.

We haven't measured it.

Not established: perceptual fusion means the listener's nervous system, instruments and performers have literally become physically synchronized as one resonant system.

That's poetic language, not a scientific conclusion.

KLAAT Experiment #44 — Find the Fusion Point

Use one simple Ankora pulse.

Keep tempo and intensity constant.

Perform three approximately two-minute conditions.

Condition 1 — Independence

Play Amastacia continuously.

Do not coordinate its articulations with Ankora.

Let Drone and Pulse behave independently.

Condition 2 — Partial coupling

Begin occasionally emphasizing Amastacia on Ankora strikes.

Maybe every fourth Pulse.

Then every second.

Don't change volume dramatically.

You're changing temporal relationship.

Condition 3 — Strong coupling

Synchronize Amastacia's strongest articulations as precisely as possible with Ankora.

Create a repeating compound event:

DRONE-THUMP — DRONE-THUMP — DRONE-THUMP.

Afterward, don't ask whether they liked one more.

Ask:

“At any point did the drum and drone stop feeling like two separate sounds?”

Then:

“If so, when?”

Repeat it with different listeners.

If people independently identify a similar transition, you've discovered something extremely useful:

KLAAT has a perceptual fusion threshold.

Eventually we can manipulate that deliberately.

Ground Anchor establishes the Drone.

Threshold Gate introduces the Pulse.

Rising Pulse gradually destroys the distinction between them.

At peak:

there is no Amastacia.

There is no Ankora.

There is simply KLAAT.

And Return Path pulls that perceptual organism apart until the listener is left with the thing that was there before either instrument began:

the room.
