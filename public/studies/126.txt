KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 126
THE SOUND WITHOUT A WORD
Instrumental-music captions, temporal information, and the boundary between access and experience


Published: October 27, 2026 — 15:19 CDT (America/Chicago)
Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH STATUS
This is a critical synthesis of primary Deaf/hard-of-hearing (DHH) user research, a 2026 instrumental-music captioning experiment, an earlier behavioral/EEG study, and W3C accessibility guidance. None of these experiments used KLAAT. No KLAAT recording, website accessibility audit, DHH collaborator interview, acoustic waveform, listener report, neural response, trance response, or physiological response was analyzed for this session. The example descriptions below are editorial prototypes, NOT transcriptions of a verified recording. Numbers refer only to the cited external studies.


THE QUESTION


A visitor arrives at KLAAT's website. The visual world is sparse, monumental, and archaeosonic: dark fields, geometry, moving nodes, the suggestion of an ancient machine without an explanatory manual. The visitor encounters a recording of Amastacia, the C# agave didgeridoo, and Ankora, the elk-hide frame drum. No one is speaking. The musical information lives in a sustained acoustic field, the onset and decay of hand strikes, changing breath articulation, roughness, resonance, and the evolving relationship between drone and pulse.


What should the website communicate to someone who cannot hear that recording, or who hears only parts of its spectrum? A caption that merely reads '[music]' identifies the category but erases almost everything particular to KLAAT. A paragraph promising 'a trance-inducing, bodily vibrating journey' may communicate artistic intention, but it replaces audible facts with unverified listener outcomes. An elaborate animated caption that says 'DUM—DUM—DUM' on a steady grid might invent a regular meter that Paul's actual improvisation never supplied.


This session asks whether nonverbal music can be represented through text and optional time-aligned cues without confusing three separate things: information about an acoustic event; a person's access to that information; and the experience that person may or may not have. The question matters to KLAAT as an accessible artwork, as an honest scientific research project, and as an everyday listening soundtrack rather than only a ceremonial performance.


1. THE NOVELTY GATE: CONTENT IS NOT SYNCHRONIZATION


Before assigning Session 126, the connected KLAAT Master Instructions, Curriculum Index, Research Ledger, Session Corpus and Volume II through Session 125 were checked; the canonical Library Published Studies and the hourly research notes added after Session 125 (08:23 CDT) were reviewed. The Curation Queue was empty at the start. The October 27, 13:23 hourly note identified this specific gap, and targeted Library searches found it as a new hourly proposal rather than an already published treatment.


Session 123, THE PULSE THAT THE PICTURE INVENTED, investigated whether animated visual motion faithfully communicates Ankora's timing. It distinguished expressive imagery from a measured audiovisual event representation. That remains important, but it is not today's question. A silent animation can move in accurate time yet fail to identify Amastacia, Ankora, timbre, duration, or the existence of audible noise. Conversely, a useful prose description can identify sound sources and changing texture without moving in synchrony at all. Earlier hourly notes covered automatic speech recognition inventing speech, and website node operability. Neither establishes what CONTENT a non-speech music alternative should carry.


The new unresolved direction is semantic and informational: what can language accurately transmit about a largely wordless sound, what should remain explicitly unknown, and how much control should the visitor have over the representation? This is a genuine accessibility question, not a pretext to redesign the music or prescribe a score.


2. FOUR DIFFERENT OBJECTS THAT ARE TOO EASILY CALLED 'THE MUSIC'


The first object is the physical/acoustic performance: changing air pressure at the recording microphone, transient attacks, decay envelopes, harmonic and noisy components, room contribution, and any captured mechanical or incidental sounds. Even that microphone signal is not the whole live performance. Its frequency response, placement, dynamic processing, and recording format determine what survives.


The second object is the representation: a sentence, timestamped caption, sound-mimetic syllable, visual symbol, or animated caption. A representation can be accurate about a source yet impoverished in temporal detail. It can be temporally detailed yet inaccurate about source identity. It can also be an original companion artwork rather than an informational substitute. These purposes should be labeled, not blurred.


The third object is the visitor's access. A Deaf person, a hard-of-hearing person using hearing aids or cochlear implants, a DeafBlind visitor using a refreshable braille display, and a hearing visitor listening through a tiny phone speaker do not receive identical information. Nor do they necessarily want the same caption style. Some visitors may prioritize instrument identity, others temporal events, others atmosphere or optional interpretive language. Accessibility is not equivalent to giving everyone the same simulation.


The fourth object is the visitor's experience: interest, pleasure, irritation, absorption, physical sensation, a desire to move, fatigue, or nothing memorable. These are reports or behaviors requiring observation. A caption saying 'low drone continues' does not cause or demonstrate absorption. A user selecting 'immersive' in a questionnaire does not prove mechanical vibration or neural entrainment. The four objects interact, but they are not substitutes for one another.


3. A NEW 2026 RESULT: TEXT NEED NOT BE ONLY A GENRE LABEL


Ahn, Baek, Kim, Truong, and Hong's 2026 CHI paper introduced μCap, an instrumental-music captioning system. Rather than restricting music to broad labels, the system uses audio-feature analysis and a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline to produce time-aligned, non-lexical, sound-mimetic captions with simple visual support. The institutional abstract reports two evaluations with DHH participants, n=20 and n=15, and improvements in music appreciation, immersion, and perceived access to acoustic detail [1].


That is meaningful evidence of an alternative design direction: words and quasi-phonetic visual marks can sometimes provide more useful information than a generic '[instrumental music]' label. But the precise scope matters. The reported outcomes are user-study judgments about the researchers' system and stimuli. They are not proof that text recreates acoustic pressure, that all DHH users prefer sound-mimetic captions, or that such captions reproduce the emotional or bodily effects of hearing a didgeridoo. The public institutional repository supplied an abstract but not the full conference paper. Consequently, exact effect sizes, confidence intervals, randomization details, stimulus selection, caption-reading burden, and whether the two samples overlap could not be independently checked in this pass. The two sample sizes should not be added into a claim of 35 unique independent people without verifying the full methods.


For KLAAT, μCap suggests a possibility rather than a ready-made solution. A didgeridoo can sustain one nominal fundamental while its audible texture changes through breath, vocal-tract filtering, harmonics, and turbulence. A caption that repeats a syllable every time an amplitude detector fires could mistakenly turn those fluctuations into separate 'notes.' Ankora's strikes may overlap and decay into the drone. A stylized 'BOOM' might suggest a large bass impact even when the actual phone recording emphasizes the midrange attack. Captions need their own source-validity and temporal-validity checks, just as visual nodes do.


4. THE CONTRADICTION: STANDARD CAPTIONS MAY CARRY INFORMATION WITHOUT CARRYING THE FEELING


Revuelta and colleagues' 2020 experiment recruited 16 people reporting normal hearing and 13 reporting hearing loss, using short audiovisual stimuli and behavioral/EEG measurements [2]. In the hearing group, the mean number of button-marked emotional reactions was 19.87 in the audio condition versus 9.81 in the caption condition (reported p=0.00528). In the hearing-loss group, the means were 7.00 in the muted condition and 7.15 in the standard-caption condition, a nonsignificant difference (p=0.52218).


Those values are counts of task-specific button responses, not a universal emotional intensity scale. EEG topography is also not a direct measurement of pleasure, musical absorption, or a subjective 'trance state.' The stimuli, language, group characteristics, hearing access, and standard-caption format constrain interpretation. In particular, the study did not directly compare μCap-style sound-mimetic music captions against a carefully written, time-aware prose alternative for KLAAT-like drone and frame drum.


At first glance, this 2020 result and the 2026 μCap abstract appear contradictory: one finds standard captions limited in evoking emotional responses, the other reports improved appreciation and immersion from a richer captioning approach. They need not be mutually exclusive. They used different caption systems, stimuli, groups, tasks, and outcomes. A behavioral emotional-response button count is not the same dependent variable as a subjective immersion rating. The most defensible synthesis is not 'captions work' or 'captions cannot work.' It is that caption DESIGN and outcome definition matter, and that access to acoustic detail should not be confused with reproduction of an auditory experience.


5. THE LARGER USER STUDY: THERE IS NO SINGLE IDEAL CAPTION


May and colleagues' 2025 investigation screened 1,276 research papers and included 36 in its literature analysis. Their empirical component included 168 DHH survey respondents, 15 DHH interview participants, and five professional captioners [3]. Participants differed in whether they wanted factual sound descriptions, emotional interpretation, frequent captions, selective captions, or additional visual detail. Preferences changed with screen size, viewing context, degree of hearing access, genre, and other factors.


One especially revealing analysis assembled a hypothetical captioning policy by choosing the most common answer for each preference question. That policy did not satisfy the full preference set of any respondent, and more than half of respondents would have fewer than half of their preferences met. This is a within-sample design result, not an estimate that 'most Deaf people dislike captions.' It demonstrates why majority-vote design can produce a representation that is convenient for a producer yet ill-suited to any one actual visitor.


The study also found that music captions are not automatically best when emotionally neutral. A plurality of respondents preferred affective details for music, while other respondents wanted factual descriptions rather than an author's imposed mood. Both preferences deserve respect. KLAAT should not force one interpretive voice onto everyone. A neutral sound description and an optional artist's interpretation can coexist, provided they are clearly differentiated.


Alonzo, Shin, and Li's 2022 ASSETS work provides a related caution. Interviews with 11 DHH viewers informed a non-speech captioning interface, and 10 hearing creators evaluated an authoring prototype [4]. This supports the importance of captioning meaningful non-speech events and the need for usable creation tools. It does not establish the accessibility or efficacy of KLAAT's particular website, nor should a study of hearing creators be recast as a DHH listener-outcome trial.


6. WHAT THE WEB STANDARD ACTUALLY SAYS


WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.2.1, Level A, requires an alternative for time-based media conveying equivalent information for prerecorded audio-only material [5]. The informative W3C explanation describes making the information of prerecorded audio available through another sensory modality [6]. When audio is part of synchronized prerecorded video, Success Criterion 1.2.2 addresses captions, and other media requirements may also apply. A real classification depends on what KLAAT actually embeds, whether the material is audio-only or synchronized media, and what alternatives already exist. This session is not a compliance certification or legal determination.


Two boundaries follow. First, a transcript of a nonverbal recording need not pretend that someone spoke. It can describe sound events and temporal development. Second, the accessibility obligation is not a demand to promise the same sensation or emotion to everyone. 'Equivalent information' is an information-access goal, not a claim that written language recreates the entire perceptual experience of an acoustic ritual.


A KLAAT website with one paragraph reading 'Sonic Ritualism' may successfully express its artistic category but may not communicate what happens during a specific recording. An alternative that identifies the two instruments, sustained drone, changing breath texture, intermittent strikes, and meaningful shifts would convey more relevant information. Whether that alternative meets all applicable criteria requires testing the actual page and media element.


7. A THREE-LAYER MODEL FOR KLAAT — NOT THREE MANDATORY CAPTION TRACKS


Consider a flexible editorial model with three distinguishable layers. This is a design hypothesis derived from the external literature, not a validated KLAAT intervention.


LAYER A: SOURCE AND EVENT FACTS. Identify Amastacia and Ankora and describe audible changes in ordinary language: a sustained low drone, rougher or brighter breath texture, scattered frame-drum impacts, denser attacks, decay, a quieter interval. Only include details verified in the actual recording. If a particular event cannot confidently be assigned to Ankora rather than breath articulation, use a less specific description. A source label is a statement about what is heard; it is not a frequency or physiological claim.


LAYER B: OPTIONAL TEMPORAL DETAIL. A visitor who wants to follow the recording's evolution could encounter concise time-indexed descriptions or carefully checked sound-mimetic marks. The timestamps must refer to the actual audio file and should be checked after any trimming, encoding, or playback changes. Avoid inventing a fixed meter, beat subdivisions, exact C# pitch content, or the number of attacks from the title alone. A short caption such as '[several close drum strikes; drone continues]' may be more accurate than a decorative line of regularly spaced syllables. If the timing is approximate, say so.


LAYER C: CLEARLY MARKED ARTIST INTERPRETATION. A separate optional note might say that KLAAT is intended as an energizing, physically engaged resonance ritual with an archaeosonic visual language. That is an artist's description of intention and identity. It must not be presented as the guaranteed experience of a Deaf visitor, a hearing visitor, or anyone's nervous system. Words such as 'hypnotic,' 'grounding,' 'ancient,' or 'otherworldly' can be poetic descriptors if framed as artistic interpretation, not measured universal effects.


These layers need not appear as three simultaneous blocks on the screen. A short default alternative with an optional 'more detail' control could be more consistent with KLAAT's restrained visual design. A plain text version should remain readable independently of motion, color, or a proprietary caption animation. A visitor should not have to watch a flashing node to discover what sound it allegedly represents.


8. WHY THE SAME WORDS CAN HELP ONE STUDY AND BIAS ANOTHER


There is an important tension between accessibility and experimental interpretation. If a visitor reads 'the drum accelerates into a trance-inducing pulse' before hearing a clip, the description may change what they attend to and how they report their experience. That would be an expectation or demand-characteristic issue in a KLAAT listener experiment, not a reason to withhold necessary accessibility information from ordinary visitors.


For an artistic website, useful optional context is desirable. For a formal listener pilot seeking unprompted reports, the information each participant received must be documented. A hearing listener who read an evocative caption first is not in the same interpretive condition as someone who heard an unlabeled recording. A DHH participant receiving a text alternative should not be treated as a deficient version of an audio-only listener; they are receiving a different presentation, and the research question should be designed accordingly. One could study information access and user satisfaction without pretending that text and sound are interchangeable stimuli.


The crucial separation is between two research endpoints: (1) can a visitor correctly understand the source, temporal changes, and relevant audible events through an accessible alternative? and (2) does that visitor report engagement, pleasure, presence, or absorption? The first can be tested with comprehension questions and source-event checks. The second requires subjective reports and appropriate comparisons. Neither establishes autonomic regulation, neural entrainment, vibrotactile dose, or therapeutic effects.


9. THE AESTHETIC PROBLEM IS REAL, NOT AN EXCUSE TO OMIT ACCESS


KLAAT's sparse art works partly because it does not overexplain itself. Its geometries, mineral colors, and monumental darkness invite interpretation. A crowded screen of pulsing onomatopoeia might conflict with that atmosphere. But accessibility does not require putting a subtitle billboard over every stone circle. It requires making meaningful information available through a usable channel.


There is a productive distinction between the performance and its supporting interface. Paul's improvisation remains free: Amastacia can hold or distort its drone; Ankora can strike, hesitate, accelerate, or fall silent. A later description can be written after the fact, based on what actually occurred. The artwork is not obligated to follow a captioner's script. Nor does the caption have to become the artwork's only interpretation.


KLAAT's sound is already layered in a way that makes this question unusually rich. A drone may be continuous acoustically but change its perceived character. A drum attack may be physically discrete but perceptually blend with the field. A rhythmic impression can emerge without a stable meter. Words like 'continuous,' 'intermittent,' 'rougher,' 'closer together,' and 'fades' can describe relative acoustic relationships without claiming to reproduce the sound. Words like 'healing,' 'trance-inducing,' or 'forces bodily vibration' would jump from description into unsupported effects.


10. THE EVIDENCE LADDER — WHAT IS KNOWN AND WHAT IS NOT


ESTABLISHED OR NORMATIVE: Non-speech information is relevant to caption access. W3C's prerecorded audio-only criterion requires an equivalent-information alternative when applicable. The 2025 mixed-methods study documents heterogeneous DHH caption preferences. These are research and standards findings, not KLAAT measurements.


MEASURED, CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: The 2026 μCap abstract reports favorable subjective outcomes in two small DHH evaluations (n=20, n=15) for its particular instrumental caption system. The 2020 behavioral/EEG study reports fewer emotional-response button presses for conventional captions than audio in one hearing group and no significant muted-versus-caption increase in the hearing-loss group. Neither result settles what works for a KLAAT drone/pulse recording.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE: A carefully written KLAAT text alternative could improve recognition of the instruments and the broad evolution of a take compared with a generic music label. Optional time-aware details might improve understanding for some users while increasing reading effort for others. A separate artist-interpretation option might improve engagement for some and feel intrusive to others. These are hypotheses.


SPECULATIVE: A caption style might be experienced as especially fitting for KLAAT's archaeosonic world, or might help some visitors construct a compelling internal sense of motion. The degree, direction, and generality of those effects are unknown.


UNSUPPORTED: That a caption recreates the exact bodily sensation of low-frequency sound; that DHH visitors necessarily prefer one visual rhythm; that caption-driven immersion is equivalent to auditory trance; that KLAAT's sound has produced neural entrainment, autonomic effects, or therapeutic change; that any sample count in the cited studies proves universal preference. None of these follows from the evidence.


11. THE UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS THAT DESERVE REAL DATA


The most interesting next questions are specific. Can a person who cannot hear the clip identify that the low drone persists under intermittent impacts after reading a short alternative? Does a time-indexed description help them recognize a change in density better than a paragraph? Do sound-mimetic captions clarify or distort a didgeridoo's breath-driven spectral changes? Can a caption describe noise/roughness without falsely implying malfunction? How much detail can appear on a small phone screen without making KLAAT's visual world exhausting? Would a visitor prefer neutral description, artist interpretation, both, or neither? How do answers change for people with partial hearing, braille access, different reading preferences, or familiarity with didgeridoo and frame drum?


These questions cannot be answered by imagining what a DHH person would want. The 2025 preference research specifically cautions against that kind of one-size-fits-none design. They also cannot be answered by an audio onset detector alone. An onset detector can help locate events but cannot decide which events are meaningful to a visitor, whether a syllable communicates the right timbre, or whether the result preserves KLAAT's identity.


12. WHAT THIS ADDS TO THE RESEARCH PROGRAM


KLAAT's scientific architecture already separates performer flow from listener absorption, acoustic pressure from physical vibration, physical onsets from perceived beats, and expressive visuals from synchronized event displays. Session 126 adds another necessary separation: INFORMATION ACCESS from EXPERIENCE EQUIVALENCE. This is not a claim that art should be stripped of poetry. It is a way to keep artistic interpretation available without allowing it to masquerade as measured acoustic fact or a promised human outcome.


The strongest durable finding is not a particular caption style. It is a design and measurement principle: describe the sound that is actually present; make interpretive language optional and identifiable; preserve visitor choice; and test whether the alternative communicates useful information to the people it is meant to serve. This protects the art's mystery, the audience's agency, and the credibility of future KLAAT research.


OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — KRX-126: ONE CLIP, TWO DESCRIPTIONS


Without changing the recording or KLAAT's performance, choose one existing unedited 30–60-second Amastacia–Ankora clip. Draft (A) a short, factual description of instruments and major audible changes and (B) a separate optional, clearly labeled artist-interpretation paragraph. Do not invent timestamps, tempo, or emotional effects; mark uncertain sounds as uncertain. If a DHH collaborator voluntarily reviews them, ask which information is useful, inaccurate, missing, or overly interpretive, and what level of detail they prefer. Preserve their words without turning one opinion into a universal rule. If no collaborator is available, keep the drafts as untested editorial prototypes, not a validated accessibility feature.
