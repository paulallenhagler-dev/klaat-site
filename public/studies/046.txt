SESSION 46 — MECHANICAL COUPLING: WHY “FEELING THE BASS” IS NOT ONE PHENOMENON
ORIGINAL SESSION TEXT PRESERVED FROM PROJECT CONVERSATION


We've spent several sessions building outward: auditory prediction, masking, roughness, auditory-object formation, and finally the room itself. Now we need to return directly to the body, because there's a distinction KLAAT needs before we can seriously study vibrotactile effects.


When someone says:


“I could feel the sound in my body,”


that description can refer to several physically different things.


A listener can receive vibration through airborne sound pressure, through direct mechanical contact with a vibrating structure, or through a mixture of both. Those pathways aren't interchangeable.


For KLAAT, this means the floor beneath the listener may matter almost as much as the loudspeaker—or Ankora itself.


Acoustic coupling vs. structural coupling


Imagine Ankora suspended in the air and struck.


It moves surrounding air.


Pressure variations propagate outward:


drum → air → listener


The listener's ears receive them, and sufficiently strong low-frequency acoustic pressure can also interact mechanically with the body.


That's primarily airborne acoustic coupling.


Now place Ankora on, or mechanically connect it to, a resonant wooden platform.


The strike also produces:


drum → support → floor → chair/feet/body


That's structure-borne vibration.


The two routes can occur simultaneously:


AIR PATH
Ankora → air → ears/body


STRUCTURAL PATH
Ankora → floor → feet/chair → body


A person saying “I felt that drum” doesn't tell us which pathway dominated.


Mechanical impedance


To understand why surfaces matter, we need a new concept:


mechanical impedance.


Very loosely, mechanical impedance describes how strongly a system resists motion when a force is applied at a particular frequency.


The exact formulation depends on the mechanical system, but conceptually:


force applied / resulting velocity


gives a form of mechanical impedance.


Different materials and structures respond differently.


A massive concrete slab behaves differently from:


a suspended wooden floor,


a thin stage,


a chair,


a hollow platform,


a human body.


So putting the same vibrating source on different structures can radically change how efficiently vibration transfers.


This explains something you've probably experienced


Stand near powerful bass on:


solid concrete.


Then stand near the same system on:


a flexible wooden stage.


The measured airborne SPL might be similar.


Yet the bodily experience can be dramatically different.


Why?


Because the stage itself can move.


Your feet now receive mechanical energy directly.


The system has become:


speaker → stage → legs


in addition to:


speaker → air → ears.


This is why vibrotactile experience cannot be predicted from a conventional sound-level meter alone.


SPL measures acoustic pressure.


It does not fully characterize structure-borne vibration.


Resonance enters again


Every mechanical structure has modes at which it responds particularly strongly.


A wooden platform might have a mode where relatively modest excitation produces substantial motion.


If Ankora or a loudspeaker supplies energy near that mode:


small input → comparatively large structural response.


Move away from the resonant region:


same input → smaller motion.


This is genuine mechanical resonance.


And notice how different this is from the vague statement:


“The body's frequency resonated with the drum.”


Here we can identify:


source


frequency


structure


mode


transfer path


measurable motion.


That's the standard KLAAT should maintain.


The listener is mechanically coupled too


Suppose someone sits on a chair placed on a vibrating platform.


We now have several coupled mechanical systems:


platform


↓


chair


↓


body


Each interface transfers some frequencies efficiently and others poorly.


Change the chair and the experience changes.


A heavily padded chair may attenuate some vibration.


A rigid wooden chair may transfer more.


Standing creates another coupling configuration.


Lying directly on the platform creates another.


Therefore:


posture is an acoustic variable.


More precisely, it is a vibromechanical coupling variable.


That becomes important if KLAAT ever develops a standardized audience arrangement.


Human vibrotactile perception is frequency-dependent


The skin doesn't contain one universal “vibration receptor.”


Several mechanoreceptor systems contribute to touch, and their sensitivity differs with frequency, location and stimulus conditions.


For vibration, the Pacinian system is especially important at relatively higher vibrotactile frequencies and is remarkably sensitive under appropriate conditions.


Other mechanoreceptive channels contribute more strongly at lower frequencies and to different forms of skin deformation.


The exact boundaries are not hard walls, but the larger principle is solid:


30 Hz vibration and 200 Hz vibration are not equivalent tactile stimuli.


Even if a sensor tells us they have equal displacement or acceleration, they may not feel equally strong.


Body location matters


Your fingertips are exceptionally sensitive tactile organs.


Your back isn't your fingertip.


Your feet aren't your sternum.


Different body regions have different:


receptor densities,


tissue structures,


contact areas,


mechanical loading,


and perceptual thresholds.


Therefore a statement such as:


“Humans can feel 40 Hz at X amplitude”


is incomplete unless we specify:


where?


how large is the contact surface?


what type of mechanical stimulus?


what posture?


what waveform?


This is exactly why vibrotactile research can become messy very quickly.


This has a major implication for KLAAT


Suppose you perform for 30 seated listeners.


Person A's chair sits directly on a flexible wooden section of flooring.


Person B's chair sits over a structural beam.


Person C sits on a thick cushion.


Person D has both feet firmly planted.


Person E has crossed their legs and their feet barely touch the floor.


They are not receiving the same vibrotactile performance.


Even though they're hearing essentially the same composition.


That's a big deal.


If bodily vibration becomes a serious KLAAT component, eventually we'll need to think about delivery architecture, not just instruments.


The floor could become a third instrument


We've described:


Amastacia, the Drone


and


Ankora, the Pulse.


Imagine eventually adding a deliberately designed resonant platform.


Not another musical instrument.


A mechanical distribution system.


Ankora or low-frequency reproduction could excite it.


The audience sits or stands on it.


Now:


Ankora creates temporal events.


Amastacia creates sustained acoustic structure.


The platform converts selected low-frequency energy into distributed tactile motion.


That gives KLAAT control over a channel ordinary concerts often leave accidental.


But here's the danger


It would be extremely easy to turn this into gimmicky “bass massage.”


That's not what we want.


The interesting question isn't:


“How much vibration can we make?”


It's:


How does the tactile component relate temporally and perceptually to the auditory component?


Remember Session 36.


If:


HEAR THUMP + FEEL THUMP


occur together, they can be bound into one multisensory event.


So a platform doesn't merely increase intensity.


It gives us control over:


auditory–tactile correspondence.


Later we could manipulate:


same timing


slightly delayed vibration


different tactile rhythm


tactile Pulse disappearing while acoustic Pulse continues


Those are genuine experimental dimensions.


And Amastacia creates another possibility


Ankora naturally produces transient vibration:


impact → decay.


Amastacia produces sustained energy.


If a future tactile system reproduces part of Amastacia's low-frequency structure, the listener could receive:


continuous tactile field


plus


discrete tactile impacts.


That mirrors the acoustic architecture:


Drone + Pulse


but now in touch.


Then KLAAT becomes:


auditory Drone


auditory Pulse


tactile Drone


tactile Pulse.


Four controllable streams.


That's where this project starts becoming genuinely unusual.


We must separate vibration metrics


Another trap lies ahead.


Vibration can be described using:


displacement — how far something moves,


velocity — how quickly it moves,


acceleration — how quickly velocity changes.


Those quantities relate mathematically but aren't interchangeable.


For sinusoidal motion, frequency strongly affects their relationship.


A tiny displacement at high frequency can involve substantial acceleration.


A larger slow movement may have much lower acceleration.


So eventually saying:


“The platform vibrated at level 5”


will be meaningless.


We'll want actual measurements.


A phone accelerometer can give us crude exploratory data.


A calibrated accelerometer would eventually be much better.


KLAAT's live/recorded distinction now gets clearer


We've previously said a recording may preserve auditory information while losing tactile information.


Now we can state the mechanism more precisely.


Live KLAAT may involve:


airborne acoustic pressure


+


floor vibration


+


seat vibration


+


body contact


+


auditory perception.


Headphones reproduce primarily:


auditory pressure at the ears.


That's not a lower-quality copy of precisely the same stimulus.


It's a different sensory configuration.


This is why a future KLAAT release and a KLAAT physical installation should perhaps be treated as related but distinct works.


Evidence boundary


Established: acoustic and structure-borne vibration are physically distinct transmission pathways.


Established: mechanical impedance influences vibration transfer between coupled systems.


Established: structures possess resonant modes that can greatly alter vibration amplitude across frequency.


Established: human vibrotactile sensitivity depends strongly on frequency, body location, contact area and stimulus conditions.


Established: posture and mechanical coupling affect vibration reaching the body.


Highly plausible: flooring and seating materially influence the bodily experience of live KLAAT.


Highly plausible: part of the difference between headphone KLAAT and live KLAAT will arise from missing structure-borne vibration.


Testable: the same Ankora performance should produce different tactile ratings on concrete versus a flexible wooden floor even when airborne listening level is approximately controlled.


Unknown: how much structure-borne vibration currently reaches listeners during your actual KLAAT performances.


We haven't measured it.


Not established: stronger bodily vibration automatically produces greater autonomic, therapeutic or psychological benefit.


More vibration is simply more mechanical stimulation until evidence demonstrates something beyond that.


KLAAT Experiment #46 — Find the Hidden Instrument Under Your Feet


Take Ankora to two surfaces:


A — rigid concrete or another very solid floor


B — a suspended wooden floor, deck or similarly flexible structure.


Place your phone flat on the floor approximately 1 meter from Ankora.


Use any accelerometer/sensor app capable of recording the phone's acceleration over time.


Play the same simple Ankora pulse for about 60 seconds on each surface.


Then do something even more useful.


Take your shoes off and stand at the phone's position while someone else plays—or reverse roles if necessary.


Rate:


audible impact: 1–10


and separately:


vibration through feet/legs: 1–10.


Don't combine them into one “intensity” score.


If the wooden surface produces substantially greater accelerometer motion and tactile sensation without an equally large change in perceived airborne loudness, you've demonstrated the distinction this session is about.


Then you'll know that beneath Ankora and Amastacia there is already a potential third component of KLAAT that we haven't deliberately composed yet:


the mechanical pathway connecting the performance space to the listener's body.


And once we can measure that pathway, we can stop saying merely:


“You can feel KLAAT.”


We can begin asking the much more useful question:


Exactly what is moving, at what frequencies, through which path, and how does the nervous system perceive it?


That question takes us directly into the next phase of the study: vibrotactile thresholds, receptor channels and the boundary between hearing a low-frequency event and physically feeling one.
