THE SOUND THE HAND MAY TURN UP

When self-generated sound is suppressed, enhanced, or neither: a 2026 reversal in auditory prediction research

SESSION 122 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

GOVERNING PRINCIPLE Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH STATUS A critical synthesis of primary experimental literature, including an August 2026 result that materially revises a familiar account of auditory sensory attenuation. No KLAAT recording, musician physiology, listener response, neural effect, therapeutic benefit, or trance induction is measured or demonstrated here. Amastacia remains the C#-centered agave didgeridoo; Ankora remains the elk-hide frame drum. Their spontaneous relationship is the object of study, not a score to be imposed.

CENTRAL QUESTION

When Paul makes an Ankora strike or sustains Amastacia's drone, is the resulting sound necessarily experienced as less intense because the brain anticipated his own action? Or can generating a faint sound make it seem stronger than the identical sound heard passively? And, if either effect occurs, what can it tell us about the difference between Paul's deeply embodied playing experience and a listener's experience of the same improvised performance?

The surprising answer is that experimental results run in BOTH directions. The direction can depend on signal intensity, the predictability of its timing and level, the listener's task, and which endpoint is measured. The most important new finding is not a new “brainwave effect.” It is a correction to an overconfident rule: SELF-GENERATION IS NOT A UNIVERSAL VOLUME KNOB THAT ALWAYS TURNS SOUND DOWN.

This matters because KLAAT is created through a close, continuous action–sound relationship. The pulse is made by a hand; the drone is made by breath, lips and a resonating bore; each source also feeds tactile and proprioceptive information back to the performer. Paul may experience the event as an inseparable action and sound. A passive listener receives a different bundle of cues. That difference is meaningful without requiring that either person's report be the objectively correct one.

1. THE NOVELTY GATE: WHAT THIS SESSION ADDS

Session 79 established the distinction between performer flow and listener absorption. Sessions 82 and 101 examined the circular-breathing handoff; Session 116 considered breathing and spontaneous manual action; Sessions 119 and 121 treated visual anticipation and headphone spatial delivery. An October 17 hourly note identified action-linked auditory N1 suppression as a candidate performer–receiver mechanism. None of those numbered studies established a rule for how the DIRECTION of self-generation effects changes with sound intensity, nor did they resolve the conflict between auditory-evoked potentials, loudness judgments, and actual detection sensitivity.

The decisive extension here is the 2026 Salgado–Sturm–Via–SanMiguel experiment, read alongside a preregistered 2021 psychophysical experiment and earlier work. This session asks whether the popular 'the brain cancels its own sounds' explanation survives when the sound is weak and when subjective loudness is separated from sensitivity. It does not repackage the earlier note's general performer-versus-replay comparison. It develops a specific contradiction and a stricter inference framework for authentic KLAAT.

2. FOUR THINGS THAT MUST NOT BE CONFUSED

FIRST, THE ACOUSTIC SIGNAL. A calibrated microphone measures air-pressure fluctuations at a position. Spectra, attack times, and level envelopes are properties of that received signal under stated conditions. The instrument's sound does not become physically quieter merely because its maker expected it.

SECOND, SUBJECTIVE LOUDNESS. A listener can judge one tone as louder than another even when the physical levels are matched. Such judgments may reflect perceptual scaling, decision criteria, expectation, or attention. A point of subjective equality (PSE) is the comparison level at which two stimuli are judged equally intense. It is a behavioral estimate, not a direct reading of a neuron.

THIRD, PERCEPTUAL SENSITIVITY. A detection threshold, discrimination just-noticeable difference (JND), or signal-detection d-prime asks how reliably a person can distinguish stimuli. A shift in which of two sounds is judged louder does not require improved ability to detect the sound or distinguish small differences. A bias and a sensitivity change are not interchangeable.

FOURTH, NEURAL RESPONSES. EEG N1 and P2 or MEG N100m describe time-locked electrical or magnetic responses under experimental conditions. A smaller N1 does not directly quantify how many decibels quieter a sound seems, whether it is pleasant, or whether someone is in trance. The sources of N1 and P2 are not pure one-to-one labels for 'sensory cortex' and 'conscious meaning'; attention, motor preparation, orienting, and overlapping components complicate interpretation.

A fifth category, physiological activation (respiration, heart rate, autonomic responses), would require independent measurements. None of the following experiments measured KLAAT activation, bodily energy or altered awareness.

3. THE CLASSIC RESULT: SELF-TRIGGERED SOUNDS CAN PRODUCE SMALLER AUDITORY RESPONSES

Martikainen, Kaneko and Hari (2005; online 2004) tested 12 healthy participants with magnetoencephalography. A participant pressed a button about every five seconds to trigger a tone; a comparison condition presented an identical sequence of externally triggered tones. The auditory N100m response peaked about 90 milliseconds after sound onset. Relative to externally triggered tones, responses were suppressed by 24 ± 7% in the left and 18 ± 4% in the right hemisphere. These are MEG response-amplitude percentages, not percentage changes in loudness, acoustic energy or enjoyment [1].

Baess and colleagues (2011) then mixed self-initiated and externally initiated tones within the same blocks. Auditory N1 suppression remained and was reported as larger than in a traditional blocked comparison [2]. This reduced one contextual explanation: the effect was not merely an artifact of spending one block 'acting' and another block 'listening.' It supported an action-linked prediction account, but it did not establish that every self-generated sound is heard as softer.

Heinks-Maldonado and colleagues (2005) tested auditory feedback during actual vowel production. Unaltered own-voice feedback elicited a smaller N100 than pitch-shifted own-voice or an alien voice substituted during speaking [3]. That is closer to a performer producing a continuous sound than a button press, but it is still speech, not a didgeridoo drone. A voice involves its own anatomy, hearing routes and motor control; the finding does not determine what Paul's ears do when Amastacia's bore responds to his lips.

A sensible first model is an action-based prediction or 'forward model': the motor system can help anticipate an action's sensory consequences. Yet this is a model family, not proof that a literal subtraction process always removes sound from consciousness. The experiments chiefly demonstrate differences in evoked responses under specific conditions.

4. THE 2015 REVERSAL: FAINT SELF-GENERATED TONES MAY SEEM LOUDER

Reznik and colleagues (2015) asked 28 participants to compare self-generated and externally generated tones at two intensity regimes [4]. At higher intensities, self-generated sounds were judged less loud. Near threshold, the direction reversed: self-generated sounds were judged louder. The sounds were matched physically in the relevant comparison, so the result could not be explained by the participant actually producing a louder tone.

The experiment established an intensity-by-agency interaction in reported loudness. Its design did not estimate a full PSE/JND psychometric function for every condition, so it could not by itself establish whether the change was a shift in sensitivity or in the response to an ambiguous sound. Nor can a tone just above threshold be equated to a soft Amastacia overtone inside a loud, harmonically rich drone. Threshold is defined relative to the listener, frequency, masking environment, and test method.

Nevertheless, the study overturned the idea that prediction has only one perceptual consequence. A motor act may help a listener anticipate and register weak feedback while the same sort of act makes a strong, predictable event less surprising. The sign of the effect is a research question, not a foregone conclusion.

5. THE 2021 TEST: LOUDNESS BIAS CHANGED; SENSITIVITY DID NOT

Paraskevoudi and SanMiguel (2021) preregistered a study that separated detection and loudness discrimination [5]. Thirty-one normal-hearing adults were recruited; 28 were analyzed after three exclusions. They used 300-millisecond, 1,000-Hz pure tones. In the discrimination task, the clearly audible standard was 74 dB SPL, with comparisons from 71 to 77 dB SPL; the near-threshold standard was set five decibels above each listener's measured threshold, with comparisons within ±3 dB. The study's design was not a musical listening test, and its dB values are NOT KLAAT playback targets.

The researchers observed an interaction between self-generation and intensity in the PSE: for clearly audible sounds, self-generated standards were judged softer; for near-threshold sounds, self-generated standards were judged louder. Crucially, they did not find the corresponding self-generation effect on detection thresholds or discrimination JND. The data support a change in the estimate of loudness without evidence of a general improvement in sensory resolution.

That distinction is decisive for KLAAT. If Paul later describes a delicate breath articulation as more salient during playing than on replay, that need not mean his ears detected more acoustic detail in a signal-processing sense. Likewise, if a drum strike feels less prominent while he plays than while he hears a recording, that would not prove reduced hearing sensitivity or a weaker strike. Reports about salience, loudness and detail must be kept separate.

6. AUGUST 2026: A NEW NEURAL TEST COMPLICATES THE RULE FURTHER

Salgado, Sturm, Via and SanMiguel (2026) studied 47 healthy adults in an auditory intensity-discrimination task while recording EEG [6]. The design crossed self-generated versus externally generated sounds with near-threshold versus clearly audible intensities. Participants made loudness comparisons; researchers estimated PSE and examined N1 and P2 responses.

The behavioral result replicated an important part of the earlier story: near-threshold self-generated sounds were judged louder than corresponding external sounds. The frontocentral N1 was also larger (more negative) for those weak self-generated sounds. But at suprathreshold intensities the anticipated perceptual attenuation was NOT statistically significant, and N1 suppression was also not significant. P2, rather than N1, showed reliable suppression for suprathreshold self-generated sounds. The authors also found that self-generation changed the usual N1 scaling with sound intensity.

This is not a clean replacement of 'suppression' by 'enhancement.' It is a more interesting outcome: the same broad motor-sensory situation can show enhancement in one intensity range and one neural measure, no significant effect in another, and attenuation in a later component. A nonsignificant result is not proof of zero effect; it says this experiment did not establish that effect under its design and precision.

The researchers discuss an important design difference. Near-threshold and suprathreshold trials were interleaved. Participants could predict that pressing would cause a sound but not reliably predict its intensity. Sustained effortful listening for weak trials could carry over into louder trials. This might help explain why a previous study found clear suprathreshold attenuation while the 2026 study did not. It is a plausible explanation, not an experimentally isolated cause.

The 2026 paper also cautions that frontocentral N1–P2 effects may include orienting or arousal-related activity, not only auditory-cortex gain. The study itself calls for designs that separately manipulate intensity predictability, attention and autonomic state. Thus it is unwarranted to translate its EEG results into a story that Paul's brain 'amplifies quiet resonance' or that listeners undergo any particular altered state.

7. TIMING PREDICTION AND SOUND PRODUCTION ARE NOT THE SAME MANIPULATION

In a vocal-feedback experiment, Chen and colleagues (2012) had participants sustain a vowel while a mouse click triggered a pitch-shift in their auditory feedback [7]. A predictable condition delivered the shift without delay; an unpredictable condition delayed it by 500–1,000 milliseconds. Relative to externally triggered shifts, predictable self-triggered changes suppressed P2, whereas unpredictable self-triggered changes enhanced P2. Timing predictability mattered.

An October 2024 study by Tast, Schröger and Widmann adds a different warning [8]. Thirty participants pressed approximately every two seconds to trigger a 50-millisecond, 800-Hz harmonic sound at 76.2 dB SPL. Sounds followed 80% or 50% of presses. An omission-related N1 appeared in the 80% condition but not the 50% condition, while sound-evoked N1 suppression was similar between those probability conditions. Thus the neural response to an EXPECTED SOUND THAT FAILS TO OCCUR and the attenuation of a SOUND THAT DOES OCCUR need not change together.

For KLAAT, three separate prediction problems coexist: the player's prediction of the consequence of a hand/breath gesture; a listener's prediction of the next Ankora attack; and a listener's inference about what a quiet or missing event means. They cannot be merged into one 'entrainment' explanation. The action system and the listening system may use related principles while responding to different evidence.

8. WHY THIS IS RELEVANT TO AMASTACIA AND ANKORA—AND WHY IT DOES NOT TRANSFER DIRECTLY

Amastacia is not a push-button tone generator. The lips and mouth alter a coupled acoustic oscillator; the bore returns pressure fluctuations to the player; the vocal tract reshapes harmonics; circular breathing introduces overlapping motor processes. The source can sound continuous even as the motor act changes. There is no one-to-one onset for every neural trial as there is in a button-press experiment. The player hears airborne sound from the instrument and room, plus bodily and bone-/tissue-conducted feedback. A microphone in front of the bell receives a different acoustic mix.

Ankora offers more discrete events. A hand initiates a strike, receives mechanical contact, and hears an attack plus membrane/room decay. But the same hand may be adjusting force, strike location, timing, damping and frame support. The performer knows the intended movement; the audience does not receive that motor command. The listener may anticipate the strike from prior rhythm or visible hand motion, but that is not the same as producing it.

The relevant scientific possibility is not that Paul hears every strong Ankora hit as softer and every faint Amastacia detail as louder. That would be a leap beyond the experiments. The narrower hypothesis is that MOTOR AGENCY MAY ALTER THE WEIGHT ASSIGNED TO AUDITORY FEEDBACK, AND THE DIRECTION OF THAT CHANGE MAY DEPEND ON ITS RELATIVE AUDIBILITY AND PREDICTABILITY.

The word 'relative' matters. A quiet harmonic inside a loud drone is not necessarily a near-threshold isolated tone. Masking, attention and the recording chain affect whether it is available to hearing. A drum strike with a large acoustic peak may have a quiet tail; it would be an error to assume one perceptual-gain direction applies uniformly to the entire event. The same performance could contain multiple acoustic and sensory regimes without any obligation to structure them intentionally.

9. WHY THE PLAYER'S FLOW IS NOT A LISTENER EFFECT

Paul often reports a spontaneous, deeply absorbed or trance-like playing state. That first-person report is important artistic evidence of his own experience. It is not a measurement of an audience's neural state. A player controls breath, posture and gesture; receives mouth, hand and chest feedback; and anticipates actions from within. A listener may instead be absorbed in the drone, energized by the pulse, unsettled by roughness, or simply indifferent. These are separate, valid possibilities.

Auditory sensory attenuation and enhancement research cannot determine which of these states occurs. An EEG N1 difference does not equal flow; a PSE shift does not equal bodily energy; a familiar beat does not prove autonomic synchronization. In the absence of KLAAT-specific data, the proper status of these claims remains open.

This also has implications for KLAAT as an everyday soundtrack. Someone hearing an unedited clip while moving around a kitchen, walking, or working is not performing Amastacia and Ankora. Their own movements and goals may shape attention, but the cited self-generated-tone experiments do not predict that they will be more alert, safer, or better at a task. KLAAT's artistic value does not depend on making that causal claim.

10. AN EVIDENCE MAP THAT RESISTS THE TEMPTING STORY

ESTABLISHED IN EXTERNAL CONTROLLED EXPERIMENTS - Self-triggered tones can evoke smaller auditory N100m/N1 responses than matched external tones; a 12-person MEG study reported 24 ± 7% and 18 ± 4% hemisphere-specific suppression. - Some near-threshold self-generated tones are judged louder, not softer, than matched externally generated tones; the 2015, 2021 and 2026 experiments converge on this direction under their respective tasks. - In the preregistered 2021 study, PSE changed without a demonstrated detection-threshold or JND change. - In the 2026 study, near-threshold frontocentral N1 was enhanced; suprathreshold P2 was attenuated, but suprathreshold perceptual attenuation and N1 suppression were not statistically established.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE AND CONTRADICTIONS - Timing, intensity expectation, trial interleaving, task demands, feedback manipulation and stimulus audibility can alter the sign or measurement location of action effects. - The 2024 omission experiment shows that omission responses and sound-evoked suppression can dissociate. - Speech feedback, 1-kHz tones and brief button-triggered events do not recreate an extended two-instrument improvisation. - Neural evoked-response amplitude, subjective loudness bias and sensory sensitivity are related research outcomes, not equivalent measures.

PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES - Paul's immediate perception of weak breath/noise details or drum-tail events may differ from his later replay perception, even when the recorded signal is unchanged. - Agency-related expectation may be one contributor among tactile feedback, room position, recording response, familiarity and post-performance attention. - The relation may differ for a stable drone, a new onset, and a faint residual decay. No direction is predicted without testing.

SPECULATION, NOT A FINDING - Feeling internally connected to one's own sound may contribute to the artistic character of KLAAT's performer flow. This is an interpretation, not an established mechanism or explanation of trance.

UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS - KLAAT suppresses, enhances or entrains the auditory cortex in Paul or an audience. - Amastacia's low C# frequency uniquely causes sensory gain or an altered state. - A listener must feel a stronger pulse than its maker; or Paul's own quiet details are necessarily enhanced. - Any ERP result demonstrates trance, healing, improved concentration or autonomic regulation. - The experimental tone-level numbers prescribe KLAAT recording, mixing or listening volume.

11. WHAT A SERIOUS KLAAT TEST WOULD HAVE TO SEPARATE

An ideal causal test would match the acoustic signal reaching the same person's ears while varying whether they generated the sound. For a live didgeridoo/frame drum this is technically difficult: producing the sound also changes tactile feedback, bone conduction, posture, motor preparation, sound location and expectation. Playing versus replay is therefore an ecological contrast, NOT a clean agency manipulation.

Even if the same digital file is used, the player's in-room hearing while creating it cannot be reconstructed exactly from a phone recording. Device frequency response, compression, room location and speaker or headphone playback may dominate any perceived difference. A convincing study would need calibrated ear-level delivery or a carefully designed action-triggered playback control, alongside an independent source recording. Those are future laboratory possibilities, not requirements for making music.

For a low-pressure KLAAT field study, it is more useful to document separate reports than to pretend to isolate the neural mechanism. Ask about the salience of faint drone details, strength of distinct drum attacks, clarity of decays, physical engagement and overall absorption separately. Record context and uncertainty. Avoid making a single 'resonance score' stand in for all of them.

12. THE CREATIVE CONSEQUENCE: LET THE MUSIC BE MADE FROM INSIDE

The research does not tell Paul to play more quietly, strike more strongly, alter his tempo, add an omission, or train a particular prediction schedule. It tells us why the creator's hearing and the audience's hearing need not be the same experience even when both are authentic. The musical world can emerge from spontaneous action without a scientist deciding how it should unfold.

KLAAT's defining tension remains intact: one person generates a continuous, organic acoustic field through two physically different instruments, and another person encounters the resulting sound without the same internal motor history. That gap is not a defect to engineer away. It is a reason to measure carefully, listen openly, and let reports from actual listeners revise the theory rather than reverse-engineer their experience from Paul's.

OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — KRX-122: THE QUIET DETAIL / THE BIG STRIKE

After any ordinary, unplanned Amastacia–Ankora improvisation, jot down—without stopping or altering the performance—one quiet detail you remember noticing while playing (a breath texture, overtone or drum tail) and one conspicuous Ankora impact. Later, listen once to the unedited recording at a comfortable level and note whether each seems more prominent, less prominent, or simply different. Write down playback device, room, approximate listening level, delay since playing, and whether the detail is actually captured. Do not try to make the two experiences identical or call a difference neural attenuation. A single comparison is a diary entry, not an experiment. It can reveal what would be worth studying when proper recordings and listener responses become available.
