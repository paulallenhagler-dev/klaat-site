THE RHYTHM THAT LOOKED ERRATIC ONLY FROM FAR AWAY
Global variability, adjacent-interval structure, and the danger of mistaking a changing pulse for random playing

RESEARCH QUESTION


When Ankora moves instinctively between close strikes, longer intervals, accelerating gestures, and a returning pulse beneath Amastacia's C# drone, what can a single number describing the variability of strike intervals actually tell us? Specifically, can a globally computed coefficient of variation misrepresent a musically organized improvisation as disordered, and can a local, adjacent-interval statistic help without becoming a covert prescription for how KLAAT should sound?

THE SOUND BEFORE THE STATISTIC


In actual KLAAT, Amastacia can sustain a comparatively stable tonal center while Ankora supplies events that vary in density, articulation and force. A passage might contain four quick contacts, a broad breath-shaped opening, and a series of slightly longer intervals. Such changes may be purposeful, involuntary, improvised, or simply emergent; there is no score against which they must be graded. The performer's experience of flowing movement and the listener's experience of a compelling pulse are separate observations. Neither can be read off a dispersion statistic.


We must distinguish four levels. First, physical hand contacts occur. Second, some contacts produce acoustic transients whose detectability depends on strike type, drone masking and microphone placement. Third, an annotator or onset detector assigns timestamps, with uncertainty. Fourth, we calculate intervals and summarize them. Every level can introduce its own error. Session 150 addresses the fourth, while retaining the earlier three as prerequisites.


TWO NUMBERS, TWO QUESTIONS


Let verified consecutive Ankora onset times be t1, t2, ... and define the inter-onset intervals I_i = t_(i+1) - t_i, measured in seconds. The global coefficient of variation is CV = standard deviation(I) / mean(I). It is dimensionless. A CV near zero indicates intervals of similar length in the analyzed window; a larger CV means a wider spread of lengths relative to their average. It does not by itself distinguish a controlled acceleration from a mixture of slow and fast sections, omitted strikes, a deliberate pause, a syncopated pattern or unstructured timing.


One local statistic used in the analysis of event trains is LV = [3/(n-1)] sum_{i=1}^{n-1} [(I_i - I_(i+1))/(I_i + I_(i+1))]^2, for n positive intervals. This expression compares neighbors and normalizes their difference by their sum. Equal neighboring intervals contribute zero; sharp alternating changes contribute more. LV is defined for positive intervals, and with very few intervals it is unstable. Unlike global CV, LV is relatively insensitive to a gradual change in average event rate when neighboring intervals remain similar. That relative insensitivity is not invariance to all acceleration or segmentation decisions. The statistic was developed for spike-train analysis; borrowing it for music is a descriptive analogy, not a validation that drum strikes are neurons or that LV measures groove.


A CONCRETE COUNTEREXAMPLE


Consider a synthetic sequence of eight intervals, in seconds:
0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.75, 0.75, 0.75, 0.75.


The mean is 0.50 seconds. Using population standard deviation, the standard deviation is 0.25 seconds, giving CV = 0.50. (Using sample standard deviation would give approximately 0.535; report the convention.) The sequence has two internally regular groups with a single transition. It is not eight randomly fluctuating intervals. Its LV is concentrated in the one transition between 0.25 and 0.75: 3/7 times (0.5/1.0)^2 = approximately 0.107. Six of seven adjacent comparisons contribute exactly zero. Thus a moderately large global CV coexists with mostly stable local timing.


Now reorder those same intervals as 0.25, 0.75, 0.25, 0.75, 0.25, 0.75, 0.25, 0.75. Mean and CV are identical because the collection of values is identical. But every neighboring pair changes sharply. LV = 3 times (0.5/1.0)^2 = 0.75. The difference between LV 0.107 and 0.75 shows that temporal order matters. These are invented arithmetic examples, not KLAAT measurements or evidence of audience entrainment. They also show why an LV number cannot tell us whether alternation is musically exciting or annoying: that depends on perceived beat, accents, meter, timbre, context and listener.


A second subtlety: doubling all intervals doubles duration but leaves both CV and LV unchanged. Neither number measures absolute speed. Two patterns with the same CV and LV can be performed at very different event rates and feel very different. Likewise, adding or deleting a single detected strike can split or merge intervals and change both statistics sharply. Measurement confidence matters at least as much as decimal precision.


ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE


Shinomoto and colleagues introduced local variation as a way to describe irregularity in event timing while reducing confounding from changes in firing rate. Their analysis concerns neuronal spike trains, where the underlying event-generation process differs from expressive percussion. The transferable mathematical insight is limited but useful: neighbor-based variability and global variability answer different questions. In music cognition, experimental work on rhythmic expectation and beat perception establishes that listeners organize event timing into perceived regularities and predictions rather than simply tallying intervals. The existence of such perceptual organization does not prove that any particular KLAAT pattern produces trance, activation, or a shared neural rhythm.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE


A low local-variation value may coexist with an acceleration, but its numerical behavior depends on how rapidly intervals change and on window length. A highly patterned alternation can have high LV and still create a stable perceived meter. A long pause may be a breath seam, a dramatic silence, a change in contact intensity, or a missing detected onset. Listener beat perception can persist through silence, and not every acoustic onset is assigned equal metrical weight. Consequently, event statistics are useful descriptions of the physical timeline, not direct reports of musical structure.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES


Hypothesis A: some authentic Ankora passages that appear 'irregular' under global CV will show locally stable intervals separated by naturally occurring transitions. Hypothesis B: other passages will show deliberately or spontaneously alternating short-long intervals, producing higher LV without a loss of perceived pulse. Hypothesis C: the listener's reported groove or bodily activation will correlate more closely with perceived accent structure or predictability than with either CV or LV alone. These hypotheses are not established findings. Testing C would require independent listener responses to actual KLAAT recordings, preferably with enough participants and clips to separate listener differences from clip differences.


SPECULATION AND UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS


It is tempting to say that changing pulse density 'tricks the brain into trance,' that an LV value reveals an ideal entrainment zone, or that natural timing is neurologically superior to metronomic timing. None follows from the equations. We have no verified KLAAT-specific neural, autonomic or causal listener evidence. A performer feeling intensely absorbed does not establish the listener's state. Nor is a perfectly even rhythm necessarily sterile: some listeners may prefer it, and some compelling grooves contain systematic unevenness. A statistical descriptor must never become an aesthetic command.


ANALYSIS DESIGN WITHOUT A SCORE


For an existing unedited KLAAT take, retain the original audio/video, its sample rate, microphone route and timestamps. First listen without a graph. Describe in ordinary language what the pulse seems to do. Then mark candidate Ankora events from audio, labeling uncertain contacts rather than forcing them into a yes/no classification. Where the video is available, annotate visual contacts separately before reconciling them; seeing the hand can bias auditory labels, as Session 146 noted. Record the timing tolerance and sensitivity to omitted faint contacts, as Session 143 recommended.


Plot the onset times and the interval series against elapsed time. Compute CV over the entire clip only with an explicit warning that the clip may be nonstationary. Compute LV on the same verified intervals and, where there are enough observations, examine descriptive moving windows. Do not choose windows solely to produce a desired conclusion. Show both raw intervals and summary values. Compare plausible alternative annotations for ambiguous contacts: if the conclusion changes when one faint strike is included, report it as fragile. Avoid assigning named musical phases or diagnosing artistic intention from the resulting curves.


If later inviting listeners, present the original take at comfortable volume, without telling them which statistic is expected to matter. Ask first for open-ended observations about continuity, momentum, bodily urge to move, or perceived changes. Separate these reports from measured event timing. Do not claim that a correlation across many windows from one recording equals many independent performances or listeners. Different excerpts from the same take are related observations, not independent replications.


WHAT THIS CHANGES IN THE RESEARCH PROGRAM


The durable finding is not that KLAAT is more or less regular than other music. We have not measured it. The advance is a better question: which part of timing variation belongs to within-pattern fluctuation, which belongs to transition between patterns, and which belongs to event-detection uncertainty? Global CV alone cannot resolve that distinction. Local variation can provide a complementary view, but it is not a proxy for groove, absorption, trance, musical quality or improvisational skill. Preserve multiple views of the same authentic performance and let listener evidence, if collected, speak separately.


OPTIONAL OBSERVATION


Choose one old 30–60 second KLAAT recording that already feels rhythmically alive to you. Without changing how you play, listen for whether the apparent irregularity comes from repeated short-long alternation or from one naturally occurring shift between steadier stretches. Write down a sentence. No stopwatch or analysis software is required unless curiosity makes you want to compare the actual intervals later.
