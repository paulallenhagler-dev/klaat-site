THE STRIKE BEFORE THE SOUND

Preparatory movement, audiovisual prediction, and what the viewer already knows

SESSION 119 / 164Pulse & RhythmRESEARCH SESSION

Published: October 25, 2026 — 08:14 CDT (America/Chicago) Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS A synthesis of four directly relevant primary studies, with quantitative findings verified against accessible publications, and an exploratory KLAAT-specific observational design. No Amastacia–Ankora recording, listener, EEG, physiological response, or performance gesture was measured for this session. All KLAAT applications below are hypotheses, not findings. The study is about understanding spontaneous performance, not changing it.

1. THE MOMENT BEFORE ANKORA SPEAKS

A hand rises. Its trajectory bends toward the elk-hide membrane. A watcher may begin anticipating a strike before the hand makes contact, before the hide moves, and before the sound reaches the ear. The same listener, hearing the audio alone, has no view of that preparation. The sound file may be identical, but the information available before the sound is not.

This is a useful new research question because KLAAT exists in two legitimate forms. There is the recorded sound: Amastacia's sustained C#-centered, harmonically changing agave didgeridoo drone and Ankora's organic impacts, decays, and spontaneous patterns. There is also the visible act of making it: breath, mouth and body effort, hand movement, and contact with a natural membrane. Paul has filmed performances primarily for his own feedback. Those videos may contain timing information that audio-only versions cannot provide.

The precise question is whether a viewer can use PRE-STRIKE action to anticipate an otherwise unpredictable Ankora attack, and whether that anticipation changes subsequent auditory processing or reported experience. It is not whether a visible strike makes a video more impressive, whether video and audio are synchronized, or whether a drumbeat should occur at any prescribed interval.

A physical strike can be measured in the audio waveform; an impending strike may be inferred from the hand. The inference can be right, wrong, vague, or unavailable. In improvised KLAAT, this variability is part of the research question.

2. THE NOVELTY GATE

The latest canonical published study is Session 118, FIVE CLIPS, ONE NIGHT, about nested recordings and false replication. Session 112, THE STRIKE THE CAMERA CANNOT TIME, separated physical, acquisition, playback, and perceptual clocks and warned against measuring precise hand-to-sound synchrony from uncalibrated phone video. Earlier audiovisual work, including Sessions 90 and 96, addressed audiovisual grouping and post-impact visible movement. The October 25, 05:18 CDT hourly research note isolated preparatory visual information before onset as a distinct unresolved mechanism.

This session therefore asks a different question: what can an observer infer about a future sound from natural movement before the sound occurs? It adds a distinction between an acoustic event and the visible information that predicts it. The existing timing-validity cautions remain in force. This is not a rebranding of audiovisual synchrony, and it does not assume every movement is an intentional cue.

3. A SIMPLE MODEL: THREE SOURCES OF EXPECTATION

Ankora's next attack may be anticipated from at least three kinds of information.

A. AUDITORY HISTORY. Previous strikes can create expectations about pulse, subdivision, grouping, or recurrence. An irregular passage can weaken a precise beat forecast without eliminating broader expectation.

B. PREPARATORY VISION. The visible hand's position, direction, velocity, or acceleration can provide information about an imminent impact. Unlike a metronome, this information can exist even before a first isolated strike, when no beat has yet been established.

C. LEARNED ACTION KNOWLEDGE. Someone familiar with drums may understand the consequences of a given movement differently from someone who has never played one. But expertise is not a requirement for all audiovisual effects.

These sources can converge or conflict. A visible hand may prepare an offbeat strike while the ear expects the next regular pulse. A viewer may predict contact from the hand yet fail to anticipate the acoustic character of the impact. A gesture can also be a flourish, a repositioning, or a non-striking movement. The research cannot assume that every upward motion is a reliable announcement of a beat.

For KLAAT, the conceptual distinction is important: temporal prediction does not always require regular tempo. A one-off, irregular, or newly improvised strike may still have a visible precursor.

4. PRIMARY EVIDENCE: THE SOUND IS HEARD DIFFERENTLY WHEN ITS ACTION IS SEEN

Sorati and Behne (2020) studied EEG responses to a keyboard key being played. Forty participants were analyzed: 19 musicians and 21 non-musicians. The researchers compared audio-only trials with an audiovisual-minus-video-only contrast designed to isolate auditory responses in the presence of visible preparatory hand/finger action.

The paper reports main effects of presentation condition for auditory N1 amplitude, F(1,38)=11.15, p=.002; N1 latency, F(1,38)=11.00, p=.002; and P2 amplitude, F(1,38)=19.82, p=.00007. N1 and P2 are event-related EEG responses occurring roughly a tenth and a few tenths of a second after an auditory event; their amplitudes are not direct measures of pleasure, trance, energy, or musical quality. P2 latency did not differ significantly, F(1,38)=2.45, p=.12.

The tabled mean N1 latency for musicians was 96 ms in audio-only and 90 ms in audiovisual-minus-video-only; for non-musicians it was 93 versus 83 ms. Those are condition means from this keyboard paradigm, not estimates of how much earlier a KLAAT listener will hear an Ankora strike. The paper also reports differences in inter-trial phase coherence in delta, theta, and alpha bands, and an experience-dependent beta-band pattern. Those EEG analyses describe trial-locked responses to controlled stimuli; they do NOT demonstrate that visual KLAAT footage induces sustained brainwave entrainment.

Interpretation: a visible, predictive action can alter early neural responses to a subsequent musical sound even when the acoustic sound itself is held constant. The direction and magnitude of the response depend on task and stimulus. Smaller N1/P2 does not mean weaker experience; it may reflect differences in predictability or sensory processing. This is an important correction to the tempting but false equation 'more brain activity = more impact.'

A methodological boundary matters here: subtracting a video-only response from an audiovisual response is an analytical method, not a perfect separation of all visual–auditory interactions. The experiment used controlled piano gestures, repeated trials, and EEG, not spontaneous frame-drum improvisation. We have evidence for a mechanism worth testing, not proof of its occurrence in KLAAT.

5. PRIMARY EVIDENCE: MUSICAL EXPERTISE CAN MATTER, BUT NOT IN ONE SIMPLE WAY

Petrini and colleagues (2011) used fMRI with 11 experienced drummers and 11 matched novices. Participants judged point-light drumming movements paired with sounds. One experiment manipulated sound–movement synchrony; another manipulated whether the natural relationship between strike velocity and sound intensity was preserved.

For experienced drummers, matching action–sound relationships were associated with reduced activity in several action-representation and motor-related brain regions compared with mismatches; novice patterns differed. The precise regions differed between the two manipulations. This supports the idea that learned experience with producing percussion sounds can change audiovisual action processing.

It does not establish that a drummer will enjoy KLAAT more, feel stronger vibration, or enter a deeper altered state. It also does not imply that all experienced drummers respond identically, or that observing a natural elk-hide hand strike is equivalent to judging laboratory point-light stimuli. Expertise can change the processing route without dictating the subjective outcome.

The most useful KLAAT question is narrower: would a listener with percussion experience report greater confidence in predicting a natural Ankora onset from the visible approach of Paul's hand? This is testable without asking Paul to play differently.

6. PRIMARY EVIDENCE: GESTURE TRAJECTORY CAN COMMUNICATE WHEN, NOT ONLY WHERE

Bishop and Goebl (2018) studied cueing gestures in piano and violin duos. Performers exchanged visual cues to coordinate entrances, while head and, for violinists, hand motion were measured with Kinect and accelerometers. The study found that features such as gesture acceleration, duration, and regularity helped communicate beat position and tempo.

Their analysis of the performed notes found mean asynchronies of approximately 44.7 ms for violin–violin, 58.5 ms for piano–piano, and 90.0 ms for piano–violin pairs. Those numbers describe ensemble coordination in that experiment; they are not perceptual thresholds, KLAAT timing targets, or evidence that one kind of music is superior. A further nuance from the authors: periodicity in head acceleration aligned with followers' tempo only 39% of the time. Visual timing information was useful but not perfectly coded or decoded.

This study is especially useful for interpreting KLAAT because it separates a gesture's visible spatial endpoint from its evolving motion. A hand does not need to be at the drumhead for a viewer to anticipate where it is going. But these ensemble musicians intentionally communicated timing to each other; Paul is not conducting a partner and need not make his improvisation more visually legible. What matters is whether his existing natural movements happen to be predictive.

A practical warning follows: a listener watching Paul's hand might forecast the next strike from its downward trajectory, but a camera recording at ordinary frame rates is too coarse to justify millisecond claims about that forecast without validation. Session 112 remains the technical safeguard.

7. PRIMARY EVIDENCE: VISUAL TIMING CAN HELP MOVEMENT, YET ALSO CONSTRAIN IT

Colley, Varlet, MacRitchie, and Keller (2018) compared 29 musicians and 28 non-musicians performing a drumming task while seeing a virtual conductor, visual metronome, or stationary circle. The conductor generally improved anticipation and synchronization during tempo-changing musical sequences. For non-musicians, it also produced more structured head movements, and some participants showed rigid ancillary movements rather than straightforward benefits.

This study introduces an important contradiction to a simplistic 'more visual information is always better' story. Visual guidance can aid some tasks while affecting movement in ways that are not uniformly advantageous. It involved active synchronization with prescribed tempo changes, not passive listening to free improvisation. It therefore supports the general capacity of visual information to guide temporal anticipation, but it cannot predict whether a KLAAT audience would find the video more absorbing, energizing, distracting, or aesthetically compelling.

8. WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT INFER ABOUT KLAAT

ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT Visible musical action can modulate early auditory EEG responses; learned drumming expertise can change neural processing of matched and mismatched action–sound events; ensemble gestures can communicate timing; and visual conducting can affect synchronization in controlled tasks. These results arise from different tasks, measures, and populations and should not be collapsed into a single effect size.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE Visual anticipation depends on whether the movement is actually visible, reliably related to the upcoming sound, and distinguishable from expressive motion. Audio-derived beat prediction may already be strong; seeing a hand may add little. The camera may conceal the hand, and the viewer may look at Amastacia rather than Ankora. Expertise may change processing but does not guarantee greater immersion. Laboratory neural response differences do not necessarily translate to conscious feeling.

PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES H1. In excerpts containing irregular Ankora entrances, a visible pre-strike hand trajectory may improve a viewer's ability to forecast which moment contains the next strike compared with audio-only listening to the same material. H2. A KLAAT performance video may elicit different reported anticipation or sense of immediacy than the identical audio without video. The sign of any difference is unknown. H3. Percussion experience may modify the size or consistency of those effects, but an expertise difference is not guaranteed. H4. The predictive value of vision may be greatest when auditory rhythmic expectation is weak and the striking hand is clearly visible. This is a conditional prediction, not a finding.

SPECULATION The ancient/ritual/archaeosonic character of a visible performer physically working with hide and agave might intensify some listeners' sense of embodied presence. That is a legitimate artistic possibility, but it may arise from narrative, aesthetic preference, novelty, perceived authenticity, or social context rather than from early audiovisual timing prediction. These possibilities must not be mistaken for one another.

UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS 'Watching Ankora entrains the viewer's brain'; 'the hand prepares the listener's nervous system for trance'; 'visible drumming guarantees stronger somatic resonance'; 'an EEG N1 reduction proves deep absorption'; 'performer flow transfers into the audience'; 'the natural materials have unique predictive neurological effects.' None follows from the evidence or has been tested in KLAAT.

9. THE MEASUREMENT PROBLEM: WHAT WOULD A FAIR COMPARISON REQUIRE?

For a future KLAAT archive, preserve one original continuous recording, its original synchronized video/audio track, and a lossless or high-quality audio-only export made without changing loudness. Do not compare a louder video with a quieter audio file. Use the same playback device and level, and avoid additional phone speaker effects when possible. Document whether the hand is visible before the strike, and whether the camera cuts or changes angle.

The key causal comparison is the SAME AUDIO with versus without authentic predictive video. That holds the acoustic signal constant while changing visual access. However, the comparison does not isolate prediction alone: a visible performer also conveys effort, expression, identity, and visual attention. To isolate preparatory timing, a more controlled design would compare authentic video with a visual control matched for brightness, motion, and attention but lacking valid pre-strike timing information. Such manipulations introduce their own artifacts and must be checked for uncanny or distracting effects. An arbitrary shifted video is not a clean control unless its timing and perceptual consequences are validated.

A passive listener question ('Did you feel anticipation?') measures a report. An objectively scored prediction task ('Will the next strike occur in this marked interval?') measures performance against the actual onset. They answer different questions. Neither is a measure of trance. Likewise, an EEG effect in an external study is not a proxy for a KLAAT listener's emotion, breathing, or movement.

For sound analysis, define the strike event using audio onset inspection, with explicit uncertainty if Amastacia's drone masks the transient. For video, annotate the first clearly visible preparatory movement without assuming that the precise motion-to-impact interval is known. Compare event-level patterns across several independent takes and note if multiple clips are excerpts of one performance (Session 118). If a small pilot has six people hearing four clips, it has six people and four stimuli, not 24 independent listeners or 24 independent performances.

Order, familiarity, expectation, and repeat exposure matter. Someone who hears a passage first may learn its strike times and no longer need visual prediction on the second exposure. Counterbalancing can reduce this bias, but cannot erase learning. In an exploratory pilot, a transparent description of these limitations is better than overstated significance.

10. WHY THIS MATTERS FOR KLAAT'S ARTISTIC IDENTITY

KLAAT does not need an engineered stage gesture. A hand already moves because the hide must be struck; breath already moves because Amastacia must be sounded. This session asks whether those naturally necessary movements are part of the audience's experience when visible.

That may help explain why live KLAAT, a video of KLAAT, and an audio-only recording feel different even when they contain the same performance. The difference need not be mystical. It can emerge from ordinary predictive perception, learned action knowledge, the viewer's attention, and the meanings attached to visible effort. None of those explanations requires a score or an obligatory phase structure.

There is also a useful artistic boundary. More predictable is not automatically more absorbing. Some listeners may enjoy the clarity of an approaching strike; others may prefer the uncertainty of hearing it arrive without seeing it. Video may strengthen a sense of physical engagement while reducing the ambiguity that makes audio-only listening compelling. This is not a problem to solve by changing the music. It is a contrast worth understanding.

11. OPEN QUESTIONS FOR THE NEXT RESEARCH LAYER

Does an irregular single strike have a measurable pre-onset visual forecast window? Are natural palm and wrist motions more informative than head or torso movement? Can a listener distinguish a strike from a near-strike repositioning? Does visual predictability influence subjective surprise, bodily activation, or perceived impact independently of the recorded amplitude? Does instrument familiarity matter more than general musical training? Do handheld camera framing and codec timing distort the inferred lead-up? Does the effect survive when the listener is walking or doing another task rather than looking at the screen? These questions are unresolved for KLAAT and should remain open until actual material and listeners are available.

12. ONE OPTIONAL OBSERVATION

When you eventually review one of your existing unedited KLAAT phone videos, watch a short section with the sound muted. Without trying to change your technique, notice whether you can tell from your own hand movement when Ankora is about to be struck—and whether some movements look like strikes but do not become strikes. Then listen normally. Treat it as a curiosity about what the video reveals, not a performance exercise or a claim about other listeners.

The central conclusion: the sound of an improvised Ankora strike can be unchanged while the viewer's information about its imminent arrival changes. Whether that visual foreknowledge strengthens, weakens, or leaves unchanged KLAAT's absorption and activation is an empirical question, not a mandate for the music.
