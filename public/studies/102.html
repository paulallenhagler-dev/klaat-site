KLAAT / RESONANCE RESEARCH / SESSION 102

THE MEMORY THAT WAS NEVER IN THE MUSIC

Autobiographical recall, personal association, and why a listener’s past is not an acoustic property of Amastacia or Ankora

SESSION 102 / 164Listening & PerceptionRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS A critical synthesis of published experiments and naturalistic diary work. No new KLAAT recording, participant, physiological, or neural measurements were collected. The findings below concern other music, primarily familiar popular songs. Their relevance to an original, largely nonmelodic, improvised drone-and-frame-drum performance is a question to investigate, not a demonstrated effect.

CENTRAL QUESTION

Imagine someone hearing KLAAT for the first time. Ankora’s hide gives the room a pulse; Amastacia sustains a rough, breathing drone. The listener unexpectedly thinks of an old landscape, a night drive, a relative, or a place they have not visited in years. Did the music contain that memory? No. The sound may have provided a cue, but the remembered episode belongs to the listener’s history.

This distinction matters because an artist can make an unusually evocative work without dictating the images, events, or emotional meanings it evokes. KLAAT’s archaeological, ancient-feeling atmosphere is part of Paul’s artistic language. It is not a literal recording of a universal ancestral memory. Science can investigate whether particular people report memories during authentic listening, what those memories are like, and which factors make retrieval more or less likely. It cannot read a listener’s personal past from the sound’s frequency spectrum.

THE CENTRAL FINDING

Music can cue autobiographical memories—remembered events from one’s own life—and some experiments find those memories unusually vivid. But cue familiarity, personal association, liking, age, emotional expression, and listening context matter. A first exposure to unfamiliar KLAAT cannot be assumed to reproduce the memory-cueing behavior of chart songs someone heard throughout adolescence. Nor is a recalled life event the same as being absorbed in imaginary scenery, experiencing a chill, or involuntarily hearing the music again after it stops.

1. WHY THIS IS A NEW QUESTION

Session 85 separated attention and inner imagery during listening. Session 97 examined how an entire listening experience is remembered afterward. Session 99 separated emotional chills from physical vibration. Session 100 addressed involuntary musical imagery: the sound or musical pattern returning in the mind after playback. Session 101 examined circular breathing as performer-side motor coordination. None of those is a dedicated study of MUSIC-CUED AUTOBIOGRAPHICAL RECALL: a remembered event from the listener’s own life, elicited while music is heard.

The recent Hourly Research notes through October 19 at 14:13 CDT address such distinct questions as cognitive load during musical pauses, co-improvisation, headphone externalization, color associations, low-cut filters, respiration-phase effects, and self-generated sound attenuation. A targeted Library search for music-evoked autobiographical memory did not identify a dedicated prior KLAAT study or recent hourly pass. This session therefore addresses a new outcome category, rather than renaming imagery or post-listening replay.

2. FIVE EXPERIENCES THAT CAN SOUND SIMILAR IN CONVERSATION

A listener saying ‘it took me somewhere’ is reporting something real about experience, but the phrase does not tell us which phenomenon occurred.

AUTOBIOGRAPHICAL MEMORY: ‘I remembered sitting in my grandfather’s workshop when I was twelve.’ There is an identifiable personal episode or life period. Details may be inaccurate, reconstructed, or blended; subjective vividness does not verify historical truth.

SCENE IMAGERY: ‘I pictured an enormous dark desert.’ The image need not be a memory. It could be a novel construction, fantasy, metaphor, or visual association.

EMOTIONAL APPRAISAL: ‘It felt ancient and unsettling.’ This describes affect and meaning, not a retrieved event.

MUSICAL IMAGERY: ‘Later I kept hearing that drum figure in my head.’ This is closer to Session 100’s after-sound phenomenon, even if no life episode accompanies it.

PHYSIOLOGICAL OR BODILY RESPONSE: ‘My skin tingled and I wanted to move.’ This might reflect emotion, movement urge, temperature, vibration, or other causes. It is not evidence of autobiographical recall, much less of a particular neural or altered-consciousness state.

A single person may experience several of these at once. The categories are analytic tools, not rules for how someone must listen.

3. A MEASURED STARTING POINT: THE MUSIC–MEMORY CONNECTION

Janata and colleagues’ earlier song-cueing research, described in subsequent naturalistic work, found autobiographical memories associated with approximately 30% of presented popular-song excerpts in an undergraduate sample. This figure belongs to a particular stimulus set and population; it is not a general rate for any piece of music. In Janata’s 2009 fMRI experiment, 13 participants heard 30 excerpts drawn from popular music of their extended childhood and adolescence. On average 56.2% of excerpts were rated familiar, and 42.3% were judged somewhat or strongly autobiographical. Dorsal medial prefrontal activity varied with rated autobiographical salience during listening. The finding supports a relationship between personal salience and brain responses in that design; it does not locate a universal ‘memory center’ that KLAAT activates, and it cannot establish causation from an unmeasured KLAAT drone.

The selection of old chart songs was not incidental. It deliberately increased the chance that listeners already had associations with those songs. A listener encountering an unfamiliar KLAAT recording does not have that same song-specific history. Conversely, repeated exposure to KLAAT during meaningful moments might eventually make it a personal cue. That possibility is plausible, but it requires longitudinal observation rather than extrapolation from nostalgic pop.

4. FAMILIARITY CHANGES ACCESSIBILITY, NOT JUST INTENSITY

Jakubowski and Francini presented 24 music cues to 100 young adults in a repeated-measures design, varying familiarity, emotional valence, and emotional arousal. Familiar pieces elicited more autobiographical memories and faster retrieval. Familiar music also appeared to evoke more positive and arousing memories, but those differences were attributable to greater liking of familiar pieces in their analysis. Emotional expression mattered differently: negatively valenced, low-arousal music was associated with more negative memories; positively valenced, high-arousal music with more arousing memories; low-arousal music with memories rated more important.

This is a critical warning against an easy story. If listeners later report more memories after hearing KLAAT repeatedly, that need not mean the recording acquired a special frequency effect. They may simply know it better, like it more, have paired it with personal events, or have learned a narrative around it. Familiarity, preference, prior life association, and musical stimulus are tangled unless the design separates them.

For Paul’s art, this is not a reason to avoid repetition. Repetition is already a core aesthetic. It is a reason not to mislabel the human history accumulating around the music as a universal property of its acoustics.

5. WHAT A REAL-WORLD DIARY ADDS

Jakubowski and Ghosh followed 31 participants for seven days as they recorded naturally occurring music-evoked autobiographical memories and listening habits. On average, participants reported approximately one such memory per day. Memories often arose during ordinary activities such as travel or housework and were frequently vivid, involuntary, socially themed, and positive or mixed in emotion.

The important connection is ecological. KLAAT is intended to live beyond formal ceremonies: a long drive, a creative work session, a walk, or daily activity may be a genuine context for its music. Autobiographical recall, if it occurs, need not require lying still in darkness. Yet this diary did not randomly assign KLAAT or control every exposure. Its daily average is a within-sample observational rate, not the probability that one unfamiliar KLAAT track will trigger a memory. Participants may miss episodes, overreport striking episodes, or differ in how they define a memory.

This suggests a future question with a different answer from ‘Does KLAAT induce trance?’: Do personally meaningful associations develop around authentic KLAAT recordings over months of ordinary use, and do they differ between new and returning listeners? That is a longitudinal question, not a claim about the first listening session.

6. CONTRADICTORY EVIDENCE: MUSIC IS NOT ALWAYS THE BEST CUE

Belfi, Karlan, and Tranel compared memories elicited by 30 songs with memories elicited by 30 famous faces. Their reported music-evoked memories contained a greater proportion of internal, perceptual details and were judged more vivid than memories cued by faces. However, famous faces elicited more memories. The experiment therefore distinguishes the FREQUENCY of retrieval from the VIVIDNESS of memories that are retrieved. A cue can yield fewer memories but richer accounts of those that occur.

Cady, Harris, and Knappenberger also found that song excerpts, printed lyrics, artist images, and immediate memory descriptions differed across some autobiographical-memory measures and lifetime periods, but hearing the song did not outperform every other cue condition. Music is not a magic key that defeats all alternatives.

In a newer controlled comparison, a study of 84 participants contrasted familiar pop-song clips with matched spoken lyrics. Music elicited autobiographical memories on 49% of trials versus 33% for spoken lyrics, even with familiarity included in the analysis. Greater feelings of reliving after songs, however, no longer held once familiarity was controlled. That contrast helps separate music’s cueing advantage from the personal-history confound, but both conditions contained lyrics and familiar popular-song material. It cannot tell us what happens with wordless Amastacia and Ankora.

7. DOES A WORDLESS DRONE HAVE A MEMORY PATHWAY?

It could, but the evidence must be graded carefully.

ESTABLISHED IN OTHER MUSIC: Familiar musical cues can retrieve autobiographical episodes. Their emotional and temporal associations influence what is recalled. Music can elicit richer perceptual detail in recalled events under some conditions.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Many studies select chart songs from participants’ formative years. Lyrics, melody, artist identity, repeated exposure, culture, social context, and personal liking may jointly help retrieval. The experiments do not isolate low-frequency drone, frame-drum pulse, C#, ritual aesthetics, or improvised texture as independent memory triggers.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: The sound’s timbre, physical weight, recurring gestures, and context might cue memories by resemblance to previously encountered sounds or environments; over time, repeated KLAAT listening may become associated with specific personal moments. These are hypotheses, not findings.

SPECULATIVE: Some listeners might experience a particular nonverbal ‘oldness’ or sense of place that makes autobiographical material more accessible. Such a narrative is artistically interesting but does not establish an ancestral, collective, or inherited memory mechanism.

UNSUPPORTED: KLAAT opens ancestral memory; the C# drone directly accesses the hippocampus; 137 BPM evokes a fixed childhood epoch; frame-drum vibration retrieves trauma; stronger autobiographical recall proves healing, trance, or neurological entrainment. No evidence here supports those statements.

8. WHY THE PERFORMER’S FLOW DOES NOT PREDICT THE LISTENER’S PAST

Paul can experience a profound, internally coherent state while creating KLAAT. That is a performer report. He is producing sound, coordinating breath and striking, receiving tactile feedback, and making decisions—or allowing them to emerge—in real time. A listener hears a different mixture of information and brings an independent autobiographical history. Even when they both describe the moment as ‘transporting,’ the referents may be different.

This is a particularly useful protection for a spontaneous art practice. If an audience member remembers their grandmother, a childhood storm, or a train journey, Paul does not need to have intended those images, encoded them in the music, or recreate the same moment next time. The listener’s association can be meaningful without becoming a composition requirement.

It also protects negative and null outcomes. Some people will feel nothing unusual; some may dislike the sound; some may have no memories; some may be reminded of unpleasant events. More vivid recall is not automatically desirable. Asking participants to disclose traumatic or intimate memories would create unnecessary privacy and ethical issues for a small exploratory art project.

9. A CLEANER KLAAT EVIDENCE CHAIN

For autobiographical recall, use five distinct observations:

1. AUTHENTIC PERFORMANCE: the unedited Amastacia–Ankora take and what Paul actually played. Record date, duration and equipment without retrospectively inventing a score. 2. DELIVERY: headphones/speakers, level, room and setting. These affect the actual sound received and the context of listening. 3. LISTENER HISTORY: prior KLAAT exposure, general familiarity, liking, and relevant context if volunteered. Do not assume everyone shares the same musical background. 4. RESPONSE TYPE: autobiographical episode, general life period, imagined scene, emotional description, post-listening musical imagery, or no notable response. A listener’s free words come first; classification follows later. 5. INFERENCE LIMIT: what can be said about this person, this excerpt, this context and this moment—without generalizing to all listeners or a particular brain state.

A small sample can reveal whether the questions are understandable and whether people spontaneously report personal memories. It cannot establish a causal physiological effect or reliable population prevalence. If an eventual controlled study is desired, compare the same KLAAT excerpt with other audio conditions under matched playback and neutral prompting, account for familiarity and liking, and predefine what qualifies as an autobiographical episode. A nonmusical comparison should be chosen for a specific question, not merely to make KLAAT ‘win.’

10. WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE AESTHETIC

KLAAT’s void-black fields, fine archaeosonic line work, ancient-feeling textures, and symbolic visual world can invite interpretation. Those visuals are artworks in their own right. They might prime certain kinds of imagery or memory reports if shown before listening. That is a contextual influence to measure, not evidence that the audio itself contains those scenes. A blind audio-only response and a response after viewing KLAAT artwork answer different questions. Neither is inherently more authentic; they simply reveal different aspects of the experience.

The project’s aim of activation rather than sedation is compatible with this uncertainty. Autobiographical memory might be energizing, distracting, comforting, unsettling, or irrelevant depending on the person. KLAAT does not need to promise a specific memory outcome to have an identity. Its artistic power can remain open-ended while its research claims become more exact.

11. THE NEW OPEN QUESTIONS

Would an unfamiliar, wordless, improvised KLAAT excerpt cue personal events at all, and how often compared with general scene imagery? Would any such memories be associated more with the drone’s timbral contour, Ankora’s pulse, a recording’s room sound, or the listener’s prior associations? Could repeated use of the same authentic recording during everyday life make it a stronger cue for events that occur AFTER the recording was first heard? Do listeners spontaneously distinguish a real past event from a newly imagined ‘ancient’ scene? Does KLAAT artwork change the proportion of reports classified as memory versus fantasy? Are memory reports emotionally welcome, neutral, or unwanted?

The literature makes these questions reasonable. It does not answer them for KLAAT.

12. OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION — KRX-102: THE MEMORY / IMAGE DISTINCTION

If a willing friend listens to a short, unchanged KLAAT recording, ask afterward only: ‘Did anything come to mind while you listened? It’s completely fine if nothing did.’ Let them answer in their own words. Do not mention memories, deserts, ritual, trance, ancestors, or childhood beforehand. If they spontaneously describe something, ask once, gently: ‘Was that something you actually remember from your life, or more of an image or feeling?’ They may decline to answer or keep details private. Note the distinction without trying to increase the effect, diagnose anyone, or change the music. One person’s response is a conversation and an observation—not an experiment proving a mechanism.

CONCLUSION

The memory that appears while KLAAT plays may be deeply personal, but it is not necessarily written into Amastacia’s drone or Ankora’s pulse. The sound and the listener meet; their histories do not become identical. Research can help Paul recognize that boundary, honor what people actually experience, and keep his improvisation free from the demand to manufacture somebody else’s past.

Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.
