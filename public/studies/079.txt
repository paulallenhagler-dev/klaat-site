KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 79
THE PLAYER IS NOT THE LISTENER
Improvisational Flow, Audible Traces, and the Limits of Transmitting an Inner State
Published: October 11, 2026 — 7:15 PM CDT


GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


CENTRAL QUESTION
When Paul plays Amastacia and Ankora instinctively and enters an unusually absorbed, trance-like state, what—if anything—does that state tell us about what another person hears or experiences? Can KLAAT preserve the freedom that produces the music while learning which audible features invite listeners into their own forms of absorption, bodily engagement, or activation?


GOVERNING DISTINCTION
THE PERFORMER'S STATE IS AN EXPERIENCE. THE RECORDING IS AN ACOUSTIC EVENT. THE LISTENER'S RESPONSE IS A THIRD OBSERVATION. NONE IS A SUBSTITUTE FOR THE OTHERS.


1. WHY THIS IS THE NEXT STUDY
Sessions 77 and 78 asked whether Ankora's rhythmic activity can grow without making its underlying Clock difficult to recover. That is a useful question, but the discussion risked quietly turning a spontaneous art into a set of mandatory rhythmic arrangements. The latest research direction clarifies the proper relationship: KLAAT already exists as an evolving, personal, improvised sound-world. Science should help explain and document what emerges, develop technical fluency, and test the response to actual performances. It should not tell Paul what his next beat, breath, section, or tempo must be.


The accumulated Library hourly notes were reviewed; the newest available file is dated October 5, 2026, 18:33 CDT, and there are no newer hourly notes after Session 78. The existing archive contains substantial work on physical coupling, timing, perception, and measurement, but no verified KLAAT performer–listener dataset. The strongest unresolved direction is therefore a missing link: performer flow versus listener experience. This is a synthesis of primary research, not a report of a completed KLAAT experiment.


2. THREE EXPERIENCES OF ONE PERFORMANCE
Imagine Paul performing an uninterrupted KLAAT passage. The agave didgeridoo Amastacia sustains a low C# Field, reshaped by breath and vocal-tract articulation. Ankora, the elk-hide frame drum, introduces a repeating, human-timed Pulse. The relationship shifts without a written score. Paul feels unusually present; gestures seem to arrive without deliberation. He might experience time passing differently or lose awareness of ordinary self-monitoring.


A listener receives a different set of information. The listener hears air-pressure fluctuations after the room, microphone, speaker, or headphones have changed them. They do not directly receive Paul's motor commands, muscular effort, attentional state, or sense of time. They can infer expressive intention from sound and, at a live event, from visible movement. They may feel energized, deeply absorbed, restless, soothed, unsettled, or nothing special. The same passage can support different responses.


This is not an artistic deficiency. It is the fundamental problem of expressive communication: an inner event must leave perceptible traces before anyone else can respond to it.


3. WHAT IMPROVISATION NEUROSCIENCE ACTUALLY SHOWS
Limb and Braun (2008) scanned six skilled jazz pianists performing improvised and overlearned material. Compared with controls, improvisation showed reduced activity in several lateral prefrontal regions alongside relatively increased medial prefrontal activity and changes in sensorimotor systems. The authors interpreted this pattern as consistent with spontaneous generation under altered self-monitoring. It is a compelling demonstration that spontaneous performance can differ neurally from executing a prepared sequence.


But six jazz pianists in an MRI scanner are not a model of all improvisation. BOLD activity is an indirect hemodynamic signal, not a reading of consciousness. The result does not mean every improviser switches off an executive-control region, nor that Paul enters the same neural state, nor that an audience receives that state by hearing the performance. The findings concern the person producing the music.


Primary source: Limb CJ & Braun AR (2008), PLOS ONE 3:e1679, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0001679. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0001679


4. TRAINING MAY MAKE SPONTANEITY EASIER, NOT LESS REAL
Pinho and colleagues (2014) studied 39 professional pianists with varying improvisation backgrounds. Greater improvisation experience was associated with lower activity in some frontoparietal executive areas during improvisation and stronger functional connectivity between prefrontal and premotor areas, even after controlling for age and classical practice hours. Their interpretation was that training can make the online generation of novel music more efficient.


That suggests a productive way to understand KLAAT technique. Circular breathing, breath articulation, switching attention between Amastacia and Ankora, recovering after an irregular strike, and sustaining a pulse are trainable capacities. Developing them may free attentional resources for spontaneous choice. Skill practice does not have to become a compositional prescription. The purpose of a technical exercise is to make the instrument less obstructive when Paul plays instinctively.


Caution: this is correlational evidence across musicians. It cannot establish that a particular KLAAT exercise causes a specific brain-network change or guarantees flow.


Primary source: Pinho AL et al. (2014), Journal of Neuroscience 34:6156–6163, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4769-13.2014. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4769-13.2014


5. THE 2024 FLOW EXPERIMENT: EXPERIENCE MATTERS
Rosen and colleagues (2024) recorded EEG from 32 jazz guitarists improvising over six provided musical settings, producing 192 takes. Musicians rated their own flow. High-flow improvisations were associated with patterns the researchers interpreted as reduced conscious control and more optimized task-specific processing, with experience-related differences in reconstructed neural sources. Four jazz experts separately evaluated the recordings' creativity and other qualities.


This is closer to the KLAAT question because it pairs a performer's self-report with recorded output. It also reveals a limit: expert judgments of creativity are not listener measures of absorption, trance, somatic energy, or daily-life usefulness. The EEG source estimates are model-dependent; an association with flow is not a universal neural signature or a causal intervention.


Primary source: Rosen D et al. (2024), Neuropsychologia 196:108824, DOI 10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2024.108824. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2024.108824


6. THE COUNTERINTUITIVE FINDING: LISTENERS CAN REPORT MORE FLOW
Loepthien and Leipold's study of music performance and listening is an important corrective to performer-centered thinking. In a questionnaire sample of 207 participants, respondents reported more intense flow during music listening than during performing, contrary to the simple assumption that producing the music must be the more immersive role. A second questionnaire sample of 383 examined listening flow, music experience, self-concept flexibility, and well-being associations. These were questionnaire and correlational studies, not simultaneous measurements of one artist and their audience.


The result does not prove KLAAT listeners will become more absorbed than Paul. It does show that listening is not merely a weaker copy of playing. A person sitting with the drone may be deeply involved while doing nothing outwardly. Another person may find the same recording ideal for driving, walking, drawing, or working—experiences that need not resemble a ceremony.


Primary source: Loepthien T & Leipold B (2022; online 2021), Psychology of Music 50:111–126, DOI 10.1177/0305735620982056. https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735620982056


7. A DIRECT PERFORMER–LISTENER COMPARISON
Dean and Bailes (2016) studied nine professional piano improvisers producing structured and free improvisations. Skin conductance changes in performers were especially evident around musical transitions. The improvisers later rated changes in their own recordings; fourteen non-musicians independently rated musical change and perceived affect. Listeners' judgments related to musical structure, but not identically to the performers' responses.


This study is unusually relevant: the performer can undergo a physiological event while the listener interprets the audible structure in another way. It suggests that performer physiology, musical changes, and listener responses can be studied together without treating them as one phenomenon. Skin conductance, however, is an imperfect and nonspecific index of sympathetic arousal; it does not identify trance or emotional meaning by itself.


Primary source: Dean RT & Bailes F (2016), Journal of New Music Research 45:361–374, DOI 10.1080/09298215.2016.1207668. https://doi.org/10.1080/09298215.2016.1207668


8. WHAT GETS TRANSMITTED IS NOT THE INNER STATE
The musician's experience can affect decisions about timing, attack, breath, loudness, timbre, repetition, and when to let something continue or disappear. Those decisions change the acoustic signal. The listener then interprets the signal through their hearing, expectations, preferences, memories, body, activity, and environment. This is a chain of influence, not a direct transfer of consciousness.


Juslin's 2000 experiment offers a concrete example. Three professional guitarists performed three short melodies to communicate four target emotions; thirty listeners rated them. Acoustic cue analysis showed that performers could communicate intended emotions through partly redundant cues, although cue use differed between players. This supports the general possibility of expressive communication. It does not show that every private feeling becomes audible or that listeners must experience the same feeling.


Primary source: Juslin PN (2000), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 26:1797–1813, DOI 10.1037/0096-1523.26.6.1797. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.26.6.1797


9. THE ACTUAL KLAAT SIGNAL IS RICHER THAN A BPM
Amastacia is not merely a C# frequency. Its sound includes a harmonic spectrum, formant-like emphasis from vocal-tract movement, breath noise, attack and release gestures, loudness changes, and room-dependent coloration. A low fundamental does not guarantee strong mechanical vibration at the listener's body; playback equipment may suppress it. Ankora is not merely a 137-BPM metronome. It produces variable attacks, resonances, decays, microtiming, and accent relations. The two sources coexist in one auditory scene.


This matters because Paul's spontaneous state may be expressed through microscopic decisions rather than a dramatic section change. A slightly delayed strike, a rough breath edge, a held drone tone, or an unplanned quiet moment may be what makes the recording feel alive. These are candidates for analysis after the fact, not targets Paul must manufacture to satisfy a theory.


10. FLOW CAN BE PRIVATE WHILE THE MUSIC REMAINS USEFUL
Consider two authentic KLAAT uses. In the first, a listener closes their eyes and follows the pulse inward, reporting altered time sense and bodily salience. In the second, a listener plays KLAAT while preparing for the day, moving through the house or working on something physical, and reports more energy and sustained engagement. Both uses are relevant. Neither should be treated as a failed version of the other.


The first context invites absorption and inward attention. The second introduces movement, environmental demands, divided attention, and practical goals. The music may support one context and distract in another. Listening environment, playback level, and personal taste are moderators, not noise to be wished away. KLAAT should be allowed to function as an energizing soundtrack without being forced into a meditation narrative.


11. FLOW, TRANCE, ABSORPTION, ACTIVATION: FOUR WORDS, FOUR TASKS
Performer flow: Paul's report of fluid, engaged, relatively effortless creative action while actively producing sound. It is an experience, not a neural diagnosis.


Listener absorption: the degree to which attention becomes captured or sustained by the music. It can occur with eyes open, while moving, or during quiet listening, but the conditions and depth may differ.


Activation: felt energy, alertness, urgency, or readiness to move. Self-report is not interchangeable with measured heart rate, skin conductance, or other physiological indices.


Trance-like or altered awareness: an experiential description that may include time distortion, narrowed attention, changes in self-awareness, or unusual bodily salience. It should be described carefully and not automatically equated with clinical dissociation, specific EEG bands, or therapeutic benefit.


The terms may overlap in one person, but KLAAT research should not collapse them into a single score called 'resonance.'


12. AMASTACIA: THE BREATH IS A MOTOR ACT BEFORE IT IS A LISTENER EFFECT
Circular breathing, when used, coordinates stored oral air pressure, nasal inhalation, embouchure, and ongoing drone production. It is an embodied skill for Paul. The sound it produces may appear continuous to a listener even while the performer's breathing mechanics change. Thus, a breath-linked physiological event in the player need not have an obvious corresponding event in the recording. Conversely, an audible change in spectral color may be highly salient to the listener even if it feels technically routine to Paul.


KLAAT's science can help Paul compare takes for unwanted breaks, breath noise, sustained tone, or tonal control when he wants that feedback. It should not turn breathing into a required respiratory cadence or claim that listeners' breathing automatically follows the didgeridoo player's breathing. That is an empirical question requiring simultaneous measurements.


13. ANKORA: MOTOR FLUENCY MAKES ROOM FOR CHOICE
A recurring frame-drum pattern can become increasingly familiar to the player through practice. Motor familiarity may permit attention to move toward phrasing, dynamics, interplay with Amastacia, and the overall feeling of the moment. It does not mean the pattern must be rigid or that the listener perceives exactly the player's internal beat.


Earlier sessions investigated recoverable meter, density, accents, and timbral grouping. Those remain valuable descriptive tools. The crucial correction is that they are optional ways to understand something Ankora already does. They are not an instruction to divide every performance into an Anchor layer and a Surface layer, nor to intensify at a specified bar. If an improvised passage feels powerful precisely because the beat drifts or becomes ambiguous, the research should document that rather than label it a compositional error.


14. THE FOUR-PHASE ARCHITECTURE AS A LENS, NOT A SCORE
Ground Anchor, Threshold Gate, Rising Pulse, and Return Path remain useful names for broad experiences sometimes heard in KLAAT. They need not be mandatory steps, equal-duration sections, or a checklist Paul must remember while playing.


Ground Anchor may describe the moment Amastacia's sound-world becomes established. It may happen immediately, gradually, or alongside drumming.


Threshold Gate may describe the listener's recognition of a recurring Ankora pulse or another perceptual crossing. It is not necessarily the first drum strike.


Rising Pulse may describe an emergent increase in felt urgency, whether from density, accent, roughness, loudness, or a combination. It need not be a deliberate tempo ramp.


Return Path may describe the listener's perception of release, thinning, disappearance of an expectation, or re-exposure of Amastacia's Field. Some genuine KLAAT performances may end differently.


Use these labels retrospectively when they illuminate a recording. If a passage does not fit, preserve the passage; revise the descriptive vocabulary if necessary. The music has priority.


15. WHAT THE EXISTING KSV MODEL CAN AND CANNOT DO
KSV-0.1—[FIELD, CLOCK, SURFACE, WITHDRAWAL]—can remain a provisional annotation vocabulary for listening back to existing recordings. It is not a proven state vector of the nervous system, and it need not govern improvisation. Its dimensions can be estimated from acoustics and listener judgments, but the validity of those estimates remains untested for KLAAT.


This session adds a methodological separation rather than a fifth compositional variable: (A) performer-reported state, (B) delivered acoustics, (C) listener-reported experience, and (D) optional physiology. Claims about links between layers require data from the same actual performances. A meaningful correlation is not proof of transmission or causation. The archive's earlier boundary and annotation gates remain useful if they support this work, not as obstacles to creating music.


16. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE
ESTABLISHED — Improvisation can recruit different patterns of brain activity than performing learned material in controlled jazz-piano paradigms (Limb & Braun, six players).
ESTABLISHED — Greater improvisation experience has been associated with changes in executive-region activity and functional connectivity in a sample of 39 pianists (Pinho et al.).
ESTABLISHED — EEG correlates of self-reported creative flow differed with expertise in a 32-guitarist, 192-take experiment (Rosen et al.).
ESTABLISHED — Listening can be associated with strong self-reported flow; in one questionnaire study, listening-flow reports exceeded performing-flow reports on the measured dimensions (Loepthien & Leipold, N=207).
ESTABLISHED — Performer physiological responses and non-musician perceptions can both relate to improvised musical structure without being identical observations (Dean & Bailes, nine performers and fourteen non-musician raters).
ESTABLISHED — Performers can convey intended emotion through combinations of audible performance cues in constrained experiments (Juslin, three guitarists and thirty listeners).


17. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE AND CONTRADICTIONS
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — MRI piano improvisation, EEG guitar improvisation, questionnaire reports, and live didgeridoo/frame-drum playing are different tasks. Their effect sizes and neural patterns cannot be assumed identical.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — The 2008 result of lateral prefrontal deactivation is not a universal 'off switch'; the 2014 expertise study also found stronger connectivity involving prefrontal and premotor regions. Different tasks, analyses, and experience levels can produce different patterns.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — A survey finding that listening flow exceeded performance flow is not a simultaneous comparison of Paul and his audience and may reflect how respondents remembered their activities.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — The player's absorption may improve musical fluency without making every take more affecting to listeners. Listener preferences, playback systems, noise, activity, familiarity, and hearing differences matter.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — An energized daily-life listening experience is not equivalent to a quiet eyes-closed session. They should be evaluated in their own contexts.


18. PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES
H1 — Paul's high-flow takes may differ in measurable breath continuity, timing variability, attack dynamics, or timbral evolution from his lower-flow takes; the direction of those differences is not predetermined.
H2 — Some high-flow takes may receive higher listener ratings for absorption or expressive coherence, but the relationship may be weak or listener-dependent.
H3 — Listener activation and listener absorption may vary independently, even for the same recording.
H4 — A listener may find a recording effective as an energizing soundtrack without reporting trance-like awareness, and another may report deep inward absorption without increased energy.
H5 — Technical fluency in circular breathing and coordinated playing may reduce Paul's need for conscious correction, without imposing a particular compositional structure.
H6 — Audible microtiming and timbral variation may carry expressive information beyond event count and nominal BPM, but any incremental predictive value must be demonstrated against simpler acoustic measures.


19. SPECULATION
SPECULATION — Paul's sense of entering a distinct KLAAT world may be expressed partly through long-range consistency of texture and gesture rather than formal section boundaries.
SPECULATION — The project's archaeological/ritual aesthetic may change listener expectation and meaning independently of low-frequency acoustics; that could matter more live than on an unintroduced recording.
SPECULATION — Some of KLAAT's most effective everyday tracks may emerge from the same spontaneous performances that feel deeply immersive to Paul, even though their users experience a different kind of involvement.
SPECULATION — Training motor fluency may allow greater creative freedom and stronger listener engagement, but this needs within-performer longitudinal evidence.


20. UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS
UNSUPPORTED — Paul's trance-like performance experience proves the audience enters trance.
UNSUPPORTED — An EEG pattern from jazz guitarists identifies Paul's current brain state.
UNSUPPORTED — Flow, absorption, activation, motor entrainment, and altered consciousness are one measurable phenomenon.
UNSUPPORTED — C# has a unique therapeutic or consciousness-altering property, or 137 BPM mechanically drives the brain into a corresponding EEG band.
UNSUPPORTED — A listener feeling chest energy establishes chest-tissue resonance or a healing effect.
UNSUPPORTED — An improvised take is scientifically superior because it conforms to a four-phase template or contains an Anchor/Surface hierarchy.
UNSUPPORTED — Greater performer flow necessarily creates greater listener pleasure, stronger autonomic arousal, or a better daily soundtrack.


21. THE METHODOLOGICAL UPDATE: THE TWO-SIDED EXPERIENCE GATE
The new TWO-SIDED EXPERIENCE GATE asks KLAAT to preserve the difference between making and hearing. If Paul describes a high-flow passage, that report should be attached to the actual recording with its timing and conditions, not translated into a claim about listeners. If listeners describe an unusual response, preserve their own words before fitting them to a theory. If a microphone shows increased low-frequency energy, describe that acoustic measurement without assigning it a bodily or neural outcome.


This gate is permissive, not prescriptive. It is designed to protect spontaneity by moving most analysis outside the performance. It also protects scientific credibility by refusing to infer audience effects from the performer's inner experience alone.


22. WHY THIS IS A MORE PRODUCTIVE RESEARCH DIRECTION
A research program can spend years inventing ever more elaborate descriptions of hypothetical transitions. The accumulated KLAAT curriculum already has many useful tools: onset annotation, uncertainty intervals, acoustic level and spectrum, beat recoverability, bodily self-report, and listener boundary judgments. What it lacks is paired, authentic data from the same spontaneous performances. The next major discovery is likely to come from such data, including surprises that invalidate parts of our existing framework.


Paul does not need to compose toward a questionnaire. He needs to play, keep the recording, and occasionally attach a small observation to it. The audience can then tell us what the sound actually did for them. The study can develop without colonizing the act of playing.


23. OPTIONAL KRX-079 — TWO TAKES, TWO SIDES
Purpose: Find out whether Paul's felt performance state and listeners' responses are related in his actual KLAAT sound, without changing how he composes.


On two ordinary days, record one natural 8–15-minute Amastacia/Ankora take each. Do not impose a tempo, phase plan, crescendo, or special technique. Record at a consistent, non-clipping level and note the room, microphone position, and whether both instruments were present throughout. Immediately after each take—before listening back—Paul can jot down three short ratings (0–10): personal absorption/flow, felt physical effort, and sense of creative freedom, plus one sentence about what he remembers. This should take under a minute and must not interrupt the music.


Invite roughly 6–10 consenting listeners to hear both recordings in randomized order at comfortable levels, ideally on the same playback setup per listener. Do not tell them which take Paul liked or which one he found more immersive. Ask first for an unprompted description. Then collect separate 0–10 ratings for absorption, felt energy, urge to move, and desire to hear the piece again. Allow 'not applicable.' Ask whether they would use it for quiet listening, everyday activity, both, or neither. A listener can also identify a particularly compelling moment if one occurred, but no phase labels are required.


Look first for within-listener differences between the two takes, then compare the pattern with Paul's own ratings. Keep raw comments. If listeners disagree, report that honestly. Two takes and a small convenience sample are exploratory, not causal proof. If no relationship appears, that is a valuable finding: Paul's flow may be important to the creation process without predicting any one audience response. The experiment is successful if it reveals something about actual KLAAT, not if it confirms a preferred theory.


DECISIVE QUESTION
WHEN PAUL DISAPPEARS INTO THE ACT OF PLAYING, WHAT CAN A LISTENER ACTUALLY HEAR—AND WHAT EXPERIENCE DOES THAT SOUND MAKE POSSIBLE FOR SOMEONE WHO WAS NOT THERE?


CONCLUSION
KLAAT is not a laboratory composition waiting to be optimized. It is a living practice with an established identity: Amastacia's resonant breath, Ankora's organic pulse, raw texture, duration, repetition, and the feeling of an ancient acoustic world emerging in real time. The strongest science-informed approach is to make the techniques more fluent, the recordings more faithful, and the observations more honest. A theory is useful only when it helps Paul understand the music he already makes or reveals a genuine listener response worth investigating. Experience leads; science refines.
