THE GROOVE THAT CHANGES WHEN YOU LISTEN FOR THE DRONE
Selective attention, auditory scene organization, and the perceived relationship between Amastacia and Ankora


Guiding principle: Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


RESEARCH QUESTION
An unchanged improvised performance contains Amastacia's C# drone, breath articulations, changing harmonics, and Ankora's overlapping impacts and decays. Can a listener experience a different rhythmic foreground simply by attending to the drone rather than the drum? If so, does this provide a more precise explanation of why the same KLAAT take might invite absorbed stillness for one listener and active movement for another, without requiring a change in the recorded sound?

1. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE: HEARING IS AN ORGANIZING ACT
Auditory scene analysis refers to how the hearing system groups sound components into perceptual streams and objects. Frequency proximity, harmonic relationships, common timing, onset synchrony, spatial location, and continuity all influence grouping. Bregman's Auditory Scene Analysis (1990) synthesizes foundational demonstrations; its explanatory framework is not itself evidence that a particular KLAAT passage produces two distinct streams.


A classic primary experiment by Cherry (1953) showed the selectivity and limitations of attending to one of two competing speech messages. The speech paradigm differs from instrumental music: a sustained drone and a drum are not rival conversations, and comprehension outcomes cannot be transferred directly. Nevertheless, it establishes that listening instructions and attention can alter which part of a simultaneous acoustic scene dominates conscious report.


Carlyon et al. (2001) examined attention's role in the build-up of auditory streaming. In controlled tone sequences, listeners' perceptual organization could depend on attentional conditions. Such results demonstrate context dependence, not an unrestricted power to hear any arbitrary grouping on command.


No study cited here measures KLAAT-specific absorption, autonomic changes, neural entrainment, or trance.


2. WHAT THE ACTUAL INSTRUMENTS MAKE POSSIBLE
Amastacia is not merely a single sine tone: its C#-centered drone carries harmonics, breath articulation, turbulence and timbral variation. Ankora is not a perfect impulse train: elk-hide strikes include onset noise, membrane resonances, decay and sometimes near-overlapping events. These properties may provide distinct perceptual cues for grouping. Ankora's abrupt attacks are likely to be salient, but Amastacia's continuous sound can furnish an enduring perceptual anchor. Whether a listener perceives one integrated texture or two differentiated sources will depend on recording quality, room acoustics, playback, musical passage, and prior listening experience.


A single 60-second excerpt can support several truthful descriptions: 'the drum drives the movement,' 'the drone carries the tension,' or 'the two instruments move as one.' Such reports are not necessarily contradictions. They might describe different attentional selections or different perceptual organizations of an unchanged mixture.


3. QUANTITATIVE CLARIFICATION: ATTENTION DOES NOT ALTER THE WAVEFORM
Let x(t) = a(t) + d(t) + n(t), where a is Amastacia's microphone contribution, d is Ankora's, and n includes room and recording noise. This additive representation is an engineering approximation, not a perfect physical decomposition of interacting sources. Replaying the same digital file under matched playback conditions keeps x(t) fixed. An attentional instruction may change the listener's report R, so R = F(x, attention, listener, context), but that does not imply the source energies changed.


For an illustrative response-scale example (NOT KLAAT DATA), a person might rate 'urge to move' 7/10 after attending to drum attacks and 4/10 after attending to the drone. The difference is 3 scale points. It is a hypothetical contrast, not an effect size or a prediction. Even a real difference would not identify a neural mechanism without additional measurements and controls.


4. THE HARD PROBLEM: INSTRUCTION, EXPECTATION, AND DEMAND
An experimenter saying 'listen to the drum' both directs attention and tells the participant what seems important. Higher movement ratings could arise from attention, expectations, an attempt to comply, or normal trial-to-trial variability. A listener may also fail to maintain the requested attentional focus. The recording's repeated presentation introduces familiarity and order effects, precisely the concerns of Session 158. Therefore an instruction contrast is not automatically a clean causal intervention on attention alone.


If tested, counterbalance instruction order across listeners; avoid telling participants that one condition is supposed to be more energizing; include a neutral free-listening condition; preserve verbatim descriptions; and record device, volume, and whether the listener was already familiar with the excerpt. The primary comparison should be within the same unchanged audio, not across two different performances. A pilot with a handful of volunteers can reveal feasibility and surprising descriptions but cannot establish a population-wide effect.


5. EVIDENCE LADDER
ESTABLISHED: auditory perception organizes mixtures; selective attention can alter the processing and report of competing auditory information in controlled experiments; identical digital playback need not produce identical subjective reports.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: attention influences perceptual streaming for some stimuli and tasks; its direction and size depend on stimulus construction, instructions, listening history, and individual differences.
PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: attention to Amastacia versus Ankora could shift perceived pulse prominence, absorption, bodily activation, or urge to move in a particular recorded passage.
SPECULATIVE: switching auditory foreground could help explain the transition between KLAAT as an immersive ritual and KLAAT as an energizing daily soundtrack.
UNSUPPORTED: that KLAAT has already caused neural entrainment, a reproducible trance state, therapeutic benefit, or a special C#-specific physiological response; that directing attention reliably intensifies any of these outcomes.


6. WHY THIS MATTERS ARTISTICALLY
The sound is not obliged to choose one function. An improvisation can preserve the friction between continuous breath and discrete impacts. The listener's shifting attention may be part of the experience, not a defect in the composition. Scientific inquiry here should characterize what people actually notice, not prescribe a structure to make them notice it. Paul's performer flow remains distinct from the listener's absorption: feeling deeply immersed while playing cannot be substituted for evidence of an equivalent state in someone hearing a recording.


7. UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS
Does attention instruction change reported pulse more than reported timbral richness? Are effects stronger in recordings where Ankora's attacks are clearly audible than in dense, noisy passages? Do listeners naturally alternate foreground without instruction? Does playback on a bass-capable system make the drone harder to ignore? Can a listener's free-form language reveal auditory organization better than a single 'trance' rating? Are shifts stable within the same person or dominated by order and familiarity? Each question is open for KLAAT-specific testing.


8. OPTIONAL LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION
Choose one existing, unedited 60–90-second KLAAT recording. Invite two or three people to listen once without guidance and simply describe what carried their attention: Amastacia, Ankora, or their combined texture. Ask separately whether they felt still, activated, moved to tap, or something else. Keep their words and playback conditions. Do not coach a desired effect, change the music, or interpret the responses as proof of trance. If the descriptions diverge, that divergence is a useful observation, not a problem to fix.
