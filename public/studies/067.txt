KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 67
TWO SPEEDS: Event Urgency, Perceived Beat, and the Subdivision Paradox
Published: 2026-10-07 19:13 CDT (America/Chicago)


RESEARCH POSITION
The connected Master Instructions, Curriculum Index, Research Ledger, and Session Corpus were consulted before this session. The latest canonical publication is Session 66. No Hourly Research notes were found after the prior publication; the latest hourly note remains October 5 at 18:33 CDT. This session advances a contradiction exposed by checking the literature behind Session 66's most important new prediction: increasing Ankora's Surface Pressure at stable BPM might make Rising Pulse feel faster. Controlled research shows that adding subdivisions can, under certain tasks, make the BEAT itself seem slower. Both findings can be relevant without being interchangeable.


THE PRINCIPLE
MORE EVENTS DO NOT NECESSARILY MEAN A FASTER PERCEIVED BEAT.
KLAAT MUST SEPARATE THE SPEED OF THE CLOCK FROM THE SPEED OF WHAT HAPPENS AROUND IT.


1. THE CLAIM THAT NEEDS A STRESS TEST
Session 66 proposed a high-value KLAAT hypothesis: as Ankora adds subdivisions, accents, and density around a stable scaffold, listeners may experience subjective acceleration without literal BPM change. That is plausible as a claim about urgency, activity, or overall musical speed. But it becomes too broad if 'perceived speed' is treated as a single variable. What exactly speeds up in the listener's experience: the inferred beat, the surface event stream, the felt urgency, the urge to move, or the bodily activation? These are different questions and can have different answers.


2. THE SUBDIVISION PARADOX
In a controlled study, Repp and Bruttomesso (2009) found that inserting metrical subdivisions into beat intervals could make the underlying beat seem SLOWER, a filled-duration illusion. In one perceptual comparison, a subdivided comparison sequence needed to be about 2.6% faster on average than a simple reference to sound equally fast to trained musicians. The study also found compensatory speeding in some reproduction and performance tasks. This is not a KLAAT measurement, and the effect size is not transferable to Ankora at ~137 BPM. But it directly contradicts any blanket rule that more notes make the perceived beat faster.


3. TWO SPEEDS, AT MINIMUM
CLOCK SPEED: the listener's inferred beat rate and metrical level.
EVENT SPEED: how rapidly attacks, subdivisions, accents, and local changes arrive.
A third judgment matters artistically:
URGENCY: how rapidly the music seems to be developing, pressing, or demanding attention.
And a fourth matters to KLAAT's mission:
ACTIVATION: a listener-reported state, not something guaranteed by any of the first three.
These four should never be collapsed into one rating.


4. THE PHYSICAL NUMBERS ARE SIMPLE; THE PERCEPT IS NOT
At the provisional Ankora anchor of 137 BPM, a nominal quarter-note-level interval is approximately 438 milliseconds. Two equal subdivisions introduce events roughly 219 milliseconds apart; four subdivisions yield approximately 109 milliseconds. Neither operation changes the 438-millisecond scaffold period. The ear receives more events, while the beat period remains the same in the score or intended performance. Yet listeners may infer half-time, double-time, or another metrical level. The research must record which level they actually hear.


5. WHY THE APPARENT CONTRADICTION IS PRODUCTIVE
Madison and Paulin (2010) found that speed judgments for real music cannot be reduced to nominal beat tempo: musical signal properties and event density matter. Repp and Bruttomesso, by contrast, focused on perceived beat tempo and the timing of subdivided intervals. These are not necessarily incompatible. A passage can feel busier or more urgent while the main beat feels unchanged or slightly slower. It is also possible for a listener to feel neither. The important discovery is methodological: a vague question such as 'Did it feel faster?' can conceal opposing mechanisms.


6. ANKORA CAN KEEP THE CENTER WHILE THE EDGE ACCELERATES
Session 64's Scaffold/Surface distinction becomes more precise. Ankora's stable center is a predictable pulse that the listener can continue or tap. The unstable edge is the evolving set of events between and around those beats. Increasing the edge's event density may produce greater urgency without increasing the center's rate. But some subdivision patterns may also lengthen the perceived intervals between the main beats. The phrase 'subjective acceleration' must specify WHICH temporal layer is accelerating.


7. SURFACE PRESSURE IS NOT ONE THING
KSV-0.1 groups event density, subdivision, accent displacement, omission, and local unpredictability into SURFACE PRESSURE. That grouping was useful as a first model, but these operations are not equivalent. Density adds events; syncopation relocates salience; omission removes an expected event; accent changes hierarchy; microtiming alters local alignment. Some of these operations can strengthen the clock, while others obscure it. A single Surface Pressure score may hide mechanisms with opposite effects.


8. THE SECOND CONTRADICTION: GROOVE IS NOT MONOTONIC
Witek and colleagues (2014) found that medium syncopation elicited higher wanting-to-move and pleasure ratings than either low or high syncopation in the tested drum-break materials. Sioros and colleagues (2014) similarly found a non-simple relationship between syncopation, event density, and groove in controlled synthesized examples. Neither result says KLAAT should target a universal medium-syncopation setting. They do show why 'more instability' should not be assumed to produce more movement or activation.


9. PERCEIVED BEAT MAY MOVE TO HALF-TIME
Ankora's ~137 BPM anchor is close to twice 68.5 BPM. A listener may organize the drum at half-time even when the performer thinks in 137. If added subdivisions alter metrical salience, the listener might switch levels. A response of 'slower' may then reflect a change of beat level rather than a tiny filled-duration illusion. A KLAAT tempo test must first ask participants to tap or identify the pulse they actually perceive. Otherwise a shift from 137 to approximately 68.5 could be misclassified as a modest slowing of the same beat.


10. AMASTACIA CAN ALTER THE TIME JUDGMENT WITHOUT PLAYING THE BEAT
Amastacia is not just a sustained pitch under the drum. Her breath pulses, spectral changes, and intentional thinning can mark phrases and alter the clarity of Ankora's attacks. A dense Ankora pattern inside a stable Amastacia Field may be organized differently from the same pattern during a strongly modulated or withdrawing Field. This is a KLAAT hypothesis, not an established finding. It means Field Stability may moderate the relationship between event density and clock perception.


11. GROUND ANCHOR: TIME WITHOUT ANKORA'S CLOCK
Ground Anchor establishes Amastacia's continuous Field and the listener's initial sense of duration. It need not be metrically blank: breath-scale changes may still establish recurrence. The experimental value of this phase is a baseline before a discrete Ankora scaffold exists. Do not use Ground Anchor as evidence of 'slowing' or 'speeding' unless the reference being judged is specified.


12. THRESHOLD GATE: THE LISTENER CHOOSES A CLOCK
Ankora's entrance creates candidate periodicities. The Gate should be studied as clock acquisition, not first strike. Does the listener adopt the nominal ~137 BPM, a half-time interpretation, or something else? Does Amastacia make that choice clearer or less stable? A Gate that changes perceived metric level is a different event from a Gate that simply increases physical event count.


13. RISING PULSE: TEST URGENCY AGAINST BEAT RATE
This phase is where the distinction matters most. Ankora can increase subdivision and accent activity while maintaining nominal BPM. Ask separately whether the passage feels more urgent, contains more events, has a faster underlying beat, produces greater urge to move, or increases activation. If urgency rises while perceived beat tempo falls slightly, that is not a failed experiment. It may be the most distinctive result of the session: TWO TEMPORAL EFFECTS MOVING IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS.


14. RETURN PATH: WHICH SPEED PERSISTS?
When subdivisions disappear and Ankora returns to a simple pulse, does the inferred beat seem to speed up relative to the subdivided condition, as a filled-duration account might predict? When Ankora stops, does tapping continue near the previously inferred beat? When Amastacia remains alone, does the listener report release of urgency without a corresponding change in remembered beat? Return Path can distinguish a lingering clock from the disappearance of surface activity.


15. WHY MATCHED BPM IS NECESSARY BUT NOT SUFFICIENT
The next KSV challenge must compare performances with similar nominal BPM. But equal BPM does not guarantee equal metrical perception, equal event density, equal timing precision, or equal acoustic energy. If one passage adds many Ankora strikes, its total sound exposure and transient count can increase even when every strike is played at the same strength. Matching average SPL can require softer individual strikes, creating a new timbral or tactile difference. The experiment should report these tradeoffs rather than claim perfect control.


16. MATCHED LOUDNESS IS NOT ONE NUMBER EITHER
A single long-term level measure does not guarantee equal perceived loudness for two differently punctuated passages. Temporal integration, peaks, masking, spectral distribution, and duration can matter. Psychoacoustic studies of short versus long sounds show that equal-loudness relationships depend on stimulus duration and level. Therefore measure long-term level, peak structure, and listener-rated loudness separately. For KLAAT, note that changing individual strike force to match average level may also alter the mechanically received Ankora event.


17. WHY THIS MATTERS TO KSV-0.1
Session 66 proposed KSV-0.1 = [Field, Clock, Surface, Withdrawal]. This session does not add a fifth state variable. It adds a measurement constraint: Surface must be decomposed into event density, accent displacement, omission, and irregularity during experiments, and Clock must be validated against the listener's selected metrical level. The State Vector stays provisional. Its dimensions must earn their usefulness through prediction, not terminology.


18. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE
ESTABLISHED — In controlled rhythmic tasks, metrical subdivision can create a filled-duration illusion and a slower perceived underlying beat, sometimes accompanied by compensatory acceleration in production.
ESTABLISHED — Perceived speed in real music is influenced by more than nominal beat tempo; event density and signal structure matter.
ESTABLISHED — Listeners can select different metrical levels from the same temporal material, and selection depends on tempo and experience.
ESTABLISHED — Syncopation and rhythmic complexity can influence desire to move, but more syncopation does not produce a universally monotonic increase in groove.
ESTABLISHED — Sound duration and temporal integration can influence equal-loudness judgments, making a single level metric insufficient to guarantee perceptual equivalence.


19. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — The roughly 2.6% subdivision effect in one controlled comparison is not a predicted effect size for KLAAT or for a 137 BPM frame drum.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — Laboratory sequences, synthesized piano examples, and funk drum-breaks do not establish how Amastacia + Ankora will be heard in a reverberant venue.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — A listener's answer to 'faster' may refer to beat, event stream, urgency, or metrical level; question wording changes what is measured.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — Matching mean level may change transient force and tactile reception; matching transient force may change overall level.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — A recorded playback experiment has greater control but may not preserve the full embodied experience of a live performance.


20. PLAUSIBLE / TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — At stable measured Ankora BPM, added regular subdivisions increase event-density judgments and urgency ratings without reliably increasing perceived underlying beat tempo.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Some listeners will judge the subdivided condition's underlying beat as slightly slower, consistent with a filled-duration effect; others will show no such shift or choose a different metrical level.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Accent displacement and omission alter Clock clarity differently from regular subdivisions even at similar overall event density.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Amastacia Field Stability changes how easily listeners identify Ankora's scaffold under dense Surface conditions.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — In Return Path, removing subdivisions can reduce urgency while preserving or even shifting perceived beat tempo in the opposite direction.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Separating Clock, Event Speed, Urgency, and Activation yields more reliable KSV validation than a single 'felt faster' rating.


21. SPECULATION
SPECULATION — KLAAT may be able to create the sensation of 'being pulled forward' while the listener's beat estimate stays constant or slows.
SPECULATION — A stable Amastacia Field plus a rapidly changing Ankora Surface may produce a characteristic split between temporal orientation and subjective pressure.
SPECULATION — A deliberate transition from dense subdivision to sparse, exposed Ankora beats may feel like an opening of time rather than a conventional deceleration.
SPECULATION — If the two-speed dissociation replicates in live performance, it may become a defining compositional technique for Rising Pulse and Return Path.


22. UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS
UNSUPPORTED — More Ankora strikes always make the beat feel faster.
UNSUPPORTED — More density always increases activation or groove.
UNSUPPORTED — The filled-duration effect must occur in KLAAT or must be 2.6% at 137 BPM.
UNSUPPORTED — A subjective speed rating proves a change in brain oscillation, autonomic state, trance, or healing.
UNSUPPORTED — Half-time listening is inferior to 1:1 synchronization.
UNSUPPORTED — Equal average SPL means equal subjective loudness, equal tactile impact, or equal risk.
UNSUPPORTED — The State Vector has been validated because its categories sound musically plausible.


23. METHODOLOGY UPDATE: THE TWO-SPEED GATE
Add TWO-SPEED GATE to KRX. Every experiment claiming perceived acceleration must preserve:
(A) nominal and measured Ankora scaffold BPM;
(B) selected perceived beat/metrical level and beat-tapping period where feasible;
(C) physical event rate and subdivision density;
(D) perceived event speed / busyness;
(E) urgency / tension rating;
(F) movement urge and movement behavior where feasible;
(G) activation and bodily impact separately;
(H) average level, peak structure, and perceived loudness;
(I) Amastacia Field condition and listener position;
(J) presentation order, familiarity, and whether the passage is live or playback.


New rules:
DO NOT USE 'FASTER' WITHOUT SAYING WHICH CLOCK OR WHICH EXPERIENCE.
A DENSER SURFACE IS NOT A FASTER SCAFFOLD.
AN URGENT PASSAGE CAN CONTAIN A PERCEPTUALLY SLOWER BEAT.
DO NOT MAKE LOUDNESS MATCHING CLAIMS FROM A SINGLE LEVEL NUMBER.
IF CLOCK AND SURFACE PREDICT OPPOSITE EFFECTS, KEEP BOTH RESULTS.


24. CORE-KLAAT TRANSLATION
The music remains Amastacia and Ankora, performed by a human. The research may use recordings, taps, microphones, and analysis software, but none of those tools becomes the composition. Amastacia supplies the continuous Field, breath-scale phrase, and spectral context. Ankora supplies the scaffold and the changing event Surface. Silence exposes the expected clock. Repetition establishes the reference. Duration lets the listener acquire and revise that reference. The room and body shape what is received. The compositional discovery sought here is not a generated rhythm; it is a better understanding of what Paul's hands and breath already do.


25. KRX-067 — THE TWO-SPEED CROSSOVER TEST
Prepare four short, human-played Amastacia + Ankora passages using the same nominal ~137 BPM scaffold and the same four-phase miniature: Amastacia Ground Anchor; simple Ankora Threshold Gate; manipulated Rising Pulse; sparse Ankora/Amastacia Return Path. Record the actual event times. Keep total duration and recording/listening position consistent.


Condition A — SPARSE / REGULAR: a legible Ankora scaffold with few intervening strikes.
Condition B — DENSE / REGULAR: same scaffold plus evenly placed subdivisions.
Condition C — SPARSE / DISPLACED: similar event count to A, but accents or selected strikes displaced while the scaffold remains recoverable.
Condition D — DENSE / DISPLACED: event density similar to B, with accent displacement/omission added.


Do not electronically generate the music. For controlled listening, recordings are acceptable research stimuli. Calibrate playback safely; document average level, peaks, spectral balance, and listener loudness judgments. If exact loudness matching requires unnatural changes to Ankora force, preserve natural playing and analyze loudness as a covariate rather than pretending the passages are identical in every other respect.


Randomize or counterbalance passage order across listeners. Do not use the words 'faster' or 'activation' as advance instructions. After each excerpt ask distinct questions: (1) What beat did you hear? Tap it or choose its rate; (2) How busy were the events? (3) How urgent did the passage feel? (4) How much did you want to move? (5) How activated did you feel? (6) How loud did it seem? Repeat the sequence with several listeners and, where practical, on a separate day.


Pre-register the main comparison B versus A. The principal result is not simply whether B 'wins'. It is the pattern across outcomes: does density raise BUSYNESS and URGENCY while perceived BEAT RATE stays stable, slows, or shifts to half-time? Then use C and D to ask whether displacement changes Clock clarity or movement independently of density. On Return Path, compare the release of urgency with the persistence of the tapped clock.


A strong KLAAT-specific finding would be a repeatable divergence between event urgency and perceived beat tempo at approximately constant measured scaffold BPM. A null result would also be useful: it would show that the proposed two-speed mechanism is not robust under current KLAAT performance conditions. Do not turn any result into a therapeutic claim.


The final experimental question is:
CAN ANKORA MAKE THE SURFACE FEEL MORE URGENT WITHOUT MAKING THE LISTENER'S UNDERLYING CLOCK FEEL FASTER — AND CAN AMASTACIA HELP HOLD THAT CLOCK IN PLACE?
