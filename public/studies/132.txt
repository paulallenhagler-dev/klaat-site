KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 132
THE PLANT IS NOT THE PIPE
Agave tissue, acoustic causality, and the difference between Amastacia's material story and its measured sound
Published: 2026-10-29, 15:12 CDT


GOVERNING PRINCIPLE
Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


THE QUESTION


Amastacia is not an anonymous plastic cylinder. Its agave identity matters to KLAAT: the plant's long life, the transformed flowering stalk, the uneven physical surface, the fact that breath enters something once alive, and the contrast with Ankora's stretched elk hide all contribute to the work's archeosonic character. But a consequential scientific question follows: if a listener hears Amastacia's C# drone and its changing harmonic color, how much of that sound can legitimately be attributed to “agave” as a material?


That question is more precise than asking whether agave instruments sound good. It asks what kind of evidence would allow us to separate botanical origin, the geometry of a particular tube, its internal finish, the player, and the recording. It also asks what happens when the available plant science was conducted on leaves, spines, or a basal stem rather than the flowering stalk reportedly used for the instrument.


The central conclusion is deliberately two-sided. MATERIAL CAN MATTER TO THE ACOUSTICS OF A WIND INSTRUMENT. BUT A MATERIAL NAME IS NOT A MEASUREMENT OF THAT INSTRUMENT'S ACOUSTICS. A genuine agave history can be artistically important without being converted into an unsupported explanation for trance, a unique harmonic signature, or a conservation rule.


This is a study of an instrument Paul already plays instinctively, not a request to change the improvisation. No score, tempo plan, breath pattern, phase structure, or timbral target follows from the research.


1. WHAT WE ACTUALLY KNOW ABOUT AMASTACIA


KLAAT's working description identifies Amastacia as a C# agave didgeridoo. Its drone is made by Paul's vibrating lips and shaped through breath, tongue, mouth cavity, vocal tract, and the instrument's acoustic load. Ankora supplies a separate hand-generated pulse. The two instruments are played together in the practice, and the sound is experienced by Paul during physically demanding, sometimes deeply absorbing improvisation.


The botanical identification is less complete. The research archive describes a dried agave flowering stalk, but it does not establish a verified species, harvest location, original stalk segment, age at harvest, drying protocol, bore construction, interior coating, repairs, or microscopic tissue composition for this particular instrument. “Agave” identifies a genus, not one species or a measured set of wall properties. Even the distinction between a naturally hollow interior and a manually cleared or shaped bore needs maker-specific confirmation rather than assumption.


The C# designation is a musical pitch description, not an assay of plant chemistry. For equal-tempered C#2, a reference frequency is about 69.3 Hz at A4 = 440 Hz; the exact acoustic fundamental and its stability for Amastacia have not been established here by a calibrated recording. The perceived “low” sound can also contain substantial upper harmonics. A phone's playback of the drone is not a direct measure of the plant wall's stiffness.


A serious study should therefore preserve three separate identities: (a) the instrument's material and maker provenance, (b) the physical acoustics of the completed bore-and-player system, and (c) the symbolic meaning of that instrument in KLAAT. The third can be true as art even while the first two remain incompletely documented.


2. THE BOTANICAL CATEGORY ERROR: AN AGAVE IS NOT ONE UNIFORM MATERIAL


A leaf, terminal spine, basal storage stem or “piña,” rhizome, and reproductive flowering axis are different plant organs. Their tissues have different functions. One organ may emphasize water storage, another defense or structural reinforcement, and another the support and elevation of flowers and fruit. Measurements on one organ cannot simply be copied to another because the plant's Latin genus is the same.


Morán-Velázquez and colleagues (2020) measured the chemical composition of Agave fourcroydes spines and leaf fibers. Their structural-carbohydrate analysis reported approximately 52% cellulose in spines versus 72% in leaf fibers, along with 45% versus 24% hemicellulose and 3% versus 2% pectic polysaccharides, respectively. The paper also reported about 1.5 times as much total lignin in the spines as in the fibers. Those percentages describe analyzed fractions of those tissues under the study's methods. They are not a chemical profile of Amastacia. The important experimental result is the difference within a single species: even a spine attached to a leaf is not compositionally interchangeable with the leaf's extracted fiber.


A second study, by Pérez-López and colleagues (2021), used mass-spectrometry imaging and ion mobility to map fructans in Agave tequilana basal stem and rhizome. It distinguished fructan species from degree of polymerization DP3 through DP17, with different distributions across tissues. This is unusually fine-grained evidence that even within one plant organ the chemical landscape is spatially organized. Yet those samples came from storage and vegetative propagation tissues, not a dried flowering stalk; fructan localization is not an acoustic attenuation coefficient. The study tells us that agave tissues can be heterogeneous. It does not tell us how a particular finished didgeridoo vibrates.


There is also a scale mismatch. A chemical percentage from a destructively processed laboratory sample does not yield the density, wall thickness, anisotropic elastic modulus, porosity, microcrack distribution, equilibrium moisture content, surface roughness, or air-column geometry of a finished musical tube. These properties are related in complicated ways, but they are not interchangeable measurements. A high cellulose fraction is not a frequency-response curve. A lignin measurement is not a decay time. The words “organic” and “natural” are not physical units.


3. THE FLOWERING STALK IS A DIFFERENT BIOLOGICAL EVENT


The flowering axis is not merely a convenient piece of leaf fiber rolled into a tube. It is a reproductive structure built under a particular developmental and resource budget. Park S. Nobel's 1977 field study of Agave deserti followed water relations during a 159-day period from inflorescence emergence until fruit removal without loss of seed viability. The abstract reports approximately 3.1 kg of water stored in the inflorescence and a final dry inflorescence mass of about 1.25 kg. The approximately 68 leaves lost 24.9 kg of wet mass over the period, with the author partitioning water and dry-mass changes among leaves, inflorescence, and lateral floral branches.


These are valuable plant-physiology numbers, but they have a narrow meaning. The 3.1 kg describes a water-budget component during growth, not the water content of a dried instrument. The 1.25 kg is the dry mass of an entire studied inflorescence, not the mass of Amastacia or a standardized segment. Neither figure is an equilibrium humidity specification, an estimate of cracking probability, a wall resonance, or a safe transport temperature. Species and growth setting differ from Amastacia's unverified source. Nobel's work establishes that the reproductive axis is a substantial, dynamic biological structure; it does not provide the properties of an instrument made from one.


A later carbon-budget study of Agave deserti found that stored reserves supplied much of the carbon used to construct the inflorescence, with new photosynthesis contributing the remainder. That finding adds developmental context, not a transfer function for sound. It is tempting to romanticize a flowering stalk as “the plant's final energy made audible.” As poetry, KLAAT can work with that image. As biology, the construction of the inflorescence involves carbon allocation and growth. As acoustics, the listener hears pressure waves generated by the player's lips and coupled to the finished instrument. These are three different statements.


There is no verified agave flowering-stalk modulus, sorption curve, bore-loss coefficient, or frequency-response measurement in the primary literature checked for this session that can be transferred to Amastacia. Absence from this search is not proof that no such study exists anywhere. It means the current research program must leave that parameter unknown rather than fill it with a number from a leaf or another species.


4. THE COUNTERARGUMENT: DOES MATERIAL REALLY MATTER TO SOUND?


It would be equally careless to conclude that because bore geometry and the player matter, the tube's material is acoustically irrelevant. A controlled 2017 study titled “Acoustic dissipation in wooden pipes of different species used in wind instrument making” measured pipes made of maple, boxwood, pear wood, and African blackwood. By using input-impedance measurements and multiple air-column lengths, the researchers estimated attenuation factors and found differences among the woods. In their tested configurations, the attenuation ranking from greater to smaller was maple, boxwood, pear, African blackwood. Polishing reduced dissipation, especially in more porous woods; changing maple's grain orientation also changed attenuation.


This is an important correction to the simplistic statement “only the bore matters.” Wall properties and internal surface treatment can influence acoustic loss, at least in those wooden pipes under those laboratory conditions. But the experiment did not include agave flowering stalks, lip-driven didgeridoo performance, Amastacia's construction, or KLAAT listeners. A rank ordering of four woods cannot be extrapolated into “agave is warmer than PVC,” “agave carries more resonance into the body,” or “agave causes deeper trance.” Nor can it establish that every material difference is audible in every playing context.


In a wind instrument, several pathways coexist. The internal air column has resonances and losses. The walls may deform and dissipate energy; their surface may introduce viscothermal losses or roughness-related effects. The player's lips supply a nonlinear excitation. The vocal tract changes the source spectrum. The bell/open end radiates into a room, and the microphone or listener receives a position-dependent result. If two pipes differ in both material and geometry, a timbral difference cannot be assigned uniquely to the material. If the same pipe sounds different when played by two people, it cannot be assigned uniquely to the pipe.


The useful question is not “Does material matter?” in the abstract. It is “How large is the contribution of a specified material property, in this completed instrument, for this measured acoustic outcome, with the other relevant variables controlled or recorded?” The answer could be large, small, or hard to isolate. KLAAT has not yet performed the measurement.


5. THE STRONGER DIDGERIDOO EVIDENCE: PLAYER AND BORE INTERACT


Tarnopolsky and colleagues' 2005 Nature report and their 2006 experimental paper measured vocal-tract acoustic impedance while experienced players performed didgeridoos. They showed that strong vocal-tract impedance maxima could suppress some harmonics, leaving spectral-envelope peaks between them. In the 2006 experiment, tongue configurations produced impedance maxima in the approximate 1–3 kHz range. These changes can give a seemingly simple sustained drone a broad palette of timbral movements. Their theory paper modeled how the player's vocal tract and the instrument interact, including additional sounds from simultaneous lip and vocal-fold activity.


These studies were conducted on Australian didjeridus/yidaki and players, not on Paul or Amastacia. Their importance is mechanistic: the sound coming out of a didgeridoo is not merely a passive reading of the material from which it was made. The performer participates in the resonating system. That is particularly relevant to KLAAT because breath articulation and tonal movement are among its defining existing features. If Paul's mouth shape changes a spectral band, the recorded shift is not proof that agave walls have changed state. If a sustained C# drone remains stable while its overtone emphasis moves, that does not require a different fundamental pitch or any mystical frequency.


Smith and colleagues (2007) examined another aspect of the same problem. Seven experienced players rated the playing quality of 38 didgeridoos spanning a range of pitch and geometry, as well as 11 plastic cylindrical pipes. The researchers correlated ratings with measured input-impedance spectra. A particularly notable association was that higher-rated instruments tended to have lower input-impedance magnitude in the 1–2 kHz range, a feature compatible with greater control over spectral formants. This is not a blind proof that plastic and natural instruments sound identical; it is evidence that measurable bore impedance can help explain perceived playability, and that “quality” is not reducible to a species label.


For KLAAT, “what Amastacia lets Paul do” may be a more productive acoustic question than “what sound agave always makes.” It points toward the interaction of a particular bore with a particular musician's instinctive technique. This does not diminish the plant's identity. It locates the audible behavior where the experiment can actually find it.


6. THREE DIFFERENT CAUSAL CLAIMS THAT MUST NOT BE MERGED


Claim A: “Amastacia is made from agave.” This is a provenance claim. It may be supported by a maker's documentation, photographs of construction, or other evidence about the object. A scientific species identification would require additional expertise and suitable material evidence. An artist's accurate provenance statement does not need to be an acoustic theory.


Claim B: “Amastacia has a characteristic measured response.” This is an acoustic claim. It could be investigated with dimensions, bore imaging or profiling where non-invasive, input impedance, controlled recordings, and uncertainty estimates. A frequency-response measurement does not, by itself, identify the plant species or the contribution of its chemistry.


Claim C: “Listeners experience Amastacia in a particular way because it is agave.” This is a causal perceptual claim. It is the hardest. A listener could react to the sound, the appearance, a verbal description of its material, cultural expectations, or the combination. If a person is told “this is an ancient desert plant” before listening, that information may shape interpretation without changing the acoustic waveform. To isolate a material-specific auditory cause would require carefully controlled stimuli and a credible counterfactual, not simply asking whether the instrument “felt organic.”


The same discipline applies to the combined KLAAT sound. Ankora's attacks may alter how listeners attend to Amastacia's drone, but this does not reveal agave chemistry. A performer may feel vibration through the lips, teeth, face, chest, or instrument contact while a distant listener hears airborne sound. That asymmetry is not proof of a plant-specific physiological effect. It is a reason to keep performer experience, microphone signal, listener report, and measured physical stimulus separate.


7. WHY A RECORDING ALONE CANNOT SOLVE THE MATERIAL QUESTION


Suppose a future phone recording shows a strong band near the drone fundamental and several higher harmonics. That is an acoustic observation about a recorded performance under a particular capture chain. It does not identify the wall material. The recorded spectrum combines the player, tube, radiation, room, microphone, placement, and processing. Even a high-quality isolated recording cannot tell us what the same geometry would sound like if its walls were made of a different material. The counterfactual instrument does not exist in that recording.


A simple mathematical bookkeeping model is:


Y(f,t) = F[excitation by player, vocal-tract impedance, bore geometry, wall/surface losses, radiation, room, capture](f,t).


This is a conceptual function, not a validated fitted equation for Amastacia. Some interactions are nonlinear; changing one component can change the player's response to the others. Consequently, subtraction of two arbitrary recordings is not an estimate of a “pure agave contribution.” A comparison between Amastacia and the industrial PVC didgeridoo is especially confounded if their keys, lengths, bore profiles, mouthpieces, surface treatments, and Paul's technique differ. The organic/industrial duality is artistically powerful precisely because the instruments are not interchangeable. It should not be advertised as a controlled material experiment.


A hypothetical comparison could instead use manufactured tubes matched for internal geometry, wall thickness, and mouthpiece, with known materials and repeated acoustic-impedance measurements. Even that would require attention to surface finish, environmental conditions, wall rigidity, and player adaptation. Such a study might quantify a material effect for that pair of tubes. It would not automatically identify the cause of a KLAAT listener's absorption or bodily activation. KLAAT does not need to build these test instruments now; the point is to understand the logic of the claim.


8. CONSERVATION: WHY BOTANICAL MISIDENTIFICATION COULD LEAD TO BAD ADVICE


The stakes are not only rhetorical. If someone applies a leaf-fiber moisture percentage to a dried flowering-stalk didgeridoo, they might invent a storage humidity target. If someone treats the stalk as hardwood, they might recommend a wood oil, solvent, heat treatment, or sealing method that is inappropriate for the actual finish. If someone assumes that a natural tube must be porous, they might infer that every visible discoloration means moisture damage. None follows from the studies reviewed here.


Instrument care requires the construction history: whether the bore was manually cleared, what coating or sealant was used, whether there are glued joins, and what the maker recommends. A modern agave didgeridoo may contain additional materials, which could dominate its response to water, cleaning agents, or temperature. The outside surface and the internal bore may have different treatments. The actual object's condition matters more than a generic “agave wood” rule.


For Amastacia, a conservative evidence posture is non-intervention. Do not cut, sand, soak, oil, heat, freeze, probe aggressively, or remove coating in order to validate a research hypothesis. Visible changes, a persistent crack, an unusual odor, or a repeatable shift in playability are reasons to document and seek maker advice, not to diagnose from a leaf paper. Ordinary comfortable performance and non-invasive observation can continue; this session does not establish that Amastacia is fragile, unsafe, or unusually durable.


9. THE CULTURAL AND AESTHETIC BOUNDARY


An agave instrument can have a deep-time, desert-derived, ritual aesthetic without a claim that ancient Indigenous communities used the exact same instrument in the same way. The Australian didgeridoo/yidaki has its own specific Indigenous cultural histories and practices. KLAAT's artistic identity should neither erase those histories nor invent a documented pre-Columbian agave didgeridoo tradition from the mere fact that agave was used historically for other purposes.


This is a productive boundary, not a creative restriction. “Archeosonic Ritualism” can describe the collision of materials, repetition, breath, diagrammatic imagery, and the sensation of something old without pretending to be a reconstruction of a particular ancient ceremony. The actual origin story of Amastacia, once documented, can stand on its own. The sonic identity is Paul's contemporary improvisational work with Amastacia and Ankora. The evidence layer should not force the symbolic layer to become laboratory language.


10. WHAT A FUTURE AMASTACIA DATA SHEET WOULD AND WOULD NOT PROVE


A compact, non-destructive instrument record could include: maker and provenance if known; maker's species identification with confidence level; whether the source was a flowering stalk; original blank photographs if available; finished instrument length; outside diameters at a few visible points; mass measured on a suitable scale; mouthpiece description; known repairs and finishes; visible cracks or surface changes; and the date and conditions of documentation. Interior dimensions should be recorded only if the maker already has them or if a safe non-invasive method is available. The data sheet is a record of the object, not a reason to dismantle it.


Later, if the equipment and interest exist, controlled acoustic documentation could add microphone placement, room, temperature, relative humidity, recording format, a few naturally occurring phrases, and possibly a specialist input-impedance measurement. A repeatable shift in measured response could support an instrument-state question. But unless material, bore, excitation, and recording are appropriately controlled, it would still not isolate a chemical cause.


No species should be guessed from appearance alone. No acoustic “agave signature” should be inferred from one waveform. A good record can say UNKNOWN. That word protects the integrity of the eventual manual and prevents future researchers from mistaking a plausible story for an observed property.


11. THE EVIDENCE LADDER


ESTABLISHED IN THE CITED NON-KLAAT RESEARCH: Different organs or tissues of agave can differ markedly in measured chemistry. The Agave fourcroydes spine/leaf-fiber comparison yielded approximately 52% versus 72% cellulose under the reported analytical procedure. Agave tequilana basal stem/rhizome fructans show spatially structured DP3–DP17 distributions. Agave deserti flowering has a measurable water and biomass budget. Didgeridoo vocal-tract impedance and instrument input impedance contribute to the audible spectrum and playability. Laboratory wooden pipes of different species can have different acoustic dissipation.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE: How much wall material influences an actual wind instrument depends on its bore, thickness, internal finish, wall structure, frequency range, excitation, and test method. The four-wood attenuation ranking and 1–2 kHz didgeridoo correlations are not numerical estimates for Amastacia. Botanical values from different species and organs are not agave-wide constants.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: Amastacia's particular wall and surface properties may influence attenuation, response, or playability; the bore profile and Paul's vocal-tract shaping may explain a substantial share of its timbral motion. Maker provenance and non-invasive acoustic measurements could narrow these uncertainties. A listener's response to the sound may differ from a response to a material description or image. None of these propositions has been directly tested on Amastacia.


SPECULATION: The physical history of a flowering stalk may eventually help explain distinctive details of this individual instrument's feel or sound, beyond what bore shape and technique explain. The material story may also deepen a listener's emotional engagement independently of acoustic effects. Both ideas deserve careful observation, not certainty.


UNSUPPORTED: A measured cellulose fraction for Amastacia; a verified agave species; a universal “agave tone”; agave being intrinsically more resonant, healing, ancient, trance-inducing, or neurologically entraining than PVC; a numerical storage-humidity or freezing threshold derived from leaf data; a claim that Indigenous people historically used this exact agave didgeridoo form; or any KLAAT-specific neural, autonomic, therapeutic, or trance effect. No KLAAT instrument sample, calibrated acoustic test, or listener experiment was conducted for this session.

The most important scientific discipline here is not to strip the instrument of its meaning. It is to stop one kind of meaning from masquerading as another kind of evidence. The flowering stalk may be the origin of the object; the bore-and-player system produces its changing sound; KLAAT gives the object its artistic place. Those truths can coexist without pretending that any one of them proves the others.


CONCLUSION — A LIVING HISTORY, NOT A READY-MADE ACOUSTIC LAW


Amastacia can be profoundly specific without possessing a scientifically established “agave frequency.” Its physical origin, unusual construction, C# drone, breath-driven harmonic changes, and partnership with Ankora make it a singular part of KLAAT. The research does not need to translate that singularity into a universal material claim. In fact, the strongest available evidence says to be more careful: agave organs are not interchangeable, wood-pipe material effects are real but conditional, and the player and bore are active contributors to the final sound.


KLAAT remains experience-led. Paul should continue to discover the music by playing it. Scientific refinement begins afterward, by asking what was measured, what is known about the actual object, and what remains untested. A faithful record of uncertainty is more valuable than an invented explanation for why the drone moves people.


OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-132: ONE QUESTION FOR THE MAKER


Only if it is easy and welcome: ask Amastacia's maker whether the original blank was an agave flowering stalk, whether the species or harvest region is known, and whether the bore was cleared, coated, or sealed. Keep the reply verbatim, including any uncertainty. Do not inspect destructively, change the instrument, or play differently. If maker information is unavailable, record “provenance not yet verified” and leave the matter open. The goal is to strengthen the instrument's history, not to make it perform according to a theory.
