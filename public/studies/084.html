THE FAMILIAR SOUND THAT STAYS ALIVE

Repetition, Listening Endurance, and the Difference Between Habituation and Engagement

SESSION 084 / 164Listening & PerceptionRESEARCH SESSION

CENTRAL QUESTION

KLAAT's drone can persist, Ankora can repeat, and a performance can continue for a long time without a conventional song structure. The sound is intentionally spare yet never literally static: breath pressure shifts, harmonics move, attacks differ, a rhythm gains or loses weight, and noise textures appear and dissolve. What happens to a listener as that world becomes familiar? Does repetition support deeper absorption, produce fatigue, or do both at different moments? And could the same recording remain compelling as an energizing soundtrack during ordinary daily life?

Session 83 examined how a sound can seem continuous across a physical interruption. Session 84 moves from continuity over milliseconds or seconds to engagement over minutes, repeated hearings, and daily use. The crucial discovery from the literature is not a rule about how much novelty KLAAT should contain. It is a warning against treating four different processes as one: acoustic repetition, sensory adaptation, learned familiarity, and the listener's decision to keep listening.

GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

REPETITION CAN MAKE A SOUND MORE PREDICTABLE WITHOUT MAKING THE EXPERIENCE LESS MEANINGFUL. A LISTENER CAN BECOME LESS SURPRISED, MORE FAMILIAR, MORE ABSORBED, OR MORE BORED — AND THESE OUTCOMES MUST BE MEASURED SEPARATELY.

1. WHY THIS IS THE NEXT QUESTION

The last sessions addressed the chain from Paul's playing state to recorded sound, playback fidelity, circular breathing, and perceptual continuity. They exposed a larger gap. KLAAT's central material is sustained and repeated, yet we have not tested whether prolonged exposure strengthens or weakens listener engagement. This matters for a 30-minute release, a live event, and everyday playback while working, walking, drawing, or doing chores.

The previous methodological work was useful, but it can tempt us to overinterpret microscopic acoustic events. A listener may remain engaged through hundreds of similar drum strikes without noticing each one. Another listener may recognize Amastacia's tone after seconds and then turn attention elsewhere. Neither response is a failure of the composition. They represent different uses of music, and they cannot be inferred from event counts alone.

The existing note concerned multi-input mechanical vibration, a valuable but separate unresolved engineering problem. Today's direction follows the strongest unresolved issue in the published sessions and the project's current artistic brief: what long repetition does to a real listener in focused and ordinary-life contexts.

2. FOUR PHENOMENA THAT SOUND SIMILAR IN CONVERSATION

ACOUSTIC REPETITION means recurring events or approximately recurring patterns in the physical sound. Ankora can repeat a pulse with small variations in timing, amplitude, timbre, and decay. Amastacia can maintain a recognizable pitch center while changing harmonic emphasis and breath grain. Neither instrument needs to produce exact copies.

SENSORY ADAPTATION refers to changes in neural responsiveness during repeated or sustained stimulation. It is a physiological phenomenon studied with specific stimuli and recording methods. An adapted neural response is not a direct measure of boredom, trance, pleasure, or lack of attention.

LEARNED FAMILIARITY refers to the listener acquiring information about the sound's structure, identity, or style. Familiarity can make previously puzzling material easier to follow. It can also change expectation and personal meaning.

ENGAGEMENT refers to experience and behavior: enjoyment, absorption, attention, movement impulse, desire to continue, or voluntary replay. These outcomes need not move together. Someone can love familiar music without actively scrutinizing it; someone can concentrate intensely on an unfamiliar sound without enjoying it.

A fifth term, HABITUATION, is used differently across literatures. In experimental psychology it often means a reduction in response after repeated presentation, with controls to distinguish it from fatigue or sensory adaptation. In everyday speech it means 'getting used to it.' For KLAAT research we should always say which meaning is intended.

3. REPETITION CAN INCREASE APPRECIATION OF UNFAMILIAR MUSIC

In a 2013 experiment, Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis presented nonexpert listeners with one-minute excerpts of unfamiliar contemporary art music by Luciano Berio and Elliott Carter. The original excerpts were compared with versions containing repeated segments, either immediately or after a delay. Listeners rated the repeated versions more enjoyable, interesting, and artistic on seven-point scales [S1].

The important feature is the comparison. The study did not merely find that fans enjoy their favorite repeated songs. It manipulated repetition inside music unfamiliar to the audience. Repetition made that particular material more approachable and more appealing on several judgments.

For KLAAT, this challenges the reflexive idea that repeated Ankora strikes or a sustained Amastacia field must be inherently boring. Repetition may help a listener discover a pattern, orient attention, or build expectations. But the stimuli were short and came from composed contemporary art music. The finding does not tell us how a half-hour improvisation or a continuous drone will behave.

4. LONGER EXPOSURE CAN INCREASE LIKING — WITHOUT PROVING A UNIVERSAL RULE

Madison and Schiölde (2017) conducted a naturalistic repeated-listening study with 15 participants. Forty music examples were classified into four complexity levels, then presented 28 times across approximately four weeks. Liking rose with repeated exposure across the complexity levels; the researchers did not find the predicted inverted-U decline within the tested range. Their repeated-measures analysis reported a presentation effect of F(3,42)=12.35, p<0.00001, with no significant presentation-by-complexity interaction [S2].

That is a useful counterweight to the assumption that all repetition eventually destroys interest. The study's participants often listened during ordinary activities, making its setting more relevant to KLAAT's daily-life ambition than an exclusively silent laboratory session.

But the sample was small, participants volunteered for an unusually demanding listening regimen, the music was not KLAAT-like, and listening environments varied. It would be unjustified to infer that a KLAAT track must become more liked after 28 hearings. The study demonstrates a possible direction of change under one naturalistic design, not a law of exposure.

5. THE CONTRADICTION: NOVELTY STILL MATTERS TO LISTENING DURATION

Ran, Peterson, and Janata (2018) studied engagement with unfamiliar repeating musical loops across three experiments. They drew from a library of more than 450 stimuli and manipulated entrances, harmony, rhythm, spatialization, and timbre. They measured how long participants chose to continue listening and asked why they stopped or stayed [S3].

The strongest musical determinant of listening time was novelty from staggered instrument entrances. Harmonic variability and timbral features also mattered. But subjective enjoyment was the strongest overall predictor of listening duration, statistically mediating associations with groove, urge to move, interest, perceived complexity, and mood congruence.

This finding is not a command to add instruments or arrange dramatic entrances. It demonstrates that a listener's choice to continue can depend on changes in the sound and on how enjoyable the whole experience feels. A new sound can renew attention; so can a meaningful change in a familiar sound. The study's loop-based, multi-instrument material is still unlike a spontaneous didgeridoo/frame-drum performance.

Together, [S1]–[S3] establish a productive tension: repetition may improve aesthetic response while novelty may prolong voluntary listening. There is no universal 'correct' novelty dose, and no evidence that one fixed amount will serve every KLAAT listener.

6. EXPECTATION IS NOT THE SAME AS SURPRISE

When Ankora repeats a recognizable pulse, a listener may learn where the next attack belongs. When Amastacia sustains a tone, the listener may learn which timbral fluctuations are typical. That acquired model can make small deviations meaningful. But 'unexpected' is not the same as 'pleasant.' An unexpected crack, recording glitch, or startling loudness jump can be unwanted.

Cheung and colleagues (2019) studied musical pleasure using a computational model of harmonic uncertainty and surprise, drawing on approximately 80,000 chords from 745 popular songs. Their behavioral and fMRI work found that pleasure depended on an interaction: relatively surprising chords could be pleasing in low-uncertainty contexts, and unsurprising chords could be pleasing in high-uncertainty contexts. Neural responses in auditory cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus also reflected the interaction [S4].

This is important as a conceptual demonstration that uncertainty and surprise are not one simple axis. It is also a major transfer limitation. Their statistical model describes Western tonal chord progressions, not the continuous C# harmonic field and pulse-based structures of KLAAT. We cannot assign Amastacia an experimentally validated 'surprise value' using that chord model.

A reasonable KLAAT question is narrower: after listeners become familiar with a passage, do particular naturally occurring changes attract attention differently? Answering it would require listener reports or a controlled attention measure, not just a spectral novelty plot.

7. THE LIVING DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A LOOP AND A PERFORMANCE

Ankora's repeated strikes are not identical waveforms. Natural hand percussion contains microtiming, changing attack placement, variable membrane excitation, dynamic accent, and decays interacting with the room. Amastacia's sustained drone carries breath modulation, noise, overtones, and gradual articulatory movement. These characteristics can be measured acoustically if suitable source recordings exist.

The hypothesis that those microvariations prevent boredom is plausible but unproven. We do not know whether listeners detect them, whether they are pleasurable, or whether they matter more than broad changes in intensity or rhythm. Some variations may be perceptually invisible. Others may be interpreted as expressive, unstable, or distracting depending on context.

A useful distinction is between acoustic variation and meaningful variation. A spectrogram can show a change that listeners ignore; a subtle change in accent or timing can feel important without producing the largest measured spectral difference. The research must not promote every measurable fluctuation into a psychologically meaningful event.

8. DOES A LISTENER HAVE TO PAY ATTENTION TO BENEFIT FROM THE MUSIC?

KLAAT has at least two legitimate listening identities. It can be the main object of attention: a room-filling, immersive, physical experience. It can also be an energizing background for everyday activity. Those modes should not be ranked automatically.

Kiss and Linnell (2024) conducted two online experiments with 106 and 77 participants who habitually used background music while doing attention-demanding tasks. Participants completed a sustained-attention task at home with their preferred music versus relative silence or continuous noise. In both experiments, music was associated with less mind-wandering and more task-focus, higher reported mood and arousal, and faster reaction times. It also increased some low-arousal external-distraction states [S5].

The apparent paradox matters: music can improve some task-focus measures while also drawing attention outward. The effect may involve mood and arousal rather than the listener carefully analyzing the musical structure. This is relevant to the idea that KLAAT might energize ordinary activity without producing a meditative state.

But these participants selected familiar music they already liked. The study did not test KLAAT, did not isolate drone or percussion, and did not show that background music universally improves work. The sample was selected precisely for people accustomed to background music; other listeners may respond differently.

9. COUNTEREVIDENCE: TASK BENEFITS ARE SPECIFIC, NOT UNIVERSAL

Hofbauer, Lachmann, and Rodriguez (2024) used a within-participant design with 40 people completing immediate and delayed recall, phonemic fluency, and trail-making tasks under instrumental soundtrack conditions differing in tempo and emotional valence [S6]. Faster versus slower music was associated with better immediate recall (p=.002, eta-squared=.08) and phonemic fluency (p<.001, eta-squared=.16). However, delayed recall and trail-making showed no corresponding general tempo benefit, and individual sensitivity to background sound influenced some results.

These data challenge any single claim that 'energetic music improves cognition.' Even within one study, the outcome depended on the task. They also challenge the opposite claim that any repetitive music is a distraction. Context, personal preference, cognitive load, listening level, and task demands can all matter.

For KLAAT, the responsible question is not 'Does this music make people more productive?' It is 'For which listeners and which activities does this existing recording feel energizing, welcome, distracting, or neutral?' Actual task-performance claims require actual task measurements.

10. THE FIRST LISTEN AND THE TENTH LISTEN ARE DIFFERENT EXPERIMENTS

A first hearing includes unfamiliar timbre, uncertain rhythmic organization, and little personal history. Repeated hearings may make Ankora's accent relationships easier to anticipate and Amastacia's roughness less startling. Listeners may also form autobiographical associations: a recording can become linked to a particular activity or period of life.

This makes a one-off reaction insufficient to characterize a release. Someone who finds KLAAT unusual on the first hearing might come to value its continuity after several exposures. Someone else might enjoy its novelty initially but choose it less often later. The scientific program should allow both trajectories.

Crucially, repeated exposure and within-track repetition are different variables. Hearing one long repetitive performance once is not equivalent to hearing the same recording repeatedly over a month. Familiarity with didgeridoo music before the study is a third variable. They should not be combined into a single 'repetition score.'

11. WHEN THE PERFORMER IS ABSORBED BUT THE LISTENER IS NOT

Session 79 separated Paul's flow during improvisation from the listener's absorption. Session 84 adds time. Paul may experience a deeply continuous internal state while playing Ankora and Amastacia; a listener may become absorbed only after several minutes, may prefer the first minute, or may attend intermittently while working.

The listener's report of 'I lost track of time' differs from a measured duration judgment. Feeling energized differs from heart rate, skin conductance, or movement. Enjoyment differs from attention. If KLAAT eventually uses physiological sensors, they should complement rather than replace those distinct self-reports.

We also need to avoid assuming that Paul's high-flow takes are necessarily the most repetitive, the least variable, or the most compelling. Those relationships are empirical questions. His improvisation remains the source; the measurements follow afterward.

12. AMASTACIA: STABILITY WITHOUT STASIS

Amastacia's sustained C# drone can create a recognizable auditory identity over time. The sound's harmonic pattern, changing formant-like emphases, breath noise, and subtle amplitude changes may supply activity even when the perceived pitch center remains stable. In a long performance, a listener may stop attending to the existence of the drone while still noticing changes inside it.

That possibility is not the same as the auditory restoration discussed in Session 83. Restoration concerns perceived persistence across interruptions under masking conditions. Today's question concerns how ongoing and repeated stimulation is learned, valued, or ignored across longer durations.

The practical lesson is diagnostic rather than compositional: if Paul wants to know why a passage feels alive, inspect its actual breath and harmonic behavior. Do not conclude that a stable fundamental means the entire sound is static, and do not insist that every sustained passage needs an added flourish.

13. ANKORA: A REPEATED CLOCK CAN HAVE MORE THAN ONE FUNCTION

Ankora's recurrence can support motor anticipation, establish an orientation for listening, and create continuity. Its changing attack density, accents, timbre, and decay may also provide evolving information. The same pulse may feel calming to one listener, urgent to another, or simply suitable for maintaining momentum during daily tasks.

The approximate 137 BPM reference from an earlier short recording is not a prescription and has no demonstrated biological optimum. A listener may infer a half-time beat or track a larger rhythmic grouping. That choice can change with familiarity and listening context. Event density, perceived beat, felt urgency, and voluntary movement remain separate variables.

The artistic strength of Ankora may lie partly in the fact that repeated strokes are human acts rather than exact copies. But 'human' is not itself a validated causal mechanism for absorption. That hypothesis must survive comparison with real listeners and recordings.

14. THE TWO INSTRUMENTS TOGETHER

The most interesting unit of analysis may not be the drone or drum alone. It may be their changing relationship. A stable Amastacia field can provide a reference against which Ankora's accents become conspicuous. Ankora's attacks can redirect attention toward or away from Amastacia's breath textures. A long passage may remain recognizably the same world while the balance of what listeners notice shifts.

This is a plausible account of KLAAT's archaeosonic identity: a persistent environment with living activity inside it. It is not proof of a neural mechanism. The 'ancient' or 'ritual' meaning is an aesthetic and interpretive frame, not a measurement of acoustics. That frame can legitimately matter to listeners without being mistaken for evidence of a physiological trance effect.

15. WHAT ABOUT THE FOUR PHASES?

Ground Anchor, Threshold Gate, Rising Pulse, and Return Path can remain retrospective labels for patterns that happen to emerge in a recording. They are not obligations. A performance might move gradually from drone to pulse and back; another might begin with both instruments, hover in a dense texture, or end abruptly. The music is not incorrect if it refuses the labels.

If a listener hears something resembling Ground Anchor, that may describe initial orientation or familiarity-building. A Threshold Gate might be a change in what attracts attention. A Rising Pulse might be a subjective increase in urgency without a faster beat. A Return Path might be a reduction of events or simply a change in the listener's attention. These interpretations are optional and must not be retrofitted as though Paul intended them all.

The provisional KSV-0.1 vocabulary — Field Stability, Clock Strength, Surface Pressure, Withdrawal — remains a descriptive notebook, not a control panel. Today's work adds no fifth dimension and imposes no formal score.

16. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE

Within studied musical materials, added repetition can increase aesthetic ratings of unfamiliar music [S1]. Repeated exposure across weeks can increase liking without the expected decline, at least in one small naturalistic study [S2]. In repeating musical loops, new entrances and certain timbral/harmonic changes can affect how long listeners choose to stay, while enjoyment is a powerful correlate of listening time [S3]. In tonal harmony, uncertainty and surprise interact in predicting pleasure, with associated fMRI responses [S4]. Preferred background music can alter self-reported mood, attention states, and some performance measures [S5]. The effects of background music on cognitive tasks vary with task and listener characteristics [S6].

These are established results within their own experimental designs. They are not demonstrations that KLAAT causes trance, neural entrainment, enhanced productivity, or any specific physiological state.

17. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE

Margulis used brief composed art-music excerpts, not long spontaneous drone-and-drum performances. Madison and Schiölde used 15 volunteers with substantial music interest and variable real-life listening settings. Ran and colleagues used synthetic or constructed multi-instrument loops and measured voluntary listening time, which can be influenced by factors other than absorption. Cheung's model concerns Western chord sequences. Kiss and Linnell selected people who already listen to preferred background music; Hofbauer and colleagues used cinematic instrumental pieces and a small task-specific design.

Different outcome measures can contradict one another without the studies being invalid: liking may rise as novelty declines; a person may continue listening while becoming less attentive; subjective arousal may increase while a particular memory task worsens. No one result should be promoted into a universal rule for KLAAT.

18. PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES

H1: Familiarity with an authentic KLAAT recording may increase enjoyment and willingness to replay even if the sound becomes less surprising. H2: The listener's attention may shift over repeated hearings from the gross presence of Amastacia's drone toward smaller breath, harmonic, and rhythmic details. H3: Ankora's repeated pulse may support sustained activity for some listeners without increasing their reported inward absorption. H4: The same take may be experienced differently during focused listening and everyday tasks. H5: Listener preference for KLAAT may depend more on prior experience with drone, noise, and minimalism than on any simple acoustic 'complexity' score. H6: Natural microvariation may contribute to engagement, but its effect could be negligible or negative in some contexts.

None has been tested with KLAAT listener data. A repeated-listening pilot can begin separating them, but a small observational sample will not establish causality.

19. SPECULATION

KLAAT's appeal may partly come from an unusual combination of predictability and inexhaustible detail: the world stays recognizable while small events remain discoverable. Its spontaneous imperfections may create a sense of human presence that some listeners value. Long exposure may allow certain listeners to stop consciously parsing individual sounds and experience the piece as an environment. The same environment may function as a focused inward journey or as a companion to ordinary physical activity.

These ideas fit the artistic direction. They remain interpretations until tied to actual acoustic features and independent listener observations. We should not turn them into requirements for Paul to 'add novelty' or 'maintain an optimal complexity.'

20. UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS

It is unsupported to claim that repetition automatically produces trance, theta-band entrainment, autonomic regulation, healing, or a specific brain state; that 137 BPM or C# is a biologically privileged frequency; that small spectral fluctuations always prevent habituation; that boredom is simply neural adaptation; that more novelty always improves absorption; that familiar music always increases productivity; that one long KLAAT recording has the same effect on everyone; or that a listener's subjective bodily energy proves mechanical tissue resonance.

It is equally unsupported to claim that repetitive music must become boring, or that a recording is aesthetically inferior because its sections cannot be marked cleanly. These are questions for listeners, not assumptions to build into the score.

21. METHODOLOGY UPDATE — THE EXPOSURE–CONTEXT SEPARATION

For future KLAAT listener studies, preserve three independent exposure records: (A) how repetitive the sound is within the recording, (B) how many times the listener has heard that exact recording, and (C) the listener's prior familiarity with similar styles. Record listening mode separately: focused, casual, physical activity, desk work, or other. Do not assume these categories are equivalent.

Measure enjoyment, absorption, felt energy, attention, desire to continue, and voluntary replay as distinct outcomes. For a daily-life test, add whether the music was helpful, neutral, or distracting for the chosen activity; do not infer actual task-performance improvement without measuring it. Keep playback device and volume documented because Sessions 80–81 showed why delivery matters. Keep original audio untouched, and analyze microtiming or timbre only after hearing listener responses.

This is a safeguard against false inference, not a demand to compose differently. A result such as 'listeners liked it more on day three but paid less attention' would be informative rather than contradictory. A result such as 'some wanted silence while writing' would be equally valuable.

22. THE STRONGEST CONTRADICTION TO PRESERVE

One body of evidence says repetition can increase liking. Another says new sound entrances can sustain voluntary listening. Neither means KLAAT should alternate between a predictable loop and periodic novelty injections. Those findings arose in different musical systems and different tasks. A spontaneous performance can already contain a complex balance of continuity and change; imposing a recipe could destroy the very quality we want to understand.

The research question is not how to engineer every listener's attention. It is whether KLAAT's naturally emerging repetition supports a stable, personally meaningful listening experience — and under what conditions it does not. This is where science serves the art rather than supervising it.

23. RESEARCH DIRECTION AND PROVENANCE

The next major advance should be a modest longitudinal observation using authentic KLAAT recordings, not another invented theoretical dimension. If possible, keep the September 66-second reference as a calibration artifact and choose a longer, naturally performed take for ecological listening. Compare listeners' first and later encounters. Document what they actually heard, not what a theory predicts they should hear. A later study could examine acoustic features of the passages they spontaneously mention.

24. ONE OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-084, THE THIRD LISTEN

Choose one authentic, unedited KLAAT recording of roughly 8–15 minutes, preferably with both Amastacia and Ankora. No new composition, special phase structure, or technique change is needed. Invite three to six willing listeners who have not heard this exact take. Each person hears it on three separate days at comfortable volume, using approximately the same playback system. They may choose focused listening or a safe, ordinary low-demand activity, but should keep that context the same across the three hearings and note what it was. Do not use this as an experiment while driving or doing safety-critical work.

After each hearing, collect one spontaneous sentence: 'What stayed with you?' Then ask for simple separate 1–7 ratings of enjoyment, absorption, felt energy, and desire to hear it again. Ask whether anything felt distracting. Do not reveal Paul's favorite passages or the research hypothesis. If a participant stops early, record that rather than treating it as missing data. Compare each person's day-one and day-three pattern; report individuals rather than manufacturing a group conclusion from a tiny sample.

This pilot cannot isolate familiarity from mood, expectations, day-to-day variation, or the act of repeated questioning. It cannot prove a neurological mechanism. But it can reveal whether KLAAT becomes more familiar and compelling, less engaging, or simply different over a few real encounters — without changing one note of the music.
