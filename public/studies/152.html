THE DRONE THE HEARING AID MAY REWRITE

THE DRONE THE HEARING AID MAY REWRITE Adaptive feedback cancellation, tonal music, and the listener-side signal chain

Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH QUESTION

Amastacia produces a sustained, breath-shaped C# drone with moving overtones; Ankora supplies attacks, decays and tactile pulse. What reaches a listener's ears is not always the same waveform that leaves the instruments or the loudspeaker. In particular, some hearing aids use adaptive acoustic-feedback cancellation to prevent microphone–receiver feedback. Can these systems mistakenly treat sustained musical components as feedback, or otherwise alter their reproduction? If so, how should KLAAT distinguish the original performance from the listening apparatus without excluding hearing-aid wearers or demanding that they change their devices?

THE PHYSICAL PROBLEM

A hearing aid microphone captures external sound. Its processor applies frequency-dependent amplification and other algorithms; its receiver sends sound into the ear canal. Some of that output can leak back toward the microphone, forming an acoustic feedback loop. At sufficient loop gain and suitable phase, the loop may produce a whistle or tonal instability. Feedback management can reduce loop gain, estimate and subtract the feedback component, or change other processing parameters. These strategies are not interchangeable.

An adaptive feedback canceller estimates the acoustic return path and subtracts a predicted feedback signal from the microphone input. The estimation problem is difficult because the wanted input—especially sustained tonal music—can be correlated with the receiver output. A system may then misattribute part of a legitimate musical tone to feedback. This is a general signal-processing vulnerability, not proof that any particular hearing aid corrupts KLAAT.

Why Amastacia is a relevant probe: its drone can maintain relatively stable harmonic components while breath articulation moves spectral emphasis and loudness. A listener could hear the same recording through an unprocessed speaker, a phone speaker, headphones, or a hearing aid with a chosen program. The resulting acoustic signal at the eardrum can differ even if the source file is identical. Ankora's short attacks pose a different challenge: compression, transient management, and microphone placement may alter their clarity, but those changes should not automatically be attributed to feedback cancellation.

WHAT THE RESEARCH ACTUALLY SHOWS

Spriet, Moonen and Wouters (2010) compared six acoustic-feedback reduction implementations in hearing-aid-related tests. Their reported additional stable gain varied from approximately 3 to 26 dB across tested approaches and conditions. Five of six implementations performed worse on a tonal opera stimulus than on speech under the authors' evaluation conditions. The precise definition of 'worse' and the underlying implementation matter: it is not a universal percentage of musical information lost, and 3–26 dB is additional stable gain, not measured loss of musical loudness. Opera is not Amastacia; the study demonstrates a mechanism and a context dependence, not a KLAAT effect.

Zheng and colleagues (2023) investigated a combined deep-learning and adaptive-feedback cancellation method and reported more than 13 dB additional stable gain in their matched evaluation conditions. This counterbalances any simplistic claim that feedback cancellation must always damage sustained music: algorithm design, training conditions, signal type, acoustic fit and evaluation criterion all matter. A larger stable-gain figure alone cannot establish better subjective musical fidelity.

Arehart, Kates and Anderson (2011) assessed perceived music quality with 19 normal-hearing and 15 hearing-impaired listeners in their experimental sample. Their results indicate that nonlinear processing and noise can have greater effects on perceived quality than linear filtering for the materials and conditions tested. This broadens the caution: if a listener reports a thinner drone or less crisp pulse, feedback cancellation is only one candidate explanation among compression, frequency response, noise, fitting, hearing characteristics and playback hardware.

These are primary studies in other listening systems. None measured Amastacia, Ankora, KLAAT listener absorption, trance, autonomic activity or neurological entrainment.

FOUR DISTINCT EVIDENCE LEVELS

1. Acoustically measured: the original recording's spectrum, its sound-pressure level in a controlled playback setup, and—where specialized equipment is available—the processed output at or near an ear simulator. A recording of the room alone is not the same as hearing-aid output at the eardrum.

2. Subjectively reported: a consenting listener's account of drone solidity, tonal instability, pulse clarity, fatigue, pleasure, activation or absorption. Such reports are real observations but do not identify their own causes.

3. Physiological or neural: autonomic measures, motor activity or brain recordings, if independently collected under appropriate protocols. They cannot be inferred from a hearing-aid program name, a spectrogram or an absorption score.

4. Causal hypothesis: a particular adaptive canceller altered the musical input, which in turn changed a listener's experience. Demonstrating that chain would require controlled comparisons, knowledge of device settings, and careful exclusion of alternative processing explanations.

CONTRADICTIONS AND LIMITS

A feedback canceller may improve the usable sound by preventing whistling; turning it off may be worse. Modern devices differ widely. Some provide music-oriented settings, but a label such as 'music' does not guarantee neutral frequency response or inactive feedback management. Listener hearing differences also affect perception independently of electronics. An apparent sustained pitch change might come from the source, room, playback speaker, fitting, feedback processing, or a subjective interpretation. The available research does not rank those explanations for KLAAT.

There is an ethical boundary: a research exercise should never pressure someone to remove hearing aids, disable clinically configured safety or comfort features, or listen at uncomfortable levels. Device programs should only be compared if the listener already has suitable programs and, where needed, a hearing-care professional supports the comparison. Participation and disclosure of assistive-device information should be voluntary.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE ART

KLAAT's acoustic minimalism is not a deficiency to correct. It is exactly what makes listener-side processing an interesting scientific question: a sustained harmonic texture can expose algorithmic behavior that busy, rapidly changing music might obscure. That does not mean Paul should insert artificial pitch sweeps, compose test tones, or modify the improvisation to satisfy a laboratory protocol. The proper research object remains an authentic recording of the sound he already makes.

The same recording may be an energizing daily-life soundtrack for one listener, an absorbing low-frequency environment for another, and a technically compromised playback experience for a third. Treating those outcomes as distinct is more useful than assuming that every person received an identical stimulus. It also prevents us from mislabeling a device-induced tonal artifact as an intentional breath articulation or as a mystical resonance effect.

A PRACTICAL LISTENER-RESEARCH DESIGN

For an eventual small KLAAT listening pilot, retain the unedited source recording and a version identifier. Ask only optional, non-identifying questions about playback path: speaker, headphones, hearing aids or other assistance, and whether the listener used their usual comfortable setting. Record perceived experience separately from equipment details. Do not pool an assistive-device comparison into a general claim about listeners unless there is enough evidence to model those differences. For any within-person comparison, use the same original excerpt, comparable comfortable playback levels, randomized presentation order where feasible, and a clearly stated distinction between ordinary listening and a technical demonstration. A few observations would generate hypotheses, not establish population effects.

An objective measurement could use a controlled test fixture or probe-microphone arrangement under professional supervision, comparing aided output across permitted settings. Even then, ear-canal acoustics, individual fitting, device adaptation time and processing history must be documented. Spectral differences alone cannot tell us whether a listener felt more absorbed or energized.

ESTABLISHED / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT / HYPOTHETICAL / UNSUPPORTED

Established: acoustic feedback loops can arise in hearing aids; adaptive cancellation can increase stable gain; sustained tonal inputs can challenge some feedback-management implementations; the playback signal chain can change the delivered sound.

Context-dependent: the magnitude and audibility of artifacts depend on device, fitting, program, signal and test method. The primary studies cited here report effects under their own conditions, not universal constants.

Plausible and testable for KLAAT: particular hearing-aid programs might alter perceived Amastacia harmonic stability or Ankora attack detail; these differences might influence reported absorption, activation or fatigue.

Speculation: a particular harmonic pattern in KLAAT could systematically trigger a specific adaptive canceller. No relevant device recording has yet been examined.

Unsupported: KLAAT has demonstrated hearing-aid-specific trance, neural synchronization, therapeutic benefit, or a unique physiological effect of C#. None of those conclusions follows from the cited studies.

DURABLE RESEARCH FINDING

Playback is an active transformation, not a transparent pipe. For KLAAT listener research, hearing-aid feedback management is a separate potential confound from the source instrument, live PA feedback suppression, microphone capture, frequency-weighted SPL, and the listener's own hearing. Preserve original audio, describe the delivery path when volunteered, and never equate subjective difference with proven neural mechanism.

OPTIONAL LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION

When a willing listener who normally uses hearing aids hears an existing KLAAT recording at their usual comfortable setting, ask one open question: 'Did any part of the sustained sound seem to waver, whistle, thin out, or remain especially solid?' Record their own words without suggesting that a device malfunction occurred. No settings need to change, and no performance changes are requested.
