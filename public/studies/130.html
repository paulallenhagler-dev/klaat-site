THE EFFORT THAT IS NOT YET FATIGUE

Why a long improvisation can feel demanding without measurable muscle failure—and why the opposite can also occur

SESSION 130 / 164Body & PerformanceRESEARCH SESSION

GOVERNING PRINCIPLE Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH STATUS A critical synthesis of human experimental studies involving wind players, drummers and pianists. No KLAAT-specific endurance, electromyography, breathing, injury, recording, listener, autonomic or neurological data have been collected. This is not a prescription for how long Paul must play, when he must rest, how rapidly Ankora must strike, or how an improvisation should unfold.

EDITORIAL ABSTRACT KLAAT's future longer performances raise a practical problem: Paul may remain artistically absorbed while the mouth, respiratory apparatus, striking hand, supporting arm and attention are carrying different loads. The familiar word 'fatigue' can obscure at least four things: felt effort, an objectively reduced capacity to produce force or execute a movement, a change in audible performance, and pain or tissue symptoms. These outcomes need not occur together. In one controlled study, 14 pianists played pieces chosen to evoke different levels of exertion, yet investigators found no significant decline in the muscle or force measures they collected. In another, 49 pianists showed task-duration-related neuromotor changes, with different variability patterns among those who could sustain the tasks longer. Wind-player research found no clear post-session masseter-fatigue signal after 90 minutes, even while other oral muscles were more active. A study of 22 drummers found lower antagonist wrist-muscle co-contraction in experts and more even performance. None of these experiments studied an agave didgeridoo and elk-hide frame drum played together. The emerging insight is not 'fatigue is imaginary' or 'effort is harmless.' It is that no single feeling, waveform, BPM estimate or muscle measurement tells the whole story.

1. THE NEW QUESTION

Paul has described an immediate constraint on KLAAT: not a lack of musical ideas, but the difficulty of sustaining an extended simultaneous Amastacia–Ankora performance without additional assistance. The obvious engineering answer is to investigate amplification and looping; Sessions 128 and 129 did that. But the human constraint deserves its own measurement vocabulary. When a phrase becomes difficult after several minutes, is the limiting factor the lips, cheek-pressure handoff, respiratory effort, hand movement, frame support, attentional load, or a combination? Can a performer feel strained without a demonstrable fall in motor capacity? Could a measurable change occur while the performer still feels immersed and effortless?

This is a DIFFERENT problem from Session 120's question about improvement after rest and sleep. It is also different from Session 124's internal-versus-external attentional focus, Session 116's breath–hand timing, and the October 18 hourly notes on injury prevalence and orofacial load. Those works ask about learning, attention, coordination or risk. This session examines the contested inference from felt effort to acute physiological fatigability and from laboratory fatigability to the quality of a living improvisation. It synthesizes new primary studies not used as the center of those earlier publications. The three hourly notes after Session 129 instead concerned microphone splitting, pre-listening genre labels and outdoor stand stability; none duplicates this question. No prior published Session 130 was present in the checked Corpus Volume II, Curriculum Index or Library archive.

2. FIVE DIFFERENT THINGS OFTEN CALLED FATIGUE

First, perceived exertion is the performer's report that an activity feels demanding. A person may report effort because of concentration, breath resistance, emotional intensity, local discomfort, unfamiliarity or a genuinely increasing physical load. It is an important subjective observation, not a muscle biopsy.

Second, performance fatigability means a measurable decline in a specified capability during or after a task: for example, reduced maximum voluntary force, diminished endurance, slower execution or degraded precision under otherwise comparable conditions. A null result on a particular test does not establish that every muscle is unchanged.

Third, neuromuscular change is an observed change in activation, coordination, muscle-electrical spectral features or movement variability. Some changes reflect compensation or strategy, not damage or lost capacity.

Fourth, acoustic drift is a change in the sound: attack-to-attack variation, a new harmonic emphasis, a noisier breath handoff, quieter strikes, or a different balance between Amastacia and Ankora. In KLAAT, some such changes may be intentional, artistically welcome, or produced by room/microphone processing rather than fatigue.

Fifth, pain or persistent symptoms are a separate safety domain. Neither a low exertion rating nor a normal EMG result guarantees that repetitive exposure is safe. Conversely, exertion alone does not diagnose an injury.

The research mistake is to draw a straight line from 'I feel tired' to 'the lip muscles are failing' to 'the listener hears weaker music.' Each arrow needs evidence.

3. THE FIRST CONTRADICTION: HARD PLAYING WITHOUT DETECTED MUSCLE FATIGABILITY

McCrary and colleagues (2023) tested 14 high-level university pianists. Each performed three self-selected pieces, ten minutes each, in randomized order, chosen to represent perceived exertion levels of approximately 9, 13 and 17 on a 20-point scale. The investigators assessed maximum voluntary grip and shoulder strength and surface EMG from upper trapezius and forearm flexor/extensor muscles at baseline, after performances and following ten minutes of quiet sitting.

Despite the deliberately different perceived task intensities, they did not detect significant changes in the studied EMG amplitudes or median EMG frequencies during the repertoire performances or maximum-force tests (reported F <= 1.72, p > .09), or in force/EMG measures during the maximum-force tests (F <= 1.19, p > .31). This is a concrete null result, not proof that musicians never fatigue. Fourteen pianists, selected tasks, particular muscles and tests, and 30 total minutes of repertoire cannot establish the condition of the lips, cheeks, respiratory system or hand holding an elk-hide frame drum. A nonsignificant result is also not proof of equivalence; confidence intervals and test sensitivity matter.

What the experiment does establish is that a demanding musical task and measurable local muscle-performance fatigability are not synonyms. That distinction protects KLAAT from a misleading narrative in either direction. Feeling intense effort does not necessarily mean technique has failed. Feeling 'in the zone' does not necessarily mean the body is not under load.

4. THE SECOND CONTRADICTION: ENDURANCE CAN DIFFER WITH MOTOR STRATEGY

Goubault and colleagues (2023) examined 49 pianists performing two fatiguing piano tasks. Participants were classified into shorter- and longer-duration groups according to how long they could continue the tasks. Investigators measured muscle activation and movement with EMG and inertial sensors. With task time, EMG activation variability in many shoulder and upper-limb muscles increased more in the shorter-duration group. Acceleration variability also increased more in selected segments for that group, although other movement measures showed greater variability in the longer-duration group.

The authors interpreted these results as evidence that different neuromotor strategies partly explain differences in endurance, and that some kinematic variability may be protective in the tested tasks. But grouping people by how long they lasted and then comparing their motor patterns does not prove that teaching a particular amount of movement variability will increase endurance. The findings are associations in constrained piano tasks. Importantly, 'more variability' is not universally good or bad: its anatomical location, timing, task and function matter.

For Amastacia and Ankora, this creates a testable possibility rather than a technique command. Paul's body may naturally redistribute effort among lips, cheeks, shoulders, wrist, fingers, stance and breathing as an improvisation develops. A visible change in gesture may be adaptation, expression, fatigue or all three. The aim is to become more fluent and capable of choosing sounds—not to freeze a preferred 'efficient' posture or enforce a mathematically regular strike pattern.

5. WIND-PLAYER EVIDENCE: THE MUSCLE YOU MEASURE DETERMINES THE ANSWER

Gotouda and colleagues (2007) measured EMG in 19 brass and 14 woodwind players under different tone conditions. In a sustained-playing subset, nine brass and nine woodwind musicians played for 90 minutes; investigators compared masseter-muscle EMG spectral measures before and after. The mean power frequency did not decrease after the session in either instrument group. Jaw-closing muscle activity during playing was small compared with maximum clenching, while orbicularis oris and digastric activity was relatively larger. High-tone conditions increased the measured activity of all examined muscles in the brass group, but not significantly in the woodwind group.

This study is a warning against a lazy physiological inference. A 90-minute session did not produce the expected masseter spectral change in these players. That does NOT show that the lips were unfatigued, that breathing was effortless, that no musician experienced soreness, or that didgeridoo circular breathing has the same demands. A study designed around the jaw-closing muscles cannot settle an endurance question about lip vibration, oral-air transfer or a frame drum held for an extended time.

A separate comparison by Potter and colleagues (2015) tested 16 trumpet players and 16 matched healthy controls, with an additional single case. Trumpet players had greater cheek strength and lip endurance, but not superior tongue strength or endurance on the same apparatus. Even among related facial structures, expertise-associated differences were selective. This is cross-sectional evidence: the study does not prove playing caused the differences, and an instrument-specific lip endurance test is not an Amastacia test.

An older experiment by White and Basmajian (1974) measured four facial muscles in 18 trumpeters across 51 performance tasks and found patterns depending on proficiency, register and task. This supports a broad point: the face is not one homogeneous 'embouchure muscle.' But trumpet mouthpiece mechanics are not interchangeable with a didgeridoo bore or Paul's particular mouth position.

6. ANKORA: A WRIST CAN BE ACTIVE WITHOUT BEING ECONOMICAL

Beveridge and colleagues (2020) compared eleven expert with eleven amateur drummers across five repetitive one- and two-hand tasks at different tempos. Motion capture and wrist flexor/extensor EMG showed that experts had lower antagonist co-contraction across tasks and tempos, and that lower co-contraction was associated with more even performance. Co-contraction increased at very slow and very fast tempos in the studied task conditions. Experts also showed flexor-dominant patterns consistent with a different use of rebound.

These findings suggest that muscular economy can be relevant to temporal precision. But they do not show that the ideal Ankora technique is 'always relax the wrist,' that faster playing is inherently unsafe, or that Paul's actual striking and supporting motions match a drum-kit task. A frame drum may be held by one hand while the other strikes; that supporting-arm load is not captured by a wrist-only analysis of repetitive stick drumming. The investigators measured differences associated with expertise, not a randomized training intervention proving that deliberately reducing co-contraction improves all musicians.

For KLAAT, this opens a useful distinction: strike production, instrument support and breath production may have different bottlenecks. A looper could relieve the need to maintain one sound continuously, but it would not automatically relieve the static load of holding Ankora or the muscular work of playing a new live phrase. The equipment is an artistic extension, not a physiological cure.

7. WHAT THE FOUR STUDIES DO—AND DO NOT—CONTRADICT

At first glance the pianist studies disagree: one found no measurable acute muscle fatigability, another identified endurance-linked neuromotor changes. They need not be mutually exclusive. The tasks, durations, endpoints, groups and statistical questions differ. The first tested whether selected measures changed after relatively short repertoire performances; the second deliberately used fatiguing tasks and compared groups defined by endurance. 'No detectable decline in selected tests' and 'different patterns during tasks taken toward exhaustion' can both be true.

Likewise, the wind-player study's negative masseter result and the drummer study's efficiency differences address different structures. Neither can be turned into a general claim that music is nonfatiguing or that an EMG pattern predicts artistic intensity. These studies are most useful together because they expose the weakness of a single-metric account.

The apparent contradiction matters to KLAAT's identity. The intensity Paul feels in a deep improvised flow state is real as subjective experience. Whether it corresponds to increased muscle recruitment, lost force capacity, changed acoustic output or listener activation is an empirical question, not a metaphor we should quietly convert into a mechanism.

8. THE PROBLEM OF THE UNREPEATABLE PERFORMANCE

A controlled fatigue test often repeats a fixed task before and after sustained effort. KLAAT does not need to repeat an identical score. It is possible to investigate endurance without making spontaneous music conform to laboratory composition rules.

One route is to preserve uninterrupted recordings of naturally occurring performances and describe their time course without calling every change 'fatigue.' If the amplitude of Amastacia's upper harmonics shifts after ten minutes, possible causes include articulation, mouth shape, microphone movement, recording automatic gain control, room orientation, expressive intention and effort. If Ankora's attacks become less evenly spaced, possible causes include chosen rhythmic complexity, deliberate timing, detector errors, a changing perceived beat or fatigue. Acoustic measurement alone cannot choose among them.

A different route, only if Paul wants to understand one particular skill, is a brief optional capability check outside performance: for example, whether a comfortable circular-breathing handoff remains available before and after an ordinary improvisation. Even that is not a diagnosis. A repeated check can itself become easier with practice or different because of motivation and learning. An 'after' comparison without a matched reference day cannot isolate the effects of the performance.

The study therefore favors converging evidence, not an invasive protocol: the original audio, a simple contemporaneous note of effort and comfort, approximate duration, any intended change in technique, and—if genuinely needed—one separately defined motor skill measure. This is observation, not a score.

9. THE LISTENER IS A DIFFERENT SYSTEM

The performer may feel facial load, breathing resistance, hand impacts and frame weight. A passive listener receives airborne sound and, depending on the environment, some physical vibration. These are not the same bodily inputs. A performer can feel intensely engaged while a listener reports boredom; a listener can feel energized by a recording made during a relatively easy performance. No contradiction exists because the outcomes are different.

The same distinction applies to daily-life use. A KLAAT recording can function as an energizing soundtrack even when it is not the longest or most demanding take. Conversely, the most physically strenuous performance is not necessarily the most compelling listening experience. Listener absorption, urge to move, enjoyment, emotional tone, perceived vibration and time alteration should be asked about separately and without suggesting the expected answer. None can be read directly from Paul's muscle state.

A long-form KLAAT work may someday sustain both a ritual setting and ordinary activities. Research should test those contexts with authentic recordings rather than assuming that more minutes, more effort, or greater drum density produces a stronger effect.

10. EVIDENCE TIERS AND CAUSAL BOUNDARIES

ESTABLISHED IN NON-KLAAT HUMAN STUDIES — A controlled study of 14 pianists found no statistically detectable changes in the selected acute EMG and maximum-force fatigability measures despite different repertoire exertion categories. — A 49-pianist study found time-dependent motor/EMG variability patterns associated with shorter versus longer endurance in deliberately fatiguing tasks. — A wind-player study found no decline in its masseter EMG mean-power-frequency marker after 90 minutes in a subset of 18 players, despite measurable activity differences among oral muscles and tone conditions. — A 22-drummer study found expertise-related wrist co-contraction differences associated with temporal evenness. — A 16-versus-16 comparison found selective differences in facial strength/endurance among trumpet players and controls.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — EMG amplitude, EMG median/mean frequency, maximum voluntary force, task duration, precision and subjective effort measure different aspects of a performance. Absence of a statistically significant change in one is not proof that all other capacities were preserved. — Motor variability may reflect compensation, efficient flexibility, a chosen gesture or developing difficulty. Its direction is not universally beneficial. — Findings from piano, trumpet, brass/woodwind and drum-kit tasks cannot be directly transferred to an agave didgeridoo played with a hand-held frame drum. — Microphone processing and changing improvisational choices complicate inference from a recorded waveform to muscular condition.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT — The first bottleneck in a longer session may differ from day to day and among lip continuity, respiratory work, striking-hand control, supporting-arm load and attention. — Greater circular-breathing fluency could reduce perceived effort without changing the intended sound, or allow more expressive choice at the same effort. — Some acoustic changes over duration may track growing exertion, but others may be expressive, contextual or technical artifacts. — Looping or amplification may change how effort is distributed, without guaranteeing longer safe endurance or stronger listener effects.

SPECULATIVE — Changes in motor economy may allow longer spontaneous immersion without an increased sense of effort; whether this happens for Paul is unknown. — Listeners might prefer a later, rougher-sounding passage that a performer personally experienced as more demanding. That would be an aesthetic preference, not a validated physiological benefit.

UNSUPPORTED — KLAAT has demonstrated fatigue-resistant performance, a specific muscular injury, improved endurance, physiological entrainment, trance induction, therapeutic benefit, or neurological activation. — Paul's deep flow proves his muscles are not tiring; a tired feeling proves objective muscle failure; no pain proves safety; a normal EMG result proves there is no cumulative load. — The C# drone, a 137/184-BPM reference, a particular striking density, or a loop pedal determines an optimal session length. — A listener's 'felt the music in my body' report measures the performer's muscle activation or a specific airborne bass dose.

11. WHAT WOULD COUNT AS A GENUINE NEXT DISCOVERY

A valuable KLAAT-specific observation would identify a repeatable distinction between what Paul feels, what the audio contains, and what he can comfortably execute—without assuming these are identical. For example, if effort rises over a naturally occurring session while the audible drone remains continuous, that is a descriptive dissociation. It is not proof that muscular capacity was preserved. If the sound changes while effort remains low, the difference may be expressive rather than fatigue-driven. If discomfort persists or technique suddenly becomes unreliable, safety and recovery take precedence over documenting an 'effect.'

The more ambitious future experiment would compare multiple authentic takes across days, with comparable recording paths and predeclared outcomes, while allowing different musical content. That would establish within-person descriptive trajectories, not a universal KLAAT endurance law. The ability to remain spontaneous is not a nuisance variable to eliminate; it is the art whose constraints we are trying to understand.

12. CONCLUSION — DO NOT MISTAKE THE BODY'S REPORT FOR THE WHOLE BODY

KLAAT begins with breath, hand, hide, agave and an improvising person. It is possible for the performer to experience a demanding session without an easily measurable decline in the particular muscle capacities a study happens to test. It is also possible for physical work and coordination to change before the performer notices them. The published evidence does not license an endurance prescription, but it offers a more useful language: effort, capacity, motor strategy, audible change, symptoms and listener response are separate channels of evidence.

That separation is liberating rather than restrictive. Technique work can make more musical choices available; future equipment can support longer uninterrupted sound; the performance itself remains Paul's. Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-130: THE FIRST LIMITING FACTOR

On one ordinary, unscored improvisation that you were already going to play, make no change to the sound or duration for research purposes. After stopping naturally, write only one sentence: 'What first made continuing less comfortable or less fluid—lips/cheek handoff, breath, striking hand, arm holding Ankora, attention, something else, or nothing?' If the answer is 'nothing,' record that. Optionally note approximate elapsed time and whether the stop was simply an artistic choice. Do not push through pain or intentionally play to exhaustion. This is a personal observation, not a clinical assessment or proof of a mechanism.
