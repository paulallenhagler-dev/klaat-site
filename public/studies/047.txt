SESSION 47 — SOUND EXPOSURE & HEARING CONSERVATION: INTENSITY IS A DOSE, NOT A MOMENT
September 29, 2026


CORE QUESTION
KLAAT is activation-oriented. It uses sustained drone, repeated impact, roughness, tactile coupling, room reinforcement, and long duration. Before treating greater acoustic intensity as another compositional control, the project needs a safety model: what matters for hearing risk, what should actually be measured, and how can KLAAT preserve physical impact without confusing “stronger” with “better”?


CORE IDEA
A sound level at one instant is not the same thing as sound exposure. Hearing risk depends strongly on level × duration × repetition, with individual susceptibility and recovery conditions adding uncertainty. For KLAAT, the useful question is not merely “How loud was the peak?” or “Did it feel intense?” but “What acoustic dose reached important listener and performer positions over the whole performance?”


WHY DECIBELS ARE DECEPTIVE
The decibel scale is logarithmic. A 3 dB increase represents roughly twice the acoustic energy under comparable conditions, even though it does not sound “twice as loud.” This is why exposure guidance often uses an equal-energy principle: if level rises, allowable duration falls rapidly.


NIOSH’s occupational Recommended Exposure Limit is 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours, with a 3 dB exchange rate. Under that model, 88 dBA corresponds to 4 hours, 91 dBA to 2 hours, 94 dBA to 1 hour, 97 dBA to 30 minutes, and 100 dBA to 15 minutes for the same nominal daily dose. This is an occupational framework, not a universal recreational safe/unsafe boundary, but it demonstrates cumulative exposure clearly.


For KLAAT, a 90-minute performance at a stable high level is therefore a fundamentally different exposure from a few brief high-level Threshold Gate or Rising Pulse events surrounded by quieter passages.


LEVEL IS NOT DOSE
Three quantities must stay separate.


Instantaneous or short-term sound level asks how strong the acoustic pressure is now.


Equivalent continuous level, LAeq,T, asks what constant A-weighted sound level would contain the same acoustic energy as this varying sound over time T.


Dose asks how much of a defined exposure allowance has accumulated over a period under a chosen standard or model.


A performance can contain dramatic peaks while maintaining a lower overall equivalent level, or feel subjectively steady while accumulating a large dose through duration. KLAAT already has phases, so we can eventually measure exposure by phase instead of treating a 60–90 minute ritual as one undifferentiated number.


A-WEIGHTING IS USEFUL, BUT NOT A COMPLETE DESCRIPTION
A-weighted decibels are widely used for hearing-risk and exposure assessment. But KLAAT has an unusual low-frequency and tactile emphasis. A-weighting attenuates low-frequency contributions strongly. LAeq is therefore useful for hearing-conservation decisions but cannot tell us how physically powerful the Drone or structure-borne component feels.


This produces a permanent methodological rule:


HEARING EXPOSURE ≠ TACTILE INTENSITY.


KLAAT should eventually record at least two measurement families: calibrated airborne sound exposure for hearing conservation, and mechanical vibration measurements for floor/seat/body coupling. Collapsing both into one “intensity” number would erase the distinction Session 46 established.


PEAKS AND AVERAGES ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS
Ankora is transient-rich. Its strikes can have sharp peaks while contributing less total acoustic energy than a sustained source at a similar peak level. Amastacia is almost the inverse: the Drone can sustain energy continuously even when it lacks dramatic transient peaks.


Ankora therefore demands attention to transient and peak behavior. Amastacia demands attention to duration and accumulated equivalent level. Together they create a mixed exposure that cannot be characterized honestly by one maximum reading. A phone screen flashing “102 dB” once tells us very little about the total KLAAT exposure.


RECREATIONAL MUSIC GUIDANCE IS NOT IDENTICAL TO OCCUPATIONAL GUIDANCE
WHO’s Global Standard for Safe Listening Venues and Events recommends that the 15-minute moving-average level not exceed 100 dB LAeq,15min at a defined reference measurement position while an audience is present. WHO also recommends continuous monitoring, optimized acoustics/sound systems, hearing protection, quiet zones, and trained staff.


That 100 dB value must not become a KLAAT target. It is an upper venue/event limit intended to reduce risk while preserving artistic practice, and it does not eliminate all individual risk. A quieter KLAAT performance can still be powerful, and repeated exposures matter.


For performers, the issue is even more important. Paul may receive close-range exposure at every rehearsal and performance. A one-time audience member and the performer do not have the same cumulative exposure history.


THE PERFORMER POSITION MAY BE THE HIGHEST-PRIORITY MEASUREMENT POINT
Earlier sessions established that performer and listener experience differ because the performer also receives bone conduction, direct instrument contact, respiratory feedback, and a different spatial field. Hearing conservation adds another asymmetry: the performer repeats the exposure.


The first formal KLAAT sound map should therefore include at minimum: Paul’s ear position while playing Amastacia; Paul’s ear position while playing Ankora; the nearest plausible audience position; a representative central audience position; and a farther audience position.


ROOM ACOUSTICS CAN CHANGE EXPOSURE WITHOUT CHANGING PERFORMANCE EFFORT
A reflective room returns more acoustic energy to the listener. Room modes create spatial differences. A performer can play with the same effort in two rooms while audience-position levels differ.


This connects hearing conservation directly to the existing principle “the room is an instrument.” The room is also part of the exposure system. A venue map should eventually include calibrated level measurements alongside resonance hotspots and perceptual ratings.


KLAAT SHOULD NOT USE LOUDNESS AS ITS MAIN ACTIVATION ENGINE
This is the major compositional consequence. Earlier sessions already give KLAAT many non-level controls: roughness, adaptation, omission, prediction error, attack shape, stream fusion, reverberant accumulation, temporal density and contrast.


A sophisticated Rising Pulse should be capable of feeling more urgent while measured level changes little. If temporal density, attack clarity, spectral roughness, expectation and tactile coupling can do that work, KLAAT gains perceived intensity without spending as much acoustic exposure budget.


New design principle:


COMPOSE PERCEIVED INTENSITY; MANAGE ACOUSTIC DOSE.


EVIDENCE BOUNDARY
ESTABLISHED — Noise-induced hearing injury risk depends strongly on sound level and duration; repeated exposure matters.


ESTABLISHED — NIOSH uses 85 dBA over 8 hours with a 3 dB exchange rate for its occupational REL.


ESTABLISHED — LAeq represents an energy-equivalent continuous level over a specified interval.


ESTABLISHED — WHO recommends a maximum 100 dB LAeq,15min moving average at the reference position for amplified-music venues/events, together with monitoring and other risk-reduction measures.


ESTABLISHED — A-weighting is useful for standardized hearing-exposure assessment but does not describe vibrotactile intensity or structure-borne vibration.


SUPPORTED BUT CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — Sound exposure can vary substantially by listener position, room, source geometry, and performance configuration.


PLAUSIBLE / TESTABLE — KLAAT can produce large increases in perceived activation with relatively small changes in LAeq by manipulating contrast, prediction, roughness, temporal density and tactile coupling.


PLAUSIBLE / TESTABLE — The performer’s cumulative exposure may be a more important long-term hearing-conservation concern than the exposure of an occasional audience member.


UNKNOWN — Typical LAeq, phase-specific exposure, and peak levels for the present Amastacia + Ankora configuration have not yet been measured with calibrated equipment.


UNSUPPORTED / AVOID — “If it doesn’t hurt, it is safe.” Auditory discomfort is not a calibrated exposure meter.


UNSUPPORTED / AVOID — “Low frequencies are safe because they are felt more than heard.” Felt vibration and hearing risk are different questions.


UNSUPPORTED / AVOID — Treating 100 dBA as a recommended KLAAT operating level. It is not.


ARCHITECTURE IMPLICATIONS
Ground Anchor: establish the field without unnecessary acoustic dose. Stability, spectral detail and room interaction should do more work than raw level.


Threshold Gate: use contrast. A brief change can feel large because the baseline established expectation; the Gate does not need to be a large SPL jump.


Rising Pulse: increase activation primarily through density, onset definition, roughness, prediction and controlled tactile coupling. Level can rise when artistically justified, but it should be measured rather than guessed.


Return Path: subtraction reduces perceptual load and accumulated exposure while preserving the project’s activation-oriented identity.


KLAAT METHODOLOGY UPDATE
No future claim that a KLAAT configuration is safe or suitable for a given duration should be made from subjective loudness or an uncalibrated recording. Exposure claims require appropriate measurement.


PRACTICAL EXPERIMENT — KRX-047: FIRST EXPOSURE MAP
Use a calibrated Class 1 or Class 2 sound level meter if available. A validated phone measurement can be used for exploratory mapping, but not as the final safety instrument.


Run a representative 12-minute KLAAT passage: 3 min Ground Anchor; 2 min Threshold Gate; 4 min Rising Pulse; 3 min Return Path.


At a fixed representative audience position, record LAeq for the complete 12 minutes; phase-specific LAeq if logging permits; maximum level using a documented time weighting; distance and angle from Amastacia and Ankora; and room/floor type. Repeat at the performer-ear position and nearest intended audience position.


Do not change the performance because a number “looks too low.” The first goal is descriptive: find out what KLAAT currently is. Then compare the measurements with subjective ratings of activation and bodily intensity for each phase.


The critical question is not “How loud can KLAAT get?” It is: Can Rising Pulse produce a large increase in activation without a proportionately large increase in acoustic exposure?
