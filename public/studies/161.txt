THE SEAL THAT MOVES WITH THE MUSIC
Hearing protection, jaw articulation, and the difference between a static fit and a performing ear


GUIDING PRINCIPLE: Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


RESEARCH QUESTION
When a musician uses earplugs while articulating a didgeridoo, does a fit measured at rest necessarily describe attenuation throughout a spontaneous performance? This is a question about hearing protection and measurement validity, not a suggestion to alter Amastacia's sound or Paul's technique.


WHY THIS QUESTION NOW; NOVELTY GATE

Sessions 151–159 addressed sound-level weighting, hearing aids, rhythm analysis, background listening, spectral averages, intersample peaks, source separation, familiarity and selective attention. None of those addresses earplug seal stability during active jaw movement. A prior October 17 hourly note considered hearing-protector fit and self-sound occlusion generally. The November 8, 19:13 CST hourly note isolates a narrower unresolved question: whether a static attenuation test remains representative while the performer's jaw is moving. This is a genuine measurement-context extension, not evidence of a demonstrated problem in KLAAT. 1. WHAT IS PHYSICALLY AT STAKE
An earplug reduces some of the external sound transmitted through the ear canal. The achieved reduction depends on device design, insertion, individual ear geometry, frequency and fit. A small acoustic leak can matter, particularly when a device relies on a seal; however, its actual effect must be measured, not assumed. The ear canal and surrounding soft tissue can change shape during jaw movement. Whether those changes shift a particular plug enough to change its attenuation is an empirical question.


Amastacia demands ongoing mouth, jaw, lip and breathing coordination. That makes an active-playing condition more relevant than a photograph of an inserted plug or a single fit check taken while still. It does not mean Amastacia is unusually hazardous, that Paul's plugs leak, or that jaw motion always changes fit. Ankora adds short impulsive acoustic events; the appropriate hearing-risk assessment must consider actual exposure over time and peaks, not merely the impression of a loud drum.


2. THREE DIFFERENT PHENOMENA NOT TO CONFUSE
External attenuation: how much airborne sound from the room is reduced at the eardrum. This is the principal protective quantity.
Occlusion: changes in how the musician hears their own internally conducted voice, breath and bodily sounds when the canal is blocked. An apparent increase in internal bass does not prove poor external attenuation; it can occur with an effective seal.
Perceptual adaptation: the musician may alter force, phrasing or attention in response to how protected hearing sounds. This is behavior, not itself an attenuation measurement.


A listener's subjective report that the didgeridoo sounds 'more inside the head' cannot distinguish these mechanisms. Nor can a smartphone decibel app at the player's side measure the level behind the plug.


3. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE AND ITS LIMITS
Published musician-specific work by Crawford and colleagues (2023; International Journal of Audiology, 62, 238–244; DOI 10.1080/14992027.2022.2035831) evaluated hearing-protector effectiveness in musicians. The earlier KLAAT research note reports 24 musicians and measurements spanning 125–8,000 Hz, with substantial individual variability. This supports the need to verify personal protection rather than treating a package attenuation figure as a guaranteed result. It does NOT directly quantify how Amastacia jaw motion changes attenuation.


Research on the auditory occlusion effect, including Lee and Casali (2011; DOI 10.1177/1071181311551370), addresses changes to self-generated sound with ear-canal obstruction. Occlusion findings must not be repackaged as measurements of leakage or hearing protection. The two source leads above were inherited from the verified hourly research note; their detailed methods and numerical results were not independently re-extracted for this session. No direct controlled didgeridoo-specific dynamic earplug study was verified.


ESTABLISHED: individual protector fit matters; internal occlusion and external attenuation differ; an at-rest observation does not logically establish stability under an untested movement condition.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: jaw movement may affect canal geometry and plug position; measurable attenuation changes depend on person, device, insertion and movement.
PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE: one plug could temporarily lose attenuation during particular Amastacia articulations and reseat afterward, leaving identical before/after static tests.
SPECULATIVE: such transient changes could alter a player's monitoring or acoustic exposure in a way that affects improvisational choices. There is no KLAAT evidence of this.
UNSUPPORTED: Amastacia necessarily dislodges earplugs; a strong internal drone sensation means hearing damage; a specific protector type is universally best; KLAAT has demonstrated trance or neurological entrainment.


4. THE TIME-RESOLUTION PROBLEM
Suppose a qualified tester obtains frequency-specific attenuation A_before(f) and A_after(f) in decibels. Define Delta A(f) = A_after(f) − A_before(f). If Delta A is negative, the later test indicates less attenuation at that frequency. But if a leak opens briefly during playing and closes before the second test, Delta A may equal zero despite a real within-performance change. The before/after protocol measures persistence, not every transient.


A hypothetical 5 dB decrease in attenuation at one frequency would correspond to roughly 3.16 times as much transmitted sound intensity at that frequency, all else equal (10^(5/10)); it would not mean a 3.16-fold increase in perceived loudness or overall hearing risk. This is a mathematical illustration, NOT a measured earplug effect or KLAAT exposure estimate.


More temporally resolved measurement might use a professionally managed probe-microphone arrangement, but the probe itself could alter fit and introduce artifacts. An alternative is repeated standardized fit checks under safe movement conditions, with explicit acknowledgment that transient leaks remain unobserved. Measurement uncertainty, device repeatability and reinsertion must be recorded. A negative result is meaningful only for the tested person, protector and movement.


5. WHAT A USEFUL KLAAT TEST WOULD ACTUALLY COMPARE
The scientific question is not 'Does playing feel different with earplugs?' but 'Does measured protection remain stable during representative movement?' A careful professional protocol would hold the protector model, ear, insertion method and measurement system constant; document baseline fit; have the player perform natural, low-level jaw and lip movements and, if appropriate, a short authentic Amastacia passage at a safe level; then repeat the fit measurement. Multiple trials and both ears would help distinguish systematic changes from ordinary test noise. A recording of the movement timing, without imposing a musical score, could help identify the relevant articulation.


Even this design cannot establish continuous protection if the equipment measures only before and after. A different test design is required for that stronger claim. If a protector repeatedly loses fit, a qualified audiologist can assess sizing, insertion, alternative styles and actual exposure. Hearing protection should never be deliberately loosened or removed during potentially hazardous playing to generate a comparison.


6. WHY THIS BELONGS IN AN EXPERIENCE-LED RESEARCH PROGRAM
KLAAT's creative source remains the interaction of breath, drone, membrane and instinct. Science can protect the conditions under which that improvisation happens without dictating what should be played. A reliable monitoring environment may help preserve nuance and allow longer-term practice. It is equally possible that a well-fitted plug remains stable throughout a performance, in which case the question is resolved for that setup rather than becoming an artistic rule.


The same discipline applies to future listener studies: document the acoustic pathway before explaining the psychological response. A performer's internal perception, a microphone's recording, and a listener's playback are three different observation points. None alone proves absorption, autonomic change or trance.


UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS
Can controlled research directly quantify external attenuation changes during jaw opening, speech, chewing or wind-instrument articulation? Are effects concentrated in particular frequencies or protector designs? How much variation is due to the test itself? Does a change persist or occur only transiently? Can a safe, nonintrusive dynamic test distinguish leak from occlusion? No universal answer is asserted here.


OPTIONAL OBSERVATION
At the next routine hearing-protection consultation, ask whether the provider can check fit both at rest and after a short sequence of your ordinary Amastacia mouth movements. Note the protector model and whether the same ear was tested. No need to change a single note of KLAAT.
