THE BREATH THAT DOES NOT BREAK

Circular Breathing, Audible Continuity, and the Listener's Respiratory Independence

SESSION 082 / 164Drone & BreathRESEARCH SESSION

EDITORIAL ABSTRACT

Amastacia can sound continuous while the body producing her sound cycles through distinct acts of exhalation, oral-air delivery, and nasal inhalation. This is one of KLAAT's most unusual artistic facts: a sustained acoustic world is made by a breathing human being, but the listener cannot simply infer the performer's respiratory state from the presence of the drone. Nor does hearing that drone mean the listener's breathing follows Paul's. The next research step is to distinguish three clocks: the performer's respiratory cycle, the audible pattern of breath-shaped gestures, and the listener's own respiratory cycle. Evidence from cine MRI of wind musicians shows that circular breathing is a coordinated mechanical sequence, not breath-holding or an inexhaustible exhalation. Music-listening studies show that respiration can respond to musical structure, but the results differ across tasks and measurement definitions. KLAAT's strongest opportunity is not to force a listener to breathe in time; it is to learn how Paul's naturally occurring breath gestures, continuous tone, and Ankora's independent rhythmic pulse coexist, and whether any of those audible features predict attention, absorption, or energy. A low-pressure recording observation can begin without changing the performance.

1. WHY THIS SESSION FOLLOWS THE LAST TWO

Sessions 80 and 81 asked what survives when KLAAT travels from the room to a recording and then through a playback system. Low-frequency physical weight may be lost while Amastacia's harmonics, breath texture, and Ankora's attacks remain audible. That leaves a more intimate unresolved question: what, exactly, is the breath information a listener hears? A low drone can sound continuous even when the performer takes an inhalation. A rough or pulsing articulation may be produced by tongue and vocal-tract changes rather than a new respiratory cycle. And a listener may experience a breathing-like musical phrase without actually changing their own breathing. We need to stop treating 'breath' as one undifferentiated thing.

This topic extends, rather than replaces,

e program's earlier respiratory-entrainment work (Sessions 19 and 32). The advance here is practical: distinguish performer mechanics, audible sound, and passive-listener physiology, and link that distinction to technique and authentic recorded material. No new composition instructions are implied.

2. THE THREE BREATHS OF KLAAT

First is physiological breath: air movement into and out of Paul's lungs, together with the changing pressures and muscular coordination that permit sound production. Second is acoustic breath: the parts of Amastacia's output that listeners might recognize as a puff, hiss, harmonic shift, brief change of intensity, or breath-shaped phrase. Third is perceived or enacted listener breath: what a listener imagines, notices in the music, or actually does with their own respiratory cycle.

These are different observations. A continuous note is an acoustic observation, not evidence of continuous pulmonary exhalation. An audible sniff may reveal a moment of nasal inspiration, but a lack of audible sniff does not prove that no inspiration occurred. A listener describing the drone as 'breathing' is a meaningful subjective report, not a respiratory measurement. Even if two listeners' breathing rates rise together, that does not by itself show they are inhaling at the same instants.

3. HOW CIRCULAR BREATHING MAKES CONTINUITY POSSIBLE

In circular breathing, the player briefly uses air stored in the oral cavity to maintain outward airflow through the instrument while inhaling through the nose. The cheeks, tongue, soft palate, and oral cavity act as a coordinated, temporary pressure system. The lungs do not literally exhale and inhale simultaneously through the same pathway. Instead, the air source driving the instrument switches for a short interval. The musician then resumes ordinary pulmonary delivery without losing the tone.

This account is supported by direct imaging, not merely instrumental folklore. Peng, Phillips, Dyke, and Stewart used cine MRI with eight wind musicians—six professionals and two experienced amateurs, playing saxophone, trombone, or trumpet. They identified a consistent sequence of tongue and soft-palate movements and transient posterior oropharyngeal occlusion. The experienced amateurs had a significantly longer relative occlusion period than the professionals (P=.01), though the tiny, mixed-instrument sample cannot establish a universal proficiency benchmark. The authors did not image Amastacia, and the specific mouthpiece and resistance conditions differ from a didgeridoo. Nevertheless, the study demonstrates that circular breathing is a timed coordination skill involving multiple anatomical structures. [S1]

An independent ultrasound demonstration from a speech-neuroscience laboratory shows the cheeks and tongue helping expel oral air during a rapid nasal inspiration while sound is maintained. This is a useful visual explanation of the mechanism, not a controlled trial of didgeridoo training outcomes. [S2]

4. CONTINUITY IS NOT UNIFORMITY

The goal of circular breathing is often described as 'no break in the drone.' That is useful but incomplete. A successful transition between lung-driven and mouth-driven air can preserve sound while changing its envelope, pressure, or timbre. Such changes might be small enough to go unnoticed; they might also become part of the living texture of the performance. Whether a transition is perceptually smooth is a question about the delivered acoustic signal and the listener, not solely about the musician's technique.

A waveform can reveal brief level changes, and a spectrogram can reveal altered harmonic energy. But neither alone proves that a circular-breathing switch caused the change. Lip vibration, tongue position, instrument angle, room reflection, or Ankora's overlapping attack may be responsible. To identify a causal relation, the performer needs some independent way to mark the breath transition or a second observer needs synchronized video or airflow information. An audio-only analysis should label an event 'candidate breath transition' rather than 'confirmed nasal inhale.'

5. WHY TECHNIQUE MATTERS WITHOUT DICTATING THE MUSIC

For KLAAT, technical fluency is valuable because it can reduce the effort needed to keep Amastacia responsive during improvisation. That is different from requiring perfectly even tone. Paul may sometimes want a smooth uninterrupted Field; at other times, a gritty inhale-adjacent change or unstable harmonic may be exactly the sound that emerges naturally. Science should help distinguish an involuntary limitation from an expressive choice, not erase all traces of the body.

The most useful technical questions are specific: Can the nasal inhale be accomplished without an abrupt pressure collapse? Can the oral reservoir support the lips long enough for a comfortable breath? Does the tongue return smoothly enough to avoid an unintended pitch or timbral jolt? Does adding Ankora make the hand–breath transition harder? Does fatigue change the ease of the switch over a longer take? These can be observed without prescribing when Paul must breathe or which rhythm he must play.

6. THE TWO CLOCKS ARE NOT REQUIRED TO MATCH

Ankora's provisional reference of about 137 beats per minute corresponds to approximately 2.28 beats per second, or 438 milliseconds between nominal beats. A listener cannot reasonably be assumed to take a full breath every 438 milliseconds. A respiratory response, if it occurs, would more plausibly relate to larger musical groupings, phrase endings, changes in density, or slow dynamic envelopes. For illustration only, eight nominal beats at 137 BPM span about 3.50 seconds; sixteen span about 7.01 seconds. Those are arithmetic relationships, not evidence that KLAAT listeners breathe every eight or sixteen beats.

Amastacia introduces still another temporal structure: audible breath-shaped articulations that may or may not correspond one-to-one with Paul's pulmonary breathing. Ankora can be rhythmically compelling without tracking either one. Their independence may be part of the music's appeal. We should investigate whether listeners perceive a relationship between these layers, not insist on synchronized cycles.

7. WHAT MUSIC-LISTENING PHYSIOLOGY ACTUALLY SHOWS

Bernardi, Porta, and Sleight studied 12 practicing musicians and 12 matched non-musicians. Participants heard six musical styles, with two- and four-minute presentations and an inserted silent pause. The researchers measured breathing rate, ventilation, carbon dioxide, heart rate, blood pressure, cerebral blood-flow velocity, and baroreflex responses. Faster tempi and simpler rhythmic structures were associated with increased ventilation, heart rate, and blood pressure relative to baseline; the pause reduced several measures, sometimes below baseline. Musicians showed greater respiratory sensitivity to tempo. These were responses to specific recorded selections under laboratory conditions, not a universal effect of rhythm and not evidence about KLAAT. [S3]

A separate 2012 study by Sato, Ohsuga, and Moriya followed repeated listening to the same music track once a day for ten days. The timing of some respiratory events became more likely to coincide with positions in the music, assessed against surrogate-data comparisons, while the respiratory period did not change. That is an especially valuable distinction: *when* breaths occur relative to a piece can become more repeatable without the *overall breathing rate* changing. The result concerns one repeated-listening design, not spontaneous KLAAT exposure. [S4]

8. CONTRADICTORY RESULTS ARE PART OF THE ANSWER

Naturalistic audience studies complicate a simple respiratory-entrainment story. A 2023 Scientific Reports study measured 132 audience members across three classical concerts. It reported synchrony in some physiological and movement measures and relationships with emotional immersion. But although respiration-rate synchrony was observed, breathing behavior itself was not synchronized in the same way. Rate correlation and the alignment of inhale/exhale phases are not interchangeable. [S5]

This is not a trivial statistical issue. Two people can breathe at similarly varying rates while inhaling at different moments. A shared change in rate might reflect arousal, attention, posture, or features of the musical passage. Conversely, two listeners might inhale near a repeated phrase ending while maintaining different average rates. KLAAT research must name which of these it is testing.

9. THE PERFORMER'S RESPIRATION IS NOT A LISTENER INSTRUCTION

Paul's circular breathing allows the acoustic drone to continue through nasal inspirations. If the inhale is acoustically hidden, the listener may have no information about its timing. It would be a category error to claim that the listener is physiologically following a hidden breath. If the inhale is audible, the listener may notice it, but noticing a breath cue does not prove a matched respiratory response.

The same caution applies to a sensation of bodily energy. Someone may report a chest sensation while listening to Amastacia, but without a respiratory trace, sound-pressure measurement, and suitable comparison condition, we cannot identify whether that sensation came from acoustic vibration, altered breathing, emotional arousal, expectation, or something else. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and a subjective report is still valuable even when the cause is unknown.

10. ANKORA MAY MASK OR REVEAL THE BREATH

Ankora's transient attacks can make brief Amastacia events harder to hear when they overlap in time or frequency. Conversely, a sparse drum moment can expose a soft nasal inhale or a change in drone color. This is a plausible auditory-masking explanation, not a demonstrated effect in the September KLAAT reference. It suggests a useful retrospective observation: a breath transition that sounds obvious in Amastacia alone might be inconspicuous in the full duo recording. That does not make the transition more or less technically successful; it changes the evidence available to the listener.

A recording engineer can also unintentionally alter this relationship. Compression may lift quiet breath noise, close microphones may exaggerate nasal sounds, and noise reduction may erase them. These are reasons to retain an untouched reference take and document processing. They are not reasons to scrub every breath from the recording. The archaeosonic aesthetic may benefit from audible evidence of the human body making the sound.

11. CONTINUITY AND ABSORPTION: A REASONABLE BUT UNPROVEN LINK

One KLAAT hypothesis is that a drone continuing through many breath cycles offers a stable perceptual background. The listener can attend to slow timbral evolution while Ankora creates changing rhythmic activity. Such continuity might support absorption because it reduces the need to repeatedly reorient to a new sound source. Another possibility is the opposite: perfectly uniform continuity may eventually recede into the background, while small breath or harmonic changes restore attention. Both are consistent with general principles of adaptation and novelty; neither is an established KLAAT effect.

This is why 'more seamless circular breathing' should not be confused with 'more powerful KLAAT.' Technical continuity is a capability. Perceptual absorption is a listener outcome. The former may enable the latter under some conditions, but that relationship requires evidence.

12. A HEALTH-STUDY WARNING: THE DIDGERIDOO SLEEP-APNEA TRIAL

The most frequently cited clinical didgeridoo experiment should be interpreted carefully. Puhan and colleagues randomized 25 adults with moderate obstructive sleep apnea and snoring to four months of didgeridoo lessons and home practice or a waiting-list control. The intervention group practiced about 25 minutes per day on approximately six days per week. Compared with controls, daytime sleepiness improved by an estimated 3.0 points on the Epworth scale (95% CI −5.7 to −0.3), and the apnea–hypopnea index improved by 6.2 events per hour (95% CI −12.3 to −0.1). Sleep-quality scores did not significantly differ. [S6]

This is an intriguing small trial of training in a selected clinical population. It does not demonstrate that listening to didgeridoo treats apnea, that all didgeridoo players gain the same benefit, or that KLAAT is a medical intervention. Its waiting-list control, sample size, and lack of a comparable active training condition limit causal specificity. The artistic research should not use a clinical finding as proof of listener trance, healing, or autonomic regulation. Nor should the trial be taken as a reason for Paul to force prolonged or uncomfortable breathing exercises.

13. A PRACTICAL SAFETY BOUNDARY

Circular breathing is a motor skill, not a contest in how long a person can avoid an ordinary breath. Excessive effort, dizziness, tingling, or air hunger are reasons to stop and return to comfortable breathing. Rapid or forced breathing can change carbon dioxide balance; the KLAAT research program should not prescribe breath-holding or hyperventilation to create altered states. This is a safety distinction, not an assertion that ordinary skilled didgeridoo playing is inherently dangerous. Technique can be developed gradually, with breaks and attention to comfort.

14. RETROSPECTIVE APPLICATION TO KLAAT'S ARTISTIC LANGUAGE

Ground Anchor, Threshold Gate, Rising Pulse, and Return Path remain optional descriptions of things that may arise spontaneously, not required stages. In a recording that begins with Amastacia alone, Ground Anchor might make the sustained breath-supported Field easy to notice. In a recording where Ankora gradually becomes prominent, Threshold Gate might make the listener's attention shift from breath texture to a recoverable pulse. During a naturally intensifying passage, Rising Pulse might contain many circular-breathing transitions that go unheard because of the drum's activity. In a naturally thinning passage, Return Path might expose the faint texture of Amastacia again.

But a KLAAT take might open with both instruments, remain dense throughout, or end abruptly. The sound is not wrong. The descriptive labels simply may not apply. Likewise, KSV-0.1 remains a provisional retrospective vocabulary, not a performance controller.

15. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE

ESTABLISHED: Circular breathing involves coordinated oral-air delivery and nasal inspiration, with demonstrable tongue, palate, and oral-cavity movements in studied wind musicians. [S1, S2] ESTABLISHED: The eight-person MRI pilot found a difference in relative oropharyngeal occlusion time between its professional and experienced-amateur groups, but it was not a didgeridoo trial. [S1] ESTABLISHED: Musical listening can affect breathing and other cardiovascular measures in controlled studies; results depend on stimuli and participants. [S3] ESTABLISHED: Repeated listening can increase the coincidence of respiratory events with positions in a piece without changing average respiratory period in at least one study. [S4] ESTABLISHED: Audience respiration-rate synchrony and synchronized inhale/exhale behavior are different measurements and need not coincide. [S5] ESTABLISHED: A small randomized didgeridoo-training trial reported improvements in selected sleep-apnea outcomes, not a general listening effect. [S6]

16. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Mouthpiece resistance, instrument bore, embouchure, and experience affect how findings from brass and woodwind circular breathing transfer to Amastacia. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: A breath transition can be acoustically obvious or hidden depending on articulation, Ankora overlap, microphone placement, and processing. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Respiratory responses to music may depend on familiarity, posture, attention, emotional response, musical structure, and whether the listener is moving. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Studies that track respiratory rate cannot automatically establish respiratory phase-locking. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: The sleep-apnea trial concerns repeated active training in a selected patient group, not passive listening or generalized health effects.

17. PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES

H1: As Paul's circular-breathing fluency increases, some transition-related dips in Amastacia's amplitude or harmonic continuity become smaller or less frequent, without necessarily reducing expressive texture. H2: The audible salience of a breath transition varies with Ankora's concurrent event density and recording perspective. H3: Listener descriptions of 'breathing' or 'living' drone texture relate more closely to audible amplitude/harmonic gestures than to Paul's unobservable pulmonary inhalation timing. H4: Some listeners show recurring respiratory event timing around recognizable KLAAT gestures after repeated exposure, even if their mean respiratory rates do not change. H5: Listener absorption and activation may respond differently to audible breath texture and overall drone continuity.

All five are untested for KLAAT. H4 would require suitable respiration measurement and a repeated-listening design; a phone microphone or an informal rating alone cannot verify it.

18. SPECULATION

SPECULATION: The impression of an ancient, continuously inhabited acoustic world may depend partly on hearing a human-made drone that survives the performer's breath cycle while retaining small organic imperfections. SPECULATION: The most personally compelling Amastacia passages may occur when technique becomes automatic enough for spontaneous articulation to emerge without conscious management. SPECULATION: Some listeners may imagine or subtly adapt their breathing to the slow contours of Amastacia, while others respond primarily to Ankora's rhythm or timbral grain. SPECULATION: The audible presence of breath may communicate intimacy or effort even when it does not produce any measurable respiratory synchronization.

These are artistic and empirical possibilities, not findings.

19. UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS

UNSUPPORTED: Circular breathing means Paul is continuously exhaling from his lungs. UNSUPPORTED: A sustained C# drone automatically entrains a listener's respiration, heart rate, or brainwaves. UNSUPPORTED: A listener's respiratory rate must follow Ankora's approximately 137 BPM pulse. UNSUPPORTED: A performer entering flow causes listeners to enter the same altered state. UNSUPPORTED: A recorded breath sound proves the listener's inhale occurred at that moment. UNSUPPORTED: Didgeridoo training studies establish KLAAT as treatment for sleep apnea or any other condition. UNSUPPORTED: More hidden breaths, more audible breaths, or longer uninterrupted drone necessarily produce stronger absorption. UNSUPPORTED: A chest sensation during KLAAT proves mechanical resonance of bodily tissues.

20. METHODOLOGY UPDATE — THE THREE-BREATH SEPARATION

The durable addition is a measurement distinction, not a fifth state dimension or a composition rule: (A) PERFORMER: actual respiratory/air-delivery mechanics, recorded only when independently observed; (B) ACOUSTIC: audible envelope, breath noise, harmonic changes, and continuity in the recorded sound; (C) LISTENER: subjective descriptions, optional respiratory measurements, and separate absorption/activation outcomes.

A fourth record—Ankora's acoustic onsets and density—can help explain when Amastacia's breath cues are masked. Keep timestamps and evidence sources separate. Do not label a waveform feature 'nasal inspiration' without independent verification. Do not label a subjective report 'autonomic entrainment.' If physiological measurements are ever introduced, preregister whether the endpoint is breathing rate, respiratory phase, event coincidence, or another quantity, and use appropriate controls for autocorrelation and shared musical cues. The older event-ontology and uncertainty gates still apply.

21. WHY THIS DOES NOT REQUIRE A NEW KLAAT SOUND

No imposed phrase length. No breath-per-beat rule. No target breathing rate for Paul or the audience. No mandated circular-breathing pattern. No obligation to keep the drone perfectly smooth. No automatic requirement to turn breath noise up or down in a mix. No score, ritual script, or change to Ankora's natural rhythmic language. The technique can become more fluent while the music stays spontaneous, textured, and personal. The research should tell us what is happening in the sound before proposing that it should happen differently.

22. ONE OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-082: THE HIDDEN INHALE

Choose one ordinary 60–120-second Amastacia recording, with or without Ankora. Do not perform differently for the test. If making a fresh take, let a phone camera capture Paul's face/upper body from the side while a separate recorder captures the normal sound; a simple clap at the start can help align them. If using an existing audio-only take, skip the inhale verification and mark only candidate acoustic transitions.

After playing, identify three or four moments when Paul knows or can see that he took a quick nasal inhale during circular breathing. Without altering the audio, listen to each moment once with eyes closed. Mark it as (1) clearly audible, (2) subtly audible, or (3) not reliably detectable. Note whether Ankora was active and whether the sound changed through level, pitch impression, harmonic color, or breath noise. If a second listener is willing, let them mark possible inhalations without seeing the video; compare afterward. This is a skill-and-recording observation, not a respiratory-health experiment. A transition that is audible is not necessarily a flaw, and a hidden transition is not necessarily more artistically successful.

DECISIVE QUESTION: WHEN PAUL BREATHES THROUGH AMASTACIA WITHOUT BREAKING HER WORLD, WHAT ACTUALLY REACHES THE LISTENER—AND WHAT REMAINS INSIDE THE PERFORMER'S BODY?

23. RESEARCH DIRECTION AND PROVENANCE

This session closes a gap opened by Sessions 79–81: the acoustic record mediates between a performer's internal state and a listener's experience, and 'breath' exists on more than one level. The next valuable advance would be actual synchronized KLAAT video/audio, careful observation of natural circular-breathing transitions, and optional listener descriptions—not another compulsory compositional framework. Later, if warranted, a separate passive-listener respiratory study could distinguish event timing from rate changes and from true phase alignment. Until then, KLAAT-specific physiological claims remain unverified.
