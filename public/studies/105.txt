KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 105
WHEN THE SOUND SEEMS TO COME CLOSER
Auditory looming, rising intensity, and the difference between musical urgency and an approaching source
Published: October 20, 2026 — 15:15 CDT


GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH STATUS
A critical synthesis of published primary research, not an experiment on KLAAT. No new Amastacia–Ankora recording was analyzed and no KLAAT listener was tested. Numerical results below belong to external experiments. This session investigates a possible perceptual ingredient of activation without implying that Paul should crescendo, accelerate, alter his improvisation, or make his music threatening. It treats the existing sound as the starting point.


CENTRAL QUESTION


A long, nearly continuous Amastacia drone can feel like a place rather than an object. Then something in that place seems to advance: the buzz thickens, breath pressure makes the harmonics more conspicuous, Ankora's attacks acquire greater weight, or a dense passage begins to press toward the listener. Nothing in the room has necessarily moved. Paul may have kept the underlying pulse roughly stable. Yet a listener might report that the sound is coming closer, gathering force, or becoming urgent.


What would such a report mean scientifically? Is the listener hearing increased loudness, an actual auditory impression of approach, stronger rhythmic activation, greater emotional intensity, or simply a change in the recording or playback? The surprising answer from research on auditory looming is that rising acoustic intensity can receive perceptual priority over an equivalent fall in intensity. But that priority is not universal, and recent experiments challenge one popular explanation for it.


This session's question is therefore narrower than whether a KLAAT crescendo creates trance: CAN AN UNPLANNED CHANGE IN THE AMASTACIA–ANKORA SOUND FIELD BE EXPERIENCED AS APPROACH OR URGENCY EVEN WHEN NO SOURCE APPROACHES, AND HOW WOULD WE TELL THAT APART FROM ORDINARY LOUDNESS AND DENSITY?


1. NOVELTY GATE: WHAT IS ACTUALLY NEW HERE


The existing corpus already distinguishes pulse rate from subdivision density, the temporal binding of Amastacia and Ankora, perceptual boundaries, performer flow, listener absorption, spatial room variation, and the persistence of sound after its apparent end. Session 90, especially, addressed when two instruments may be heard as one event. Session 103 separated energized walking from step-to-beat synchronization. Session 104 studied recurrence within unscored improvisation. None is a dedicated test of the auditory-looming asymmetry: the difference between hearing a sound grow and hearing it diminish, and the specific claim that a growth in intensity can resemble an approaching object.


The Curriculum Index explicitly listed ecological acoustics and looming/approach cues as an unresolved direction. The latest Hourly Research notes through October 20, 14:17 CDT address EEG artifact, drone pitch-discrimination limits, hand-to-hide contact impulses, rating-scale recalibration, self-generated support vibration, and post-impact attentional blink. Those are important, but none supplies the present contrast between increasing intensity, physical source motion, perceived approach, and musical urgency. This is a distinct perceptual question, not another generic essay about getting louder.


2. FOUR DIFFERENT MEANINGS OF 'COMING CLOSER'


Physical approach: the instrument or loudspeaker changes its position relative to a listener. Sound pressure, spectral balance, direct-to-reverberant ratio, interaural cues, and sometimes Doppler information can change as a result. This is an external geometric event.


Acoustic growth: the recorded or received signal becomes more intense over time. This may be caused by breath pressure, microphone distance, changing radiation, a stronger drum strike, more simultaneous sources, compression, or a volume-control adjustment. The source can remain stationary.


Perceived approach: a listener experiences the sound as if its source were advancing or closing distance. This is a perceptual report, not proof of motion. It can be encouraged by an intensity rise but need not follow every rise.


Musical urgency: a listener feels momentum, tension, activation, or expectancy. That can emerge from attack density, timing, spectral roughness, expectation, meaning, and loudness. It can occur without any sense that a sound source is physically approaching.


The distinction matters because a report such as 'the drum came at me' may be metaphorical, spatial, emotional, or tactile. A good study does not silently convert that language into a brain-imaging claim or a sound-pressure measurement.


3. THE PHYSICAL SIGNAL: WHAT CAN ACTUALLY RISE?


In air, sound-pressure level is logarithmic: a 10 dB increase in level corresponds to a tenfold increase in mean-square pressure, not a tenfold increase in perceived loudness. For a stable signal at a single point, the sound-pressure amplitude ratio for a 10 dB difference is about 3.16. Perceived loudness does not follow a fixed simple conversion across all spectra, durations, levels, listeners, or contexts.


Ankora complicates the picture. Its attacks are brief, broadband, and dependent on strike mechanics and radiation; one exceptionally sharp transient may increase peak level without raising a ten-second average very much. A higher rate of attacks may increase average energy even if each strike is no harder. Amastacia complicates it differently: a stable fundamental can coexist with strong changes in upper harmonics and vocal-tract filtering, producing a subjective increase in brightness or force without a commensurate change in the low fundamental.


Thus 'it got bigger' is not a unique acoustic variable. A future analysis would need time-varying short-window level, peak level, spectral balance, onset density, and perhaps separate source tracks if available. A waveform display alone is insufficient. Neither a LUFS trace nor a microphone SPL trace directly measures the feeling of an approaching object.


4. THE CLASSIC ASYMMETRY: RISING IS NOT THE MIRROR IMAGE OF FALLING


John G. Neuhoff's 1998 Nature correspondence compared the perceived changes of tones that increased or decreased in level. Participants systematically judged the rise as a greater change than the equivalent fall; the bias was stronger at higher levels. The interpretation was ecological: in natural settings, increasing intensity can be a useful cue that something is approaching, and approaching things can demand attention sooner than receding things [1].


Neuhoff's 2001 experiments expanded the question to apparent auditory motion. Rising and falling sounds were not perceived as perfect opposites. In some conditions, approaching sounds were judged closer than equivalently distant receding sounds; the effects were stronger for tones than for noise [2]. That stimulus difference is important for KLAAT. Amastacia has a pitched, harmonic identity but also complex breath noise, rasp, and moving spectral emphases. Ankora is more transient and noise-like. The classic tonal bias cannot simply be assigned to every mixed Amastacia–Ankora moment.


The result establishes a psychophysical asymmetry in specific laboratory tasks. It does not establish that any natural crescendo makes a listener fear an approaching object. It does not establish that a long drone triggers a specific evolutionary reflex. And it certainly does not show that a KLAAT listener enters trance when the music swells.


5. PHYSIOLOGICAL ORIENTING IS REAL, BUT ITS MEANING IS LIMITED


Bach and colleagues (2008) compared rising, falling, and steady-intensity sounds while measuring behavioral, psychophysiological, and fMRI responses. Rising intensity, compared with falling intensity, facilitated autonomic orienting and phasic alertness to auditory targets. Their neuroimaging analysis also identified greater activity in the amygdala and other regions involved in auditory and attentional processing [3].


This is more than a loudness judgment: it indicates that the direction of an intensity change can matter to attention and physiology in controlled conditions. But an amygdala response is not a meter for fear, a diagnosis of threat, or a demonstration of trance. The amygdala participates in multiple kinds of salience and learning. Skin conductance can index sympathetic arousal without telling us whether the experience is pleasurable, unpleasant, empowering, or disturbing.


For KLAAT the careful inference is: a rising acoustic envelope might contribute to momentary orienting or alertness, among other cues, in some listeners. It is not evidence that KLAAT has done so. If a listener reports feeling energized, that report deserves to be recorded as an experience rather than retrofitted into an unmeasured neural mechanism.


6. AN ACTUAL MOVING SOURCE IS NOT THE SAME AS A VOLUME FADER


A real source approaching a listener can change more than intensity. Head-related filtering, interaural differences, direct/reverberant balance, atmospheric absorption, and motion trajectory can convey distance and movement. A studio gain ramp changes amplitude but does not automatically recreate all those cues. A low-frequency drone reproduced by a single loudspeaker in a room may produce strong spatial resonances without suggesting an approaching source.


Bach, Neuhoff, Perrig, and Seifritz (2009) compared sounds that conveyed richer three-dimensional motion information with sounds using intensity change alone. Approaching sounds generally produced greater skin conductance, phasic alertness, and subjective arousal or unpleasantness than receding sounds. The approach-versus-recede difference in skin-conductance amplitude was stronger when fuller motion cues were available, whereas several other effects were comparable across cue sets [4].


The nuance is essential. Intensity rise can be powerful without being the entire ecological event. Adding a genuine spatial cue can matter, but it does not mean that every spatialized sound is stronger or that an artificial pan will produce the same effect. KLAAT's room, audience position, and playback chain are part of the evidence problem, not merely production details.


7. THE SPEED OF THE THING THAT IS NOT THERE


Neuhoff's 2016 open-access study used virtual moving sounds and asked listeners to estimate speed. The first experiment began with 80 participants and analyzed 77 after excluding three who misunderstood the task. The simulated source moved at 15, 20, or 25 meters per second; approach/recession and near/far trajectory were manipulated. In the within-participant study, perceived speed was greater for looming than receding sounds. For close conditions, mean perceived speed was 45.9 versus 35.2 in the reported units of the study's analysis; for distant conditions it was 32.4 versus 29.3. The close comparison yielded a larger effect (reported d = 0.47) than the distant one (d = 0.15) [5].


A second, between-participant experiment used 200 listeners, reducing the possibility that direct comparison itself cued the hypothesis. Looming sounds were again judged faster on average (36.3 versus 27.6 in the study's reported speed estimates), and participants discriminated the three tested speeds better in the looming than receding condition. The authors reported a Speed-by-Direction interaction, F(2,392) = 9.1, p < .001 [5].


These are numerical results from a headphone-based virtual-motion task, not predictions of what an Ankora crescendo does to KLAAT listeners. The virtual source was a square wave with a 400 Hz fundamental, not a frame drum or agave didgeridoo. Participants estimated object speed, not musical activation. Their judgments do, however, demonstrate that the brain can treat approach and recession differently even when the nominal physical speed is matched.


8. THE CRUCIAL CONTRADICTION: THE ENDPOINT MAY NOT SHIFT FORWARD


A major recent challenge prevents the looming story from becoming a neat evolutionary just-so explanation. Merz and colleagues (published February 17, 2026) asked whether listeners actually hear the endpoint of a rising sound as more intense than it is. Their first experiment tested 30 participants with 1,000 Hz sine tones that changed by 15 dB over 500 or 1,500 milliseconds. After a 250 ms pause, listeners compared the final intensity with a probe differing by up to 6 dB. A second experiment tested 32 participants with a three-component harmonic tone (440, 880, and 1,320 Hz) and stationary-level controls [6].


The predicted forward shift in perceived final intensity did not appear. Across both experiments, the results contradicted that particular 'error on the side of safety' prediction. In Experiment 2, the main effect of rise-versus-fall direction was not significant, F(1,31) = 2.27, p = .142. The authors discuss a speed-prior account as an alternative to a simple forward-displacement explanation [6].


This is not proof that the older rising-intensity effects were imaginary. The studies ask different questions: comparative loudness change, speed or arrival judgments, physiological orienting, and the perceived final intensity of a changing tone are distinct endpoints. The 2026 result challenges a proposed mechanism, not the existence of every auditory looming effect. It also illustrates why KLAAT must not compress multiple observations into one label such as 'the looming response.'


9. ANOTHER IMPORTANT NULL: MORE ALERT DOES NOT ALWAYS MEAN BETTER PERFORMANCE


Seebold and He (published online October 2025; 2026 journal issue) compared looming and stationary alert sounds before visual discrimination tasks. They found no evidence that looming sounds improved performance more than stationary alert sounds in either the contrast or orientation discrimination comparison, even though alert sounds affected the tasks differently [7].


This matters for the everyday-soundtrack ambition. A KLAAT passage can feel urgent without making the listener objectively faster, more accurate, or more focused at a separate task. Activation is not automatically enhancement. Whether a sound supports driving, walking, drawing, or working depends on the task, attention demand, listening level, and listener. A soundtrack that feels intensely alive may be ideal for one activity and distracting for another.


10. A DEVELOPMENTAL CLUE, NOT A UNIVERSAL MUSIC RULE


Ignatiadis and colleagues (2024) recorded high-density EEG responses in adults and newborns while manipulating two kinds of auditory distance-motion cues: intensity and spectral filtering. Both groups showed cortical signatures favoring approaching sounds under some conditions, but newborns showed the bias for intensity manipulations and not for spectral cues, whereas adults showed both [8].


That difference is informative. It suggests that the auditory system's response to apparent approach is not one immutable detector operating identically for every cue and every listener. Some sensitivity may appear early, while experience shapes the use of more elaborate spatial information. But scalp EEG source estimates are not direct measures of fear, trance, or musical enjoyment. Nor does an early-life response imply that a KLAAT drone can access an ancient, culture-free state of consciousness.


11. WHY KLAAT IS AN UNUSUAL TEST CASE


The literature often uses a short, isolated, carefully controlled tone or simulated moving object. KLAAT is almost the opposite. Amastacia may already be sounding when a perceived increase begins. Its spectrum can change without a large change in total level. Ankora can increase event density without speeding up the inferred beat. The listener may be surrounded by reverberation or hear the recording through headphones. A long performance provides expectation, habituation, and personal associations that brief warning-sound trials deliberately remove.


That complexity is a reason to test rather than to borrow a laboratory result wholesale. A KLAAT moment that feels as though it advances might be driven by a measured level rise. Or by a sudden appearance of upper harmonics. Or by closer-spaced Ankora attacks. Or by a learned expectation that something is about to happen. Or by a listener's imagery. The strongest explanation may involve several cues; their contributions remain unmeasured until actual material is examined.


12. FOUR CANDIDATE PATHWAYS IN AN AUTHENTIC TAKE


A. DRONE ENVELOPE: Amastacia's average level increases over several seconds. Candidate consequence: a sensation of increasing presence. The relevant acoustic measurement is a calibrated or at least consistently captured level trajectory. Perceived approach remains a separate listener question.


B. SPECTRAL APPROACH WITHOUT MUCH LEVEL CHANGE: upper harmonics and rasp become more prominent while total energy changes modestly. Candidate consequence: greater proximity or immediacy, perhaps because close sound sources often preserve more high-frequency information. But the instrument's vocal-tract filtering can produce the same spectral effect with no motion, and listeners may simply hear brightness.


C. EVENT-BASED URGENCY: Ankora's attacks become denser or more strongly accented while Amastacia's level stays roughly constant. Candidate consequence: urgency or movement desire. This is a rhythmic activation hypothesis, not an auditory-motion claim. It must be kept distinct from a true rising sound-pressure envelope.


D. RELATIONAL SWELL: an Amastacia articulation and Ankora accent cluster coincide, making the composite event seem larger. Session 90 already addresses grouping; the new question is whether the resulting event is experienced as approaching, not merely as unified. This is a hypothesis, not an observed effect.


These four routes need not be mutually exclusive. Their separation is analytical, not a request that Paul perform them as four exercises or build a score.


13. THE RECORDING CHAIN CAN CREATE OR DESTROY THE EFFECT


A microphone's proximity and orientation, automatic gain control, limiting, compression, room modes, and headphone or loudspeaker frequency response can reshape dynamic changes. Automatic gain control may suppress a slow natural rise; a compressor may exaggerate or diminish the subjective weight of attacks; a room mode may make one note bloom at a seat while another seat hears relatively little change. A listener turning up the volume can produce an apparent rise that was never in the file.


For later analysis, retain the original uncompressed or minimally processed recording if possible. Document whether gain was fixed and whether any compression or normalization occurred. A waveform from an uncalibrated device can support within-file relative comparisons but not claims about the absolute sound pressure at a listener's body. No calibrated low-frequency exposure data exist for the KLAAT recording here. Protect hearing and avoid using extreme levels to manufacture a response.


14. A SMALL CAUSAL DIAGRAM FOR HONEST INTERPRETATION


PERFORMER ACTION / SOURCE RADIATION
    -> captured acoustic envelope, spectrum, onset structure
    -> playback and room transfer
    -> sound arriving at a particular listener
    -> perceived loudness / approach / urgency / bodily salience
    -> optional attention, emotion, movement, or physiology


At every arrow, another variable can intervene. The performer can feel intense pressure without a proportional far-field sound increase. A recorder can alter the envelope. A room can alter the received spectrum. Two listeners can interpret the same change differently. A report of urgency does not identify a specific neural process. This is why no single waveform, rating, or physiological sensor settles the question.


15. THE EVIDENCE LADDER


ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT
Rising and falling intensity are not always perceptually symmetric. Controlled experiments have found rising-intensity advantages in loudness-change judgments, orienting, and apparent motion/speed tasks [1–5].


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT
The magnitude and even the form of an effect depend on stimulus spectrum, motion cues, level, task, listener, and what is being judged. A 2026 endpoint-probe experiment found no forward shift in final-intensity perception, and a recent visual-task experiment found no special looming advantage over stationary alerts [6–7].


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT
Naturally emerging changes in Amastacia's level or spectrum, and Ankora's attack pattern, may contribute to perceived approach or urgency. Some listeners may describe a swell as spatial approach while others hear only a louder or denser passage. These predictions require natural KLAAT excerpts and independent listener reports.


SPECULATION
A continuous low-frequency drone might make a later increase feel as if the entire sound environment advances toward the listener. This is an evocative artistic possibility, not a demonstrated acoustic or neural mechanism.


UNSUPPORTED
KLAAT has been shown to trigger a universal threat reflex; rising volume reliably induces trance; amygdala activation means fear; louder music heals or entrains the nervous system; a measured SPL increase proves perceived approach; or a perceived approach proves actual source motion.


16. WHAT WOULD COUNT AS A GOOD NEGATIVE RESULT?


If several listeners describe a naturally intensifying passage as 'stronger' but none reports approach, the result would separate musical urgency from looming rather than invalidate KLAAT. If a passage with stable level and increasing drum density feels more urgent than one with a large level rise, that would challenge a loudness-only account. If different playback systems change the apparent advance, the recording chain becomes part of the explanation. If listeners disagree, their variation is data rather than a failure to receive the music correctly.


A negative finding is particularly valuable because it protects the artistic identity from a false causal story. KLAAT can work powerfully without behaving like a simulated oncoming vehicle.


17. ARTISTIC IMPLICATION: PRESENCE IS NOT A COMMAND


Paul does not need to make Amastacia louder, make Ankora faster, or turn the performance into a crescendo because a study found an orienting effect. The work is already driven by touch, breath, pulse, and instinct. Science can help distinguish the different ways the existing sound becomes present to someone: physically louder, spectrally closer, rhythmically urgent, emotionally gripping, or spatially approaching. Those are useful words for listening, engineering, and eventually comparing actual audience responses.


The archaeological and ritual feeling of KLAAT is an aesthetic construction, not a scientific explanation. Its strength can come from remaining unfamiliar and personal while its physical and perceptual mechanisms are investigated carefully. 'Activation' should stay open enough to include movement, attentional capture, vivid imagery, emotional energy, or a sense of being drawn into the sound. It should not be reduced to one laboratory warning response.


18. ONE OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION — KRX-105


During a normal listen to an existing, unchanged KLAAT recording, invite one willing listener to describe any moment that felt as if the sound became more present. Do not offer the words 'approaching,' 'threatening,' or 'trance' beforehand. If they spontaneously mention closeness, arrival, expansion, force, or urgency, note their exact words and approximate timestamp; 'nothing like that happened' is equally valid. Only afterward, if the original recording is available, compare the passage's short-window level, spectral brightness, and Ankora attack density with the nearby preceding passage. Do not treat one listener's report as proof of cause. Do not alter Paul's playing to satisfy the hypothesis.


The aim is not to manufacture a looming sound. It is to learn whether the sound Paul already makes sometimes feels as if it advances—and, if it does, which measured changes accompany that experience.
