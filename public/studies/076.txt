KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 76
THE REFERENCE IS A DISTRIBUTION
How Annotation Disagreement Becomes an Experimental Result Instead of a Hidden Error
Published: 2026-10-10, 19:16 CDT (America/Chicago)
Research phase: Measurement → Experiments → Performance Methodology
Status: Synthesis and proposed protocol; no new KLAAT listener or annotator results are claimed.


CENTRAL QUESTION
Session 75 gave KLAAT an event dictionary. It separated Ankora attacks from beats, Amastacia gestures from instantaneous onsets, intended phases from perceived transitions, and physical exposure from listener reports. The next obstacle is subtler: after two annotators use that dictionary, what happens when their labels disagree? If the research director quietly replaces two uncertain observations with one clean-looking consensus timestamp, the uncertainty has not disappeared. It has merely been hidden.


The governing principle of Session 76 is:
A REFERENCE RECORD SHOULD DESCRIBE WHAT IS KNOWN, HOW IT WAS MEASURED, AND WHAT REMAINS AMBIGUOUS. IT SHOULD NOT MAKE DISAGREEMENT INVISIBLE.


The connected Master Instructions, Curriculum Index, Research Ledger, and Session Corpus were consulted. The ChatGPT Library Hourly Research folder was checked; its newest available note is dated October 5 at 18:33 CDT, before Session 75. This study therefore extends the strongest open methodological problem rather than attributing nonexistent fresh observations to the hourly layer. It also revisits an earlier research strand: spatial and mechanical measurement disagreement may be informative rather than a reason to collapse multiple inputs into one number.


1. WHY THE NEXT STEP IS NOT ANOTHER THEORY OF RESONANCE
KLAAT has accumulated hypotheses about the continuous Field, rhythmic prediction, perceived urgency, omission, tactile contact, room acoustics, and listener segmentation. Sessions 73–75 shifted the program toward measurement. No verified KRX-073, KRX-074, or KRX-075 results are presently available in the reviewed archive. That makes it premature to announce that listeners independently perceive the four-phase journey, or that an Earned Wound causes bodily activation. The next useful advance is an analysis protocol that will remain honest when the first data are messy.


2. A SINGLE TIMESTAMP CAN BE A MISLEADING SUMMARY
Imagine two trained annotators hear an Amastacia breath-led spectral transition. One marks 02:11–02:16. The other marks 02:14–02:20. Both recognize a change. They disagree about its onset, duration, and perhaps the precise feature that defines it. Replacing those intervals with the midpoint 02:15 would destroy useful information: neither annotator actually claimed that the event was an instantaneous change at 02:15. A better record retains both intervals, the operational definition, the reasons for the marks, and a clearly labeled adjudication decision if one is made.


3. THREE DIFFERENT FORMS OF UNCERTAINTY
Timing uncertainty concerns when an event begins or ends. Category uncertainty concerns what kind of event it is: breath phrase, spectral shift, accent, structural boundary, or something else. Interpretive uncertainty concerns whether the event belongs to a larger phase, a smaller gesture, or a different perceived meter. These uncertainties must not be reduced to one generic confidence score. A confident classification can have imprecise timing; a sharply timed acoustic attack can have uncertain musical function.


4. ANKORA: PHYSICAL ATTACK VERSUS MUSICAL BEAT
A waveform can support an estimate of the onset of a drum attack. It cannot by itself decide whether the listener hears that attack as a beat, subdivision, syncopation, or isolated event. At the provisional 137 BPM reference, the nominal quarter-note interval is about 438 milliseconds, but half-time hearing yields a different perceptual scaffold. A 40-millisecond onset disagreement is a different problem from a two-to-one metrical disagreement. The first may be sensor or annotation variability; the second may be a valid alternative organization of the same music.


5. AMASTACIA: STATES, GESTURES, AND CHANGE ZONES
Amastacia's C# agave didgeridoo produces a sustained, breath-led Field whose acoustic changes can be gradual and overlapping. A change in spectral centroid may begin before the listener notices a phrase boundary. A breath phrase may be audible without a strong amplitude dip. A harmonic shift may occur without any agreed musical boundary. The ontology must allow simultaneous labels from different evidence channels rather than forcing every moment into one exclusive class.


6. THE RESEARCH LITERATURE DOES NOT REQUIRE ONE PERFECT GROUND TRUTH
McFee, Nieto, Farbood, and Bello (2017) analyzed hierarchical musical structure annotations and emphasized that different listeners can provide different yet partly compatible interpretations. Their L-measure was designed to compare musical hierarchies without assuming that every annotator uses the same depth of description. Work on the SALAMI and SPAM corpora demonstrates the practical importance of independent annotations at coarse and fine scales. This supports KLAAT's separation of four-phase macrostructure from breath, accents, and omissions at smaller scales. It does not prove that KLAAT's particular hierarchy is valid.


7. THE REFERENCE RECORD SHOULD HAVE LAYERS
Layer P — physical observations: Ankora attack times, source cessation, microphone-recorded amplitude and spectral features, any separately measured mechanical motion. Layer C — compositional intention: Paul's predeclared phase zones and intended gestures. Layer A — independent annotation: each annotator's point, interval, category, confidence, and evidence channel. Layer L — listener experience: spontaneous descriptions, retrospective macro boundaries, inferred Clock and metrical level. Layer D — derived analysis: matches, disagreement distributions, model estimates, and uncertainty intervals. No layer should overwrite another.


8. AGREEMENT IS CLASS-SPECIFIC
For discrete Ankora attacks, one-to-one onset matching within predeclared windows can yield precision, recall, F1, unmatched events, and timing-offset distributions. For Amastacia transition intervals, onset F1 is inappropriate: compare overlap, boundary spreads, and category agreement separately. For Clock acquisition, preserve the earliest plausible acquisition time, latest plausible acquisition time, and listener confidence. For macro boundaries, distinguish point judgments from transition zones and use a chance baseline. A pooled '92% agreement' across all these classes would be nearly uninterpretable.


9. THE TOLERANCE CURVE IS MORE INFORMATIVE THAN ONE NUMBER
Suppose Ankora annotations match at 50 milliseconds but not at 25 milliseconds. That may reveal a practical limit of the recording, annotators, or event definition. Rather than selecting whichever tolerance gives a flattering result, report a predeclared curve across several windows. The mir_eval onset evaluation framework uses tolerance-based matching for onset detection; its existence does not make any particular tolerance universally correct for KLAAT. Report the actual offsets and unmatched counts alongside F1.


10. INTERVAL AGREEMENT NEEDS MORE THAN OVERLAP
Two Amastacia annotations can overlap strongly but disagree about their start and end. Conversely, two short intervals can describe the same gradual change while barely overlapping. Preserve intersection-over-union as one descriptive statistic, but also report the start difference, end difference, duration difference, and whether both annotators identified the same acoustic phenomenon. A low overlap score should trigger a review of event definition before a declaration that the annotators heard different music.


11. ADJUDICATION IS NOT A TIME MACHINE
An adjudicator can listen again and choose a working reference label for analysis. That may be useful. It does not retrospectively prove that the chosen label was objectively correct. The original independent marks remain the primary evidence about measurement reproducibility. The adjudicated reference must be tagged as a separate derived version, with a short explanation of the decision. A changed codebook requires a new version and, where necessary, a new independent pass.


12. WHY CONSENSUS CAN BECOME A SOURCE OF BIAS
If Paul knows exactly where Threshold Gate was intended and then adjudicates listener boundaries toward that time, the analysis can appear to validate the score by construction. This is avoidable. Lock Paul's intended zones before listener collection. Keep annotators blind to those zones when they label acoustic events. Keep listeners blind to the phase names. Only combine the layers after their independent records are frozen. If blinding fails, document that failure rather than pretending the comparison is independent.


13. UNCERTAINTY MUST TRAVEL INTO THE NEXT MODEL
Suppose a future analysis asks whether a rise in Ankora event density predicts a listener's boundary near Rising Pulse. If some strikes are ambiguous, the density estimate is uncertain. If the phase boundary is a transition zone, its location is uncertain. If the listener's click has response delay, the observation is uncertain. Fitting one model to one arbitrarily cleaned dataset produces false precision. A practical first step is sensitivity analysis: repeat the planned comparison across defensible label sets, tolerance windows, and transition-zone choices. Report which conclusions survive.


14. DO NOT CALL EVERY RESAMPLING METHOD A CONFIDENCE INTERVAL
A useful sensitivity range may show how a result changes when plausible annotations are substituted. It is not automatically a frequentist 95% confidence interval or a Bayesian credible interval. Those require explicit statistical assumptions and a suitable sampling model. The measurement-error literature distinguishes error-prone observations from underlying quantities and offers correction methods when appropriate validation or repeated measurements exist. KLAAT should begin with transparent robustness checks, not sophisticated models whose assumptions cannot yet be supported by a small pilot.


15. PRESERVE THE UNIT OF REPLICATION
Twenty Ankora strikes from one take are not twenty independent performances. Twelve listeners at one event are not twelve independent venues. Two annotators labeling the same recording are not independent musical stimuli. Report results by recording, annotator, listener, and venue. When enough takes exist, reserve complete performances for held-out evaluation. Do not split adjacent windows from the same take between training and testing and then describe the result as generalization to new performances.


16. A NEGATIVE RESULT CAN IMPROVE THE COMPOSITION
If Ankora attacks are reliably annotated but the Threshold Gate is not independently perceived, the problem may be compositional rather than technical. If Amastacia gestures are not reproducibly classified, revise the gesture dictionary before testing their perceptual influence. If listener phase reports are stable but acoustic feature models fail, the current feature set may be incomplete. These are different outcomes requiring different responses. No one failure should be recast as proof of hidden resonance.


17. THE FOUR-PHASE ARCHITECTURE: GROUND ANCHOR
Ground Anchor must be represented as more than 'no drum.' Log Amastacia's Field stability, breath phrases, spectral gestures, and any quiet intervals. Separate what the microphone measures from what listeners report as continuity or enclosure. A listener may perceive a coherent initial state without identifying many discrete boundaries. The experiment should allow that answer rather than requiring a high event count.


18. THE FOUR-PHASE ARCHITECTURE: THRESHOLD GATE
Record Ankora's first attack as a point event. Record the performer's intended Gate as a compositional zone. Record each listener's first report of a recoverable Clock separately. If those differ by several beats, that difference may be the finding. Test whether the gap persists across independent takes and whether it depends on Amastacia's simultaneous breath changes. Never set Gate time equal to the first drum onset merely because that is easiest to measure.


19. THE FOUR-PHASE ARCHITECTURE: RISING PULSE
Track event density, accents, omissions, loudness, and Field changes as different variables. A denser Ankora Surface may produce a smooth increase in urgency rather than additional section boundaries. A listener may experience activation without reporting tactile vibration. Record urgency, bodily sensation, and boundary perception separately. The claim that controlled density preserves absorption while increasing activation remains a KLAAT hypothesis until tested; annotation quality is only a prerequisite.


20. THE FOUR-PHASE ARCHITECTURE: RETURN PATH
Mark the start of intended Withdrawal, reduction of subdivisions, last Ankora strike, and the later moment when a listener stops anticipating another strike. These may be separated in time. Amastacia's Field may remain stable or may itself change. The annotation scheme must distinguish 'no physical attack' from 'no perceived Clock.' A lingering felt Pulse is a perceptual report, not evidence of ongoing mechanical stimulation.


21. WHAT THIS DOES TO KSV-0.1
The provisional State Vector remains KSV-0.1 = [FIELD STABILITY, CLOCK STRENGTH, SURFACE PRESSURE, WITHDRAWAL]. Session 76 does not add a fifth dimension. Instead, every candidate state variable must have an operational definition, source, time resolution, and uncertainty description. If a component cannot be reliably observed, the model should not be treated as validated simply because it has an evocative name.


22. ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE
ESTABLISHED — Musical structure admits multiple temporal scales, and annotators may produce different yet partially compatible hierarchies (McFee et al., 2017).
ESTABLISHED — Large music-structure annotation corpora preserve multiple annotators and distinguish coarse from fine structure (SALAMI; SPAM).
ESTABLISHED — Onset-detection evaluations rely on operational reference onsets and explicit matching tolerances; changing tolerance can change the score (mir_eval onset documentation).
ESTABLISHED — Measurement error in predictors can bias statistical associations, and repeated or validation measurements can inform correction methods (Keogh & White, 2014; STRATOS guidance, 2020).
ESTABLISHED — A disagreement between independent annotators is evidence about reproducibility under the current task; it is not automatically evidence that either person's experience is invalid.


23. CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — SALAMI and SPAM mainly concern music-structure annotations and do not establish reliability thresholds for didgeridoo spectral gestures or frame-drum attacks.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — A narrow onset tolerance may be suitable for clean transient attacks and unsuitable for breath-shaped transitions.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — High agreement can arise from strong shared instructions, obvious acoustic cues, or a task that suppresses alternative interpretations.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — Sensitivity analyses show robustness to specified choices, not to every possible source of bias.
CONTEXT-DEPENDENT — Statistical measurement-error corrections require assumptions and enough data; a tiny KLAAT pilot may only support descriptive comparisons.


24. PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE HYPOTHESES
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Ankora attack annotation will be more temporally reproducible than Amastacia gradual-transition annotation under a common recording setup.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Class-specific intervals and uncertainty labels will reduce artificial disagreements compared with a single-timestamp codebook.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — Listener estimates of Clock acquisition will vary more than annotator estimates of Ankora's first attack.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — A phase-boundary model that appears strong with one consensus reference may weaken when plausible alternate annotations are propagated through analysis.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — A robust KLAAT phase effect, if present, will survive held-out performance testing and reasonable annotation/tolerance choices.
PLAUSIBLE/TESTABLE — A pulse-conditional segmentation model may outperform fixed acoustic-cue weights, but only after independent labels and uncertainty are preserved.


25. SPECULATION
SPECULATION — The most characteristic KLAAT transitions may be perceptual zones whose width is itself compositionally meaningful.
SPECULATION — A future performance notation could encode an intended transition window and a range of acceptable gestures rather than a rigid bar number.
SPECULATION — Once reliable labels exist, KLAAT may find that a small number of Field and Clock cues explain most listener segmentation, allowing a simpler performance grammar.
SPECULATION — Disagreement between listeners could reveal meaningful differences in meter or attentional focus rather than mere noise.


26. UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS
UNSUPPORTED — A consensus timestamp is the objectively true time of a listener's phase change.
UNSUPPORTED — A high pooled agreement score proves the four-phase architecture is perceived.
UNSUPPORTED — More decimal places in an onset label mean greater physiological precision.
UNSUPPORTED — A single annotated take validates KSV-0.1.
UNSUPPORTED — Statistical significance from many windows in one take constitutes independent replication.
UNSUPPORTED — A response that survives annotation sensitivity checks establishes a neural or somatic mechanism.
UNSUPPORTED — An omitted Ankora strike supplies tactile impact.
UNSUPPORTED — KLAAT induces theta, heals tissue, resets the nervous system, or causes therapeutic activation by virtue of these annotation methods.


27. METHODOLOGY UPDATE — THE REFERENCE-DISTRIBUTION GATE
Add a REFERENCE-DISTRIBUTION GATE after the Event-Ontology and Uncertainty Gate. Before testing any proposed phase mechanism, freeze the codebook; collect independent annotations; retain raw marks; adjudicate only into a separate version; compute class-specific agreement; report timing-offset and interval uncertainty; and rerun primary comparisons under predeclared plausible annotation alternatives. Keep the three timelines of performed acoustics, intended structure, and listener experience independent. Distinguish descriptive sensitivity bands from inferential confidence intervals. Compare the provisional KSV against simpler time, RMS, density, and novelty baselines on held-out takes. If the apparent result depends on one convenient reference choice, do not promote it into the KLAAT methodology.


28. PRACTICAL KLAAT EXPERIMENT — KRX-076: THE REFERENCE-ROBUSTNESS AUDIT
OBJECTIVE: Establish whether a KLAAT annotation and phase-boundary result survives reasonable uncertainty in the reference record.
MATERIAL: Use the existing 66-second September reference recording if the actual analyzable audio can be obtained; otherwise use a newly recorded human-performed 8–12-minute Amastacia/Ankora four-phase take. Do not claim to have analyzed unavailable audio. Record microphone position, sample rate, recording date, and source identity. Paul privately logs intended phase zones before any listener test.
ANNOTATION: Give two independent annotators the same frozen v0.1 codebook. Each marks (a) Ankora attacks as point events with uncertainty, (b) Amastacia breath/spectral gestures as intervals, (c) Clock acquisition and Withdrawal as transition zones, and (d) ambiguous cases explicitly. Annotators first listen without waveform display, then do a separate waveform-assisted pass; preserve both passes.
RELIABILITY: For attacks, perform one-to-one matching at predeclared ±25 and ±50 ms windows, reporting matched/unmatched counts, F1, and median/dispersion of signed offsets. For Field gestures, report interval overlap plus start/end spread and category disagreements. For phase zones, report agreement on whether a transition exists and the distribution of its location/width. Keep each class separate. Do not label the consensus as infallible truth.
SENSITIVITY: Create three analysis versions: raw annotator A, raw annotator B, and a separately adjudicated reference. Where interval uncertainty is explicit, add predeclared earliest/latest plausible bounds. Recompute the same simple boundary-alignment statistic under every version. Do not select the best-scoring reference. If there are no listeners yet, run this as a dry-run analysis of annotation reliability only; report no listener outcome.
LISTENER FOLLOW-UP: Once event labels are sufficiently stable, ask a small group of consenting listeners to hear the uninterrupted take without KLAAT phase names. Collect open-ended reports, then blind macro-boundary marks on a later pass. Compare against the predeclared intended zones, with count-preserving and spacing-preserving chance baselines. Keep participant, take, and venue identifiers for later replication.
DECISION: A finding is provisionally robust only if its direction and practical magnitude remain similar across annotators, reasonable windows, and the independently performed held-out take. If agreement is poor, improve the codebook or recording before adding participants. If acoustic annotation is reliable but listener structure does not align, revise the performance model. If no KLAAT-specific effect is detected, preserve the null rather than inventing a mechanism.
DECISIVE QUESTION: DOES THE FOUR-PHASE JOURNEY REMAIN RECOGNIZABLE WHEN WE STOP PRETENDING THAT EVERY EVENT HAS ONE PERFECT LABEL AND ONE PERFECT TIME?
