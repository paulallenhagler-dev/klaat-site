THE BASS THAT DISAPPEARS WHEN STEREO BECOMES MONO
Why channel compatibility is a separate question from recording fidelity


Guiding principle: Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


RESEARCH QUESTION
Can a faithful-sounding stereo recording of Amastacia and Ankora lose part of its low-frequency character when reproduced by a single-channel speaker, a phone, or a platform that combines stereo channels? This session studies a delivery-chain problem, not a requirement to compose differently.


NOVELTY GATE
Recent published Sessions 151–159 examine weighting, hearing aids, modulation versus beat, listening context, spectral averages, reconstructed peaks, source-separation anticorrelation, familiarity, and selective attention. Session 94 previously examined comb filtering when microphone channels are summed. This session instead isolates a downstream distribution failure: stereo content that sounds acceptable in stereo can behave differently when a consumer device or distribution workflow collapses left and right into mono. This is narrower than general room response, spatial listening, or source separation. The archive search is targeted rather than exhaustive; overlap with Session 94 is acknowledged rather than concealed.


1. ESTABLISHED PHYSICAL PRINCIPLE
A common mono downmix is M(t) = [L(t) + R(t)] / 2. The actual implementation varies: some systems use different gains, only one channel, or other processing. If L(t) = R(t), the average retains that waveform at its original amplitude. If R(t) = -L(t), their sum is zero. Between those extremes, frequency-dependent interchannel phase and correlation determine which components survive. This is algebra, not evidence that any KLAAT recording contains such anticorrelation.


For two equal-amplitude sinusoidal components at the same frequency with phase offset phi, the averaged mono amplitude relative to either channel is |cos(phi/2)|. At phi = 0 degrees, it is 1 (0 dB); at 90 degrees, approximately 0.707 (-3.01 dB); at 120 degrees, 0.5 (-6.02 dB); at 180 degrees, zero in the ideal mathematical case. These are constructed pure-tone examples. Broadband musical signals and reverberant recordings cannot be summarized by a single phase offset.


2. WHY THIS IS RELEVANT TO AMASTACIA AND ANKORA
A low C#-centered drone with sustained harmonics can make a frequency-selective loss noticeable: a missing fundamental region may change the apparent weight of Amastacia even while breath noise and upper harmonics remain audible. Ankora's transient strikes are broadband; summation may affect their frequency balance and apparent punch differently. Stereo microphones at different positions can capture differing arrival times, reflected energy, and source balances. Stereo widening, polarity mistakes, and certain effects can exaggerate these differences.


This is not a claim that Amastacia's fundamental is invariably captured strongly, nor that all phone speakers reproduce it. Small playback devices may attenuate bass independently of downmixing. To attribute a missing bass component to mono cancellation, compare the same playback path using the original left, original right, and a controlled mono sum at matched levels.


3. WHAT A CORRELATION METER CAN AND CANNOT SAY
A stereo correlation coefficient near +1 indicates strong similarity between channel fluctuations; near -1 indicates strong opposition under the chosen analysis window. A value near zero can occur for many reasons, including unrelated signals and spatially diffuse content. Correlation is a broad diagnostic, not a frequency-specific guarantee of mono compatibility. A recording may have positive overall correlation while a narrow low-frequency band cancels strongly. Frequency-resolved cross-spectrum, coherence and relative phase can help identify that band, but their reliability depends on averaging, signal stationarity, and microphone noise.


4. PRIMARY AND TECHNICAL EVIDENCE
The underlying superposition principle follows linear acoustics and signal processing; it is not a recent neuroscience discovery. Classic primary research by Schroeder and Atal (1963), 'Computer Simulation of Sound Transmission in Rooms,' IEEE Convention Record, examined how multiple propagation paths shape reproduced sound. Blauert's Spatial Hearing (1997 English edition) is a synthesis of spatial-auditory experiments, useful for understanding why stereo spatial impression and mono spectral balance are different perceptual outcomes. These references establish context, not an empirical result about KLAAT or any particular streaming service. The numerical examples above are exact consequences of the stated downmix equation and do not depend on a claimed effect size from a music-listener experiment.


A practical limitation: the audible outcome also depends on playback frequency response, compression, listening level, and room. A poor mono result does not uniquely diagnose interchannel cancellation. Nor does a stable mono spectrum guarantee that the subjective spatial experience is preserved.


5. EVIDENCE TIERS
ESTABLISHED: Linear summation can reinforce or cancel components; stereo-to-mono processing may alter spectral balance when channels differ in phase.
CONTEXT DEPENDENT: Whether a particular recording loses audible low-frequency energy depends on its channel content and playback implementation.
PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESIS: A stereo capture of an Amastacia–Ankora improvisation could retain a full drone in stereo but lose some narrowband low-frequency energy after mono downmix.
SPECULATIVE: Such a change could reduce subjective weight, absorption, or movement impulse for some listeners. No KLAAT listener data establish this.
UNSUPPORTED: Mono playback necessarily destroys KLAAT, specific frequencies induce trance, or stereo phase predicts neural entrainment.


6. THE DISTINCT EXPERIMENTAL QUESTIONS
Acoustic: Does a measured band change in level after downmix? Measure band-limited RMS or spectral power with matched gain and fixed analysis windows.
Perceptual: Can listeners identify a difference when overall loudness is controlled and playback bandwidth is adequate?
Experiential: Do listeners rate bodily weight, activation, absorption, and enjoyment differently? These are independent outcomes, not proxies for brain state.
Causal: Can the difference be attributed to channel summation rather than speaker bass response or expectation? Use matched playback and blinded order where possible.


The distinction between stereo and mono is not an artistic prescription. A rough phone recording can be a valuable record of spontaneous technique; a carefully engineered stereo recording can still have distribution problems. Both deserve to be judged by the actual listening situation.


7. UNRESOLVED DIRECTIONS
What is the frequency-dependent L/R relationship in existing KLAAT takes? Are any stereo widening effects or multi-microphone delays present? Which distribution channels actually sum to mono? How much of the perceived bass difference comes from speaker bandwidth rather than summation? Would a mono-compatible version retain the original performance's energy without losing valued spatial texture? These are questions for recording analysis, not assumptions.


OPTIONAL OBSERVATION
Choose one existing stereo KLAAT recording. At a comfortable level, listen to left-only, right-only, and an equal-gain mono average on the same headphones. Note whether Amastacia's weight or Ankora's attack changes. If no difference is audible, that is useful evidence too. Keep the original untouched; do not change the music.
