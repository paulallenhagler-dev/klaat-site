THE SIGNATURE THAT SURVIVES A DIFFERENT INSTRUMENT

Can an improvised artistic identity be recognized when the entire acoustic rig changes?

SESSION 141 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

GOVERNING PRINCIPLE Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH QUESTION

KLAAT is not just the spectral character of one agave didgeridoo or the timbre of one elk-hide frame drum. It is also Paul's physical and musical way of making those instruments act together: how a breath-driven sound persists, how attacks accumulate or relax, how texture changes without becoming a predetermined composition, and how his attention moves through a performance. The organic pairing is Amastacia, the C# agave didgeridoo, with Ankora, the elk-hide frame drum. KLAAT also has an industrial pairing: a low G#/Ab PVC didgeridoo with a Remo Bahia Buffalo 16-inch drum.

Here is the unresolved question: if a listener hears spontaneous recordings from these two different rigs without being told their origin, could they perceive a common artistic hand? If so, which audible regularities would survive the change in instruments? If not, does that make the artistic identity less real—or merely reveal that an audible identity is not the same thing as an artist's intention, history, or visual world?

This is a study of a potential KLAAT-specific discovery, not an attempt to define the correct KLAAT sound. No score, tempo, phase sequence, required transition, or prescribed performance gesture follows from it. The question is what Paul's existing musical decisions might reveal when the instruments no longer supply the same obvious acoustic cues.

1. WHY THIS QUESTION IS WORTH A FULL SESSION

The recent research corpus has become very good at preventing bad inferences. Session 137 asked why Ankora's spectrum cannot uniquely reveal its physical construction. Session 138 examined aggregation across unequal listener groups. Session 139 separated a physiological response's observed time from its possible initiating event. Session 140 distinguished discovering an attractive acoustic-response relationship from independently confirming it. These are essential safeguards. But a research program cannot survive on safeguards alone: it also needs substantive questions about the actual artistic object.

The November 1, 11:20 CST hourly note identified a promising direction: KLAAT's organic and industrial rigs could be two different physical expressions of a common improvisational identity. That note is retained as provenance. Earlier work on Amastacia's material, and Session 132 in particular, cautioned against confusing plant provenance with bore geometry and playing behavior. An October 26 hourly note asked whether listeners might recognize KLAAT among other artists using similar instrumentation. That is a related but DIFFERENT test. Identifying an artist within one instrument family does not show that a listener recognizes the same artist when the instruments change.

The novelty gate was checked against the connected Master Instructions, Curriculum Index, Research Ledger, original Session Corpus and Volume II through Session 140, and the Library's hourly notes after Session 140. The later hourly passes explore finger contact, lip hydration, hide areal mass, cochlear timing, bore-temperature gradients and headphone crossfeed. None resolves the cross-rig artistic-identity question. The present session adds quantitative performer-identification research, a distinction between three kinds of identity claim, and a way to separate an instrument cue from a potentially transferable performance tendency. It is not a restatement of the hourly note and does not pretend that an untested KLAAT signature has already been discovered.

2. THREE CLAIMS THAT SOUND SIMILAR BUT ARE NOT

ARTISTIC CONTINUITY: Paul regards both instrument pairings as expressions of KLAAT. This is a first-person artistic and provenance claim. It can be true without a listening experiment. The artist's history, intention, aesthetic language and relationship with the instruments are part of the project even when the waveform cannot reveal them.

PERCEPTUAL FAMILY RESEMBLANCE: Some independent listeners, hearing unfamiliar recordings without labels, find that takes from the two rigs resemble one another in timing, energy, texture, or behavior. This is an empirical listener-response claim. It is testable, but it need not imply that listeners know the performer or that KLAAT has a unique signature.

PERFORMER-SPECIFIC IDENTIFICATION: Listeners or a validated model can distinguish Paul's performances from comparable performances by other people, including when the test recording comes from a different rig than the training examples. This is a much stronger attribution claim. It requires alternatives and careful controls. A collection containing only Paul's own recordings cannot establish that a feature is unique to Paul, no matter how coherent the collection sounds.

A fourth, stronger claim would identify a CAUSAL mechanism: a specific cross-rig performance property makes listeners recognize KLAAT. That would require a targeted manipulation or other credible identification strategy, not merely a correlation. None of these claims establishes trance, autonomic change, neurological entrainment, or a therapeutic effect.

3. WHAT CONTROLLED ORGAN RECORDINGS ACTUALLY SHOW

Gingras, Lagrandeur-Ponce, Giordano and McAdams (2011) studied whether unfamiliar listeners could group recordings by the performer who played them. Eight professional organists recorded a short unfamiliar organ piece. The listening experiment used six of those organists, with 30 short excerpts of roughly 10–14 seconds, including repeated and deliberately more or less expressive interpretations. Forty listeners participated: 20 with little musical training and 20 musicians. All recordings used the same organ and registration, limiting variation in timbre and dynamics. That made timing and articulation particularly informative.

Using a chance-corrected grouping measure, 20 of 20 musicians and 18 of 20 nonmusicians had positive scores; 15 musicians and 13 nonmusicians were individually above chance at the paper's bootstrap threshold. The groups did not differ significantly overall (reported t(38)=1.24, p=.22). Expressive interpretations were grouped more accurately than deliberately inexpressive ones. The authors linked listeners' grouping to temporal features such as average tempo and the overlap between notes. They also found substantial performer differences in mean articulation.

These numbers are easy to misuse. This was not a test of KLAAT, of two instruments, or of free improvisation. The listeners knew the number of performers and were allowed repeated listening and rearrangement. The organists played the same notated piece, making direct timing comparisons unusually clean. Nevertheless, the experiment is a valuable counterexample to the assumption that a performer's individuality must be carried by instrument timbre alone. Some expressive information survives when timbre is held nearly constant. It does NOT establish that the same timing habits survive a radical change in instrument.

4. ANOTHER EXPERIMENT SHOWS WHY A SIGNATURE MAY NOT BE ONE SIMPLE NUMBER

Gingras, Asselin and McAdams (2013) studied twelve professional harpsichordists who recorded three Baroque pieces, including different expressive interpretations of one piece. The researchers analyzed articulation, onset asynchrony, timing and velocity, using mixed models and similarity analyses to distinguish performer-specific from piece-specific influences.

The important result is mixed rather than tidy. Piece-specific effects were especially large for articulation; performer-specific effects predominated for some other parameters. Some players displayed consistent departures from the overall mean in articulation or velocity across pieces and interpretations. The authors described higher-level expressive strategies that could appear across different pieces, interpretive goals, or parameters.

This study suggests a more realistic picture than the idea of a fixed personal BPM or a permanent ratio of loud to quiet playing. A performer's style can include RELATIONSHIPS among decisions, not merely fixed averages. Someone may respond to increasing density in a characteristic way, even if the actual tempo and note pattern change from piece to piece. But the same study also shows why we should expect context dependence: the piece being played can exert a larger influence than the person on a given feature. If that is true even when the instrument remains a harpsichord, it is especially risky to demand that a personal acoustic fingerprint remain numerically unchanged across an agave/PVC and elk-hide/manufactured-head change.

For KLAAT, the relevant question might not be, 'Does Paul always play at a particular pulse rate?' It might be, 'Does a recognizable relationship between drone articulation, hand attacks, pauses and spontaneous re-entry recur across very different performances?' That is a hypothesis about a relationship, not an instruction to create it deliberately.

5. IMPROVISED RHYTHM CAN CARRY IDENTITY INFORMATION—UNDER SPECIFIC CONDITIONS

A particularly relevant 2024 study by Cheston, Schlichting, Cross and Harrison examined 300 recorded jazz-piano improvisations by ten pianists. The team extracted 19 rhythmic features spanning swing, complexity, feel, interaction and tempo. A random-forest model was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation with a balanced set of 30 recordings per pianist. It correctly identified the pianist in 59% of recordings, versus 10% chance for a balanced ten-class classification. The correct pianist appeared among the top three predictions in 80% of recordings.

That is a meaningful quantitative result: rhythm alone carried useful information about performer identity within this curated dataset. The model's important features included synchronization with the ensemble and rhythmic information density. The authors also found that several measured stylistic tendencies changed relatively little across individual careers.

It is equally important to specify what was NOT demonstrated. The dataset was limited to 'straight-ahead' swing-based piano-trio recordings, selected within explicit tempo and meter constraints. It did not test unaccompanied drone and frame drum, a single person playing two instruments at once, or identity transfer across instrument families. Its rhythmic features relied partly on source separation and a beat-tracking framework adapted to jazz. A five-fold result on a particular corpus is not an estimate of accuracy for KLAAT. The study's own discussion notes that averaging features across entire performances can obscure meaningful local variation, and that the selected jazz style limits generalization.

There is also a useful methodological warning. A supervised classifier trained on recordings by ten different pianists has ten possible identities to choose from. With KLAAT alone, a model can learn to distinguish organic from industrial timbre, or old from new phone recordings, without learning anything about Paul. The fact that a computer separates two piles of files does not tell us which causal factor it has discovered. For a cross-rig performer claim, training and evaluation would have to be separated by actual recording session and rig, and competing performers or otherwise suitable negative controls would have to exist.

6. HUMAN RECOGNITION IS NOT AUTOMATIC, EVEN WHEN THE INSTRUMENT STAYS THE SAME

Mitchell and MacDonald (2014) recorded five saxophonists performing three jazz standards. In their first experiment, only 52% of listeners correctly identified a previously heard target saxophonist from an audio-only line-up. In a second experiment, audio-to-video and video-to-audio matching supported more reliable performer identification.

The exact 52% belongs to that study's target-identification task; it is not a universal human recognition ceiling, and its chance baseline must be interpreted using the study's actual response procedure rather than casually borrowed from a two-choice example. The broader finding is that even a recognizable musician may not be reliably identifiable from sound alone by an unfamiliar listener, while seeing movement can provide additional useful cues.

This matters especially for KLAAT because the visual and bodily presentation are not decorative afterthoughts. Paul's handling of Amastacia and Ankora, his stance, the acoustic pressure and the archaeosonic visual world all participate in the artwork. Audio-only recognition is a legitimate scientific question, but it is deliberately narrower than the full artistic experience. A failed blind audio grouping task would not refute the coherence of the live ritual. Conversely, a strong response to the complete performance cannot be credited exclusively to the recorded sound without isolating the contributions of sight, expectation and setting.

7. THE INSTRUMENT-CONFOUND PROBLEM

The organic and industrial rigs differ in several obvious ways. Amastacia is described as an agave didgeridoo in C#; the industrial PVC didgeridoo is nominally lower, at G#/Ab. The natural elk-hide frame drum and manufactured Buffalo drum differ in head material, frame construction and response. These differences are not defects to be removed. They are part of the two legitimate acoustic identities within KLAAT.

But they complicate recognition. A listener who hears the same artist across rigs may notice a persistent way of building a drone against percussion; another listener may focus primarily on the different pitch center and membrane timbre and decide the recordings are unrelated. Neither answer is automatically irrational. The experiment asks about a listener's perceptual organization under particular conditions, not about an objective label stamped into the waveform.

Suppose a simple classifier is trained to distinguish Paul's organic recordings from his industrial recordings and achieves 95% accuracy. That would demonstrate that the rigs or recording conditions are easy to tell apart. It would say nothing about whether a stable performer identity crosses them. In fact, the more easily the instruments are distinguished, the more challenging the cross-rig attribution question becomes. A model could also achieve apparent 'Paul recognition' by learning the noise floor of Paul's phone, the acoustics of his usual room, or a consistent microphone distance. Those are recording signatures, not necessarily musical ones.

The correct comparison is not 'Can we detect a difference?' but 'What evidence survives when obvious instrument and capture cues are varied or accounted for?' The answer may be a family resemblance, not a uniquely identifying fingerprint. The distinction is scientifically productive: it protects the project's identity from both exaggerated claims and unnecessarily narrow definitions.

8. THREE POSSIBLE LOCATIONS FOR A CROSS-RIG REGULARITY

FIRST, TIMING RELATIONS. A listener might recognize how the percussion's local onset density interacts with the drone's continuity. There might be recurring relationships among attack spacing, accent irregularity, breath interruptions and the return of a sustained tone. Such relations could exist without a fixed BPM or a repeatable phrase. They are measurable in principle, but detecting Ankora attacks beneath a drone is itself an imperfect source-separation problem. A measured interval distribution is not yet a listener's perceived meter.

SECOND, TEXTURAL DECISIONS. KLAAT's sonic behavior may include when a rougher breath sound enters, how a buzz is allowed to thicken, or how a transient is left exposed against sustained harmonics. These decisions could recur in function while producing different absolute spectra in agave and PVC. This is a hypothesis about dynamic RELATIONS: when a texture changes relative to other events, rather than whether a fixed frequency or amplitude occurs. The raw spectrum will be strongly affected by the bore, membrane, room and microphone, so any purported cross-rig feature must be interpreted with caution.

THIRD, PHRASE-LEVEL AGENCY. Paul may repeatedly make particular kinds of spontaneous decisions: continuing rather than resolving a pattern, leaving a small opening in the pulse, moving from stable to unstable articulation, or recovering a drone after a difficult breath transition. The recurrence of such decisions might be part of his artistic hand. Yet a researcher who listens to many takes and selects only the ones that fit this description can manufacture an apparent signature. Session 140's discovery/confirmation distinction therefore applies directly. A poetic description can be a useful hypothesis generator, but it cannot substitute for independent listening or a predeclared acoustic comparison.

These three possibilities are not mutually exclusive. Nor are they exhaustive. The point is to investigate whether a common way of behaving musically exists, without treating any one behavior as mandatory for future performances.

9. A CRUCIAL DIFFERENCE: 'INVARIANT' DOES NOT MEAN 'UNCHANGING'

A rigid interpretation of a signature would require the same tempo, spectral centroid, loudness or drum-to-drone ratio in both rigs. That is neither scientifically necessary nor artistically desirable. A useful cross-context marker may instead be a relation that changes in a characteristic way when conditions change.

Consider handwriting. The same person writes differently with a broad marker and a fine pen; the physical marks are not identical. Some aspects of spacing, pressure or movement may nevertheless recur. This is only an analogy, not evidence that music has an equivalent biometric signature. Its lesson is that a cross-rig question is about a potential TRANSFORMATION RULE, not a demand for matching outputs.

For KLAAT, the hypothetical relation could involve how an attack becomes more or less dense as the drone grows rougher, or how pauses are placed relative to a breath cycle. We cannot assume that these relations exist. The industrial drum might invite faster rebound or a different hand posture, while PVC could alter backpressure and embouchure response. The same person may appropriately behave differently on each instrument. Such adaptation is not the loss of artistic identity. It is part of the performer-instrument system.

A future result might therefore take one of several forms: clear family resemblance despite different sounds; resemblance only within each rig; resemblance only when video or context is present; or no stable pattern across the available takes. Each would teach us something different. None obliges Paul to reshape the music to improve a score.

10. WHAT A FIRST REAL TEST COULD AND COULD NOT ESTABLISH

The low-pressure starting point is an OPEN, AUDIO-ONLY FAMILY-RESEMBLANCE QUESTION. Suppose there are several authentic, unedited recordings from each rig, made on different days. A willing listener hears a few clips in randomized order without being told which rig or performer is present. Before any rating scales, the listener is asked, 'Do any of these recordings seem related? What makes you think so?' The exact wording of the reply is preserved.

This would be a descriptive pilot. It might reveal that listeners talk about attack patterns, density, texture, or the same sense of forward motion. It might reveal that instrument timbre overwhelms everything else. Both are observations. They are not yet tests of unique authorship because all the recordings could be Paul's. They are not demonstrations of absorption, physiological activation, or trance because the question is about perceived relationship.

A stronger second-stage test would need other performers or suitable matched controls. For example, compare Paul's organic and industrial takes with other artists using comparable drone-and-drum resources, while keeping capture conditions from becoming a proxy for identity. Train any human familiarization or machine model on one set of sessions, then evaluate it on genuinely different sessions and, for the strongest claim, on the other rig. The classification target, chance baseline, balancing, and uncertainty would have to be defined beforehand. There is no need to build this experiment before the recordings exist.

One tempting but invalid shortcut is to chop a single ten-minute improvisation into twenty short excerpts and count them as twenty independent performances. Shared tempo trajectory, microphone sound, instrument state and room response make adjacent clips dependent. Session 118 already addressed this issue. Another shortcut is to show listeners a label such as 'ritual drone trance' and then ask whether the takes share that character. That tests responses to a suggestion as well as the music. If the goal is an unprompted identity judgment, the description must come afterward.

11. AN ILLUSTRATIVE CLASSIFICATION EXAMPLE, NOT KLAAT DATA

Imagine a balanced two-choice task in which a listener must choose which of two unfamiliar excerpts was played by a previously introduced artist. If the two options are equally likely and guessing is unbiased, chance is 50%. Suppose one person answers correctly on eight of ten independent trials. The observed proportion is 80%, but with so few trials the uncertainty is wide; it is not proof of a stable cross-rig signature. The exact binomial probability of eight or more correct under fair guessing is [C(10,8)+C(10,9)+C(10,10)] / 2^10 = 56/1024, approximately 5.47%. That is above a conventional one-sided 5% threshold. Selecting the most successful listener or the most favorable clips after inspecting the results would further weaken the inference.

This arithmetic matters because a few encouraging comments from friends can feel like a discovery. Such comments are valuable as real subjective experiences, but they are not independent evidence that strangers will recognize the same performer across different instruments. A larger, carefully controlled study could be informative, but even an excellent classification result would establish recognition under a task—not a particular brain state, somatic mechanism, or universal emotional response.

12. THE RELATIONSHIP TO FLOW AND LISTENER ABSORPTION

Paul often experiences deep involvement while improvising. It is tempting to argue that a distinctive cross-rig signature must be the acoustic imprint of his flow state, and that listeners who recognize it will enter a similar state. Neither step follows.

Performer flow is a report about Paul's attention and action during creation. Listener absorption is a different report about someone receiving the sound. A cross-rig resemblance judgment is a third task, about perceived common origin or style. A person might correctly identify Paul's music while disliking it; another might become absorbed without recognizing it; a third might feel energized while finding the two rigs completely different. Those outcomes can coexist.

This is not an argument against studying flow, trance-like attention or bodily energy. It is a proposal to keep the claims separable. If later recordings are accompanied by Paul's contemporaneous notes about effort, fluency or absorption, one could ask whether these states covary with any acoustic descriptors. If independent listeners also report energy or absorption, a different study could examine whether those responses relate to particular features. But it would be premature to collapse identity, flow, preference, activation and physiological response into one invented 'KLAAT effect.'

13. THE AESTHETIC QUESTION IS BIGGER THAN A BLIND TEST

KLAAT's distinctive world includes its instruments, their visible physicality, breath, pulse, sparse architecture, the archaic-modern visual vocabulary and the decision to call the experience a resonance ritual rather than a conventional sound bath. Some of that identity is not encoded in the audio at all. An audio-only experiment intentionally strips away a large part of the artwork in order to answer one narrow question.

There is an important asymmetry here. A successful cross-rig blind-recognition result would be scientifically interesting: it would suggest that something about Paul's playing carries across different instruments. A failed result would be much less sweeping. It could mean the feature is weak, the sample is small, the clips are poorly matched, the listeners are unfamiliar with this musical language, or the identity is fundamentally multimodal. It would not mean that KLAAT lacks an identity, and it would certainly not establish that Paul lacks talent.

The research program should avoid using scientific recognition as a substitute for artistic judgment. Science can test what listeners notice and how reliably; it cannot appoint a laboratory classifier as the authority on what KLAAT is allowed to become.

14. EVIDENCE STATUS: THE FIVE SEPARATE LEVELS

ESTABLISHED ELSEWHERE. Human listeners can sometimes group unfamiliar performers by timing and articulation when timbre is held relatively constant (Gingras et al., 2011). Performer-specific expressive tendencies can coexist with strong piece effects (Gingras et al., 2013). A supervised model classified ten jazz pianists from rhythmic features at 59% accuracy under the reported cross-validation design, versus 10% chance (Cheston et al., 2024). Audio-only identification of saxophonists was imperfect in a different human task, with 52% target identification in one experiment (Mitchell and MacDonald, 2014).

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT. Recognition varies with instrument, repertoire, performance expressiveness, recording quality, listener familiarity, task instructions, response options and whether visual information is available. A measurable performer effect within one instrument does not automatically generalize across instruments.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT. Some unedited organic and industrial KLAAT recordings may share relational timing or textural features, and some unfamiliar listeners may perceive that family resemblance without labels. Whether this happens, and which features predict it, is unknown.

SPECULATIVE. A transferable style may arise from recurring decisions about simultaneous drone and pulse rather than fixed frequency or BPM; performer-instrument adaptation may produce different surface sounds with a related deeper behavioral organization. These are hypotheses awaiting actual recordings and appropriate comparisons.

UNSUPPORTED. KLAAT already has a validated, unique cross-rig acoustic fingerprint; listeners can identify Paul across rigs above chance; any specific spectral or rhythmic feature proves his authorship; recognition implies trance, neural entrainment, autonomic activation, healing or special properties of C#; a failed identification test disproves the artistic coherence of KLAAT. No original KLAAT recording or listener response was analyzed in this session.

15. THE RESEARCH ADVANCE

The strongest outcome of this session is a new, durable distinction for the KLAAT program:

SOURCE IDENTITY is what instrument and recording chain produced the sound. ARTISTIC CONTINUITY is what Paul regards as part of KLAAT. PERCEPTUAL RESEMBLANCE is what listeners independently hear as related. PERFORMER ATTRIBUTION is what a controlled comparison can distinguish from other performers. CAUSAL EXPLANATION is a separate question about why a resemblance or identification occurs.

These five categories should never be treated as interchangeable. They give the project a way to investigate its distinctive identity without letting either romantic language or machine-learning scores outrun the evidence. They also open a more productive research frontier than endlessly multiplying minute material or microphone confounds. KLAAT can continue evolving; the analysis should follow the performances, not command them.

16. ONE OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION

If Paul already has even one authentic recording from each rig, keep both files untouched and listen to them on the same playback device, preferably without watching the videos. Afterward, write down one sentence answering: 'What, if anything, feels like the same artistic hand despite the different instruments?' Also write down one conspicuous difference. Do not try to make the takes match, and do not choose a new performance tempo or structure to force resemblance. This is a private artist's observation, not a listener study or a confirmed acoustic result. It can simply help identify a meaningful question for later independent listening.

The work remains experience-led. The instruments, the performance and the artistic intuition come first. Science is invited to discover what is genuinely there.
