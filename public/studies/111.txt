KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 111
THE HEIGHT THE SOUND NEVER OCCUPIED
Vertical auditory localization, learned ear-shape cues, and the difference between a spatial impression and an acoustic position
Published: October 22, 2026 — 3:25 PM CDT (America/Chicago)


EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH QUESTION
A listener hears Amastacia's sustained C# drone and Ankora's changing attacks. The instruments have definite physical positions, yet the sound may be described as overhead, rising from below, surrounding the listener, or simply having no identifiable direction. What does it actually take for an ear to locate a sound above or below? Why might the low drone and the drum's sharper, brighter events carry different spatial information? And when a listener says the music seems to come from “everywhere,” how can KLAAT respect the experience without treating it as proof of a special acoustic field or altered consciousness?


This session investigates the VERTICAL-LOCALIZATION ATTRIBUTION GATE. It is not about how far away a stationary source seems (Session 110), how large an instrument seems (109), whether a source seems to approach (105), or whether an abstract visual projection captures perceived sound location (hourly research, October 22 at 09:16). It asks a narrower, new question: how the listener infers ELEVATION and front/back position from sound reaching the ears, and why the answer can fail even when the sound is powerful and clear.


No KLAAT recording was newly analyzed; no listener response has been collected for this session. All numerical results below belong to experiments with other sounds and listeners.


1. A POSITION IS NOT A QUALITY


The physical source of a sound occupies a measurable position in a room. Its acoustic waves reach each ear after being shaped by source radiation, the floor and walls, the listener's body, head and outer ears. The listener may then infer a direction. These are different facts.


A listener saying “the drum came from above” can mean at least four things: an actual elevation judgment; a metaphor for the sound's scale or emotional lift; a spatial impression generated by the recording and playback; or a recollection shaped by a visible performance, artwork or prior expectation. A recording that evokes a vaulted chamber is not necessarily a recording of a high source. A sound felt in the chest is not necessarily arriving from below. A sound that seems “all around” does not necessarily occupy every physical direction or induce a neural state.


KLAAT's visual world deliberately invites large and ancient-seeming spaces. That world is legitimate artistic experience. The research task is to avoid translating poetic space into unmeasured geometry.


2. THE TWO EARS DO NOT PROVIDE A COMPLETE MAP


For left-right localization, the auditory system can use differences in arrival time between ears (interaural time difference, ITD), differences in sound level between ears (interaural level difference, ILD), and frequency-dependent filtering. Those cues do not by themselves give a unique vertical position. Multiple positions can produce similar ITD and ILD values, forming an approximate “cone of confusion” around the interaural axis. A source above, below, in front or behind may therefore share important binaural cues with another source.


Macpherson and Middlebrooks (2002) manipulated ITD by as much as 600 microseconds and ILD by as much as 20 dB in virtual-source tests. For low-pass stimuli, listeners tended to weight timing more than level; for high-pass stimuli, level differences became more influential. The authors also found that amplitude modulation and onset duration could alter the weighting of high-frequency timing information [1]. This experiment primarily concerns LATERAL ANGLE, not an automatic formula for elevation. Its importance here is negative as well as positive: knowing a sound's side does not mean knowing its height.


This matters for Amastacia because the deep carrier may convey little stable ear-specific elevation information even when it sounds commanding. It matters for Ankora because its transient includes broadband information that may offer more spatial cues than a low-frequency component alone. Neither inference can be confirmed for an actual mixed KLAAT take without inspecting the received signal and the listener's response.


3. THE OUTER EAR IS A PERSONAL DIRECTIONAL FILTER


The pinna, head and torso filter incoming sound differently depending on direction. In particular, reflections and interference around the pinna create direction-dependent spectral peaks and notches, often in the several-kilohertz range. The auditory system can learn the association between these patterns and positions. The relevant transfer function is commonly called a head-related transfer function (HRTF). It is not a mystical frequency map; it is a measurable, listener-specific acoustic transformation.


The pinna's filtering is not simply an equalizer applied by the instrument. It depends on the angle at which the wave reaches the listener's ear. A spectrum recorded by a microphone at the center of a room is not the spectrum at each ear canal. Even a technically excellent stereo mix does not automatically preserve a listener's own HRTF cues.


Butler and Humanski (1992) compared high-pass and low-pass noise separated at 3 kHz in median and lateral vertical planes [2]. High-pass stimuli were localized more proficiently than low-pass stimuli across their binaural listening conditions (reported p < .01). Yet low-pass noise could still support respectable localization in the LATERAL vertical plane, where binaural cues were informative. They also found that the importance of pinna cues varied with elevation; their data suggested reduced influence above 45 degrees. This is an instructive contradiction to the simplistic slogan “bass cannot be localized.” Bass may support direction under some conditions; what is difficult is treating low-frequency information as a unique, reliable elevation address, particularly near the median plane.


It would be wrong to transfer that 3-kHz split directly into a claim about Ankora. The drum has a complex changing spectrum, not a laboratory high-pass noise stimulus. Likewise, Amastacia has upper harmonics and breath texture, not only a pure C# fundamental. The actual spectrum at the listener's ears matters.


4. ONE FREQUENCY CAN POINT TO THE WRONG HEIGHT


Middlebrooks (1992) tested brief, one-sixth-octave noise bursts centered at 6, 8, 10 and 12 kHz [3]. Listeners' horizontal responses remained comparatively accurate, while their vertical and front/back responses clustered into systematic, frequency-dependent errors. The researchers measured external-ear directional transfer functions across 360 source locations and found that the patterns of mistaken directions were related to which directional filters best matched the received narrowband spectra.


The lesson is subtle: more high-frequency content is not automatically more accurate height perception. The ear needs an interpretable PATTERN across frequency, not simply an isolated high-frequency line. A prominent overtone or narrow hiss band might be audible and attention-grabbing without uniquely locating a source. A broadband Ankora attack might supply more useful cues than a sustained narrow spectral feature, but its own room reflections, microphone position and recording mix can distort those cues.


This finding is directly relevant to KLAAT's sound language. Amastacia can shift from dark, rounded resonance to breathy or sharply articulated texture while Paul maintains the same underlying drone. A listener might then report a changed spatial impression. That would be worth observing. It would not prove that the physical acoustic source moved, that the sound rose through the body, or that the brain entered trance.


5. WE LEARN OUR OWN EARS


A particularly revealing experiment by Hofman, Van Riswick and Van Opstal (1998) placed molds in participants' outer ears to change their normal directional spectral cues [4]. Elevation localization was severely disrupted at first. With continued experience, participants progressively reacquired accurate localization with the modified pinnae. Their earlier localization skill was not simply erased: participants could still localize with their unmodified ears when the molds were removed.


The result challenges any assumption that one spectral notch corresponds to one universal elevation for all people. Direction is inferred using learned, individual cues. A fixed recording cannot be assumed to make every listener hear the same overhead source, even when listeners agree about loudness, mood or beat.


This is one reason an apparently precise spatial claim can be fragile. A person may have learned a slightly different acoustic map because of ear shape, hearing history, room experience or playback familiarity. The experiment does not show that listening to KLAAT retrains spatial hearing; the adaptation arose from a sustained manipulation of the outer ears and real spatial feedback. It does show that elevation perception is an active inference, not a simple readout of the physical source's height.


6. HEAD MOVEMENT CAN CORRECT WHAT A STILL LISTENER MISHEARS


Wightman and Kistler (1999) compared localization when listeners' head movements were restricted or encouraged, and when sources could move [5]. Front/back confusions that occurred with restricted head movement were greatly reduced when head movement was allowed. In another experiment, movement of a source helped most when it was controlled by the listener rather than imposed externally. These results support the importance of sensorimotor information: turning the head changes the pattern of binaural cues in a way that can help resolve ambiguity.


Perrett and Noble (1997) examined low-pass sound localization under different head-movement conditions [6]. For front/back judgments, even slight head rotation could be informative; elevation benefits were less uniform across listeners. Some listeners benefited from rotation, some from natural movement, and some from both. This prevents a second oversimplification: head motion is not a universal cure for every height illusion.


For KLAAT, physical engagement matters. People may sit, stand, sway, turn toward the performer or listen while moving through daily life. These actions alter auditory sampling and visual context. A listener who turns toward Ankora may find its location more definite; a stationary headphone listener may not. A listener's movement can therefore change perceived source position without any change in Paul's improvisation.


The research does NOT justify telling listeners to move in a prescribed way or claiming that head turns enhance trance. It simply identifies movement as a variable to record when comparing spatial reports.


7. THE HEADPHONE PARADOX


Wightman and Kistler (1989) measured listeners' own transfer functions and synthesized headphone signals intended to reproduce free-field ear-canal waveforms at 144 source locations [7]. Eight listeners generally localized the virtual sounds similarly to real free-field sources, although headphone playback produced more front/back confusions and slightly poorer elevation definition. This shows both the possibility of credible virtual elevation and its limits: preserving direction-dependent ear signals is technically demanding.


Wenzel and colleagues (1993) then tested nonindividualized HRTFs [8]. After resolving large directional confusions, 12 of 16 inexperienced listeners achieved localization accuracy broadly comparable to free-field listening, but the remaining four did not: two had poor elevation accuracy in both conditions and two showed degraded elevation accuracy specifically with the virtual sources. Front/back and up/down confusions increased with virtual stimuli in many listeners.


This pair of studies is more informative than either alone. Individually measured HRTFs can produce convincing headphone localization, but even good simulations can introduce errors. Nonindividualized HRTFs can work for many listeners, yet a minority may have pronounced difficulties. These were purpose-built spatial stimuli; they were not conventional music mixes. A normal stereo or mono KLAAT file does not acquire reliable height coordinates merely because someone listens through headphones.


In an ordinary two-speaker playback system, the loudspeakers themselves provide physical direction cues and room reflections. In headphones, sound can be perceived inside the head, near the ears, or in an externalized position depending on recording and rendering. If someone reports a drone “above me” on headphones, the statement is an interesting experience report. It is not a calibrated measurement of source elevation.


8. WHY THE DRONE AND THE PULSE MAY DISAGREE


Amastacia and Ankora are not acoustically identical objects. Amastacia's low sustained carrier is accompanied by harmonics, breath noise, and vocal-tract-shaped changes; Ankora produces time-limited attacks and decays with a potentially broader initial spectrum. Their radiation patterns, positions relative to the listener, and interactions with a room can differ. An unedited performance therefore contains multiple candidate spatial cues.


Three distinct outcomes are possible. A listener might localize Ankora clearly and describe Amastacia as diffuse. A listener might hear both as coming from the same physical performer despite their different timbres. Or a listener might report a vertical or surrounding presence unrelated to the actual placement of either instrument. These are hypotheses, not observed KLAAT results. There is no reason to choose the most dramatic one in advance.


Ankora's sharper attack may help listeners orient toward the source, but the attack's high-frequency energy is not guaranteed to survive a particular microphone, compression process, playback speaker, hearing aid or reverberant room. Amastacia's harmonics might supply some directional cues, but their mere existence does not establish usable pinna-notch information at the listener's ears. If a recording has only a single channel, the room and playback arrangement may dominate apparent position. If it is close-miked and heavily mixed, the original source geometry may be absent from the file.


KLAAT's minimalism can make the spatial question especially compelling because there are few competing sound sources. But minimalism does not create a special localization mechanism. A sparse sound field may simply make its ambiguities easier to notice.


9. FIVE THINGS A VERTICAL-SPATIAL REPORT MUST NOT COLLAPSE


PHYSICAL GEOMETRY: Where Amastacia, Ankora, microphones, loudspeakers and listeners actually were, including source heights and orientations. This is measurable.


RECEIVED ACOUSTICS: The direction-dependent sound arriving at the left and right ears, including spectral shape, timing, level, reflections and head movement. A single room microphone is not a complete substitute for this.


PERCEIVED DIRECTION: What the listener reports as above, below, in front, behind, diffuse or indeterminate. It may be accurate, biased, unstable or metaphorical.


EXPERIENCED QUALITY: Energy, bodily salience, pleasantness, threat, absorption, spaciousness and imagery. These are subjective responses and should be asked separately from elevation.


NEURAL OR PHYSIOLOGICAL RESPONSE: Brain activity, skin conductance, heart rate, movement, or vibration measured with suitable methods. Neither perceived height nor perceived energy demonstrates these responses by itself.


The causal chain is not reversible. A reported overhead presence does not establish a high-frequency notch, an overhead loudspeaker, a specific HRTF, an autonomic event, or a state of trance. Nor does an imprecise direction judgment mean the music failed artistically.


10. THE CONTRADICTIONS ARE THE LESSON


One experiment shows high-pass stimuli outperforming low-pass stimuli for vertical localization; another shows that narrow high-frequency bands can systematically mislead vertical judgments. Both can be true: useful localization depends on the configuration of spectral information and the task, not simply the amount of treble.


Head movement resolves many front/back errors, yet its elevation benefits vary across listeners. Both can be true: movement supplies dynamic information, but not every vertical cue ambiguity is resolved the same way.


Individualized HRTFs support accurate virtual positions, yet even those experiments show some increased confusions, and nonindividualized HRTFs work much better for some people than others. Both can be true: headphone spatialization is possible, but it is not universal or automatically transferred to ordinary music recordings.


These are context-dependent findings, not KLAAT-specific effect sizes. Their value is to stop us from treating any single listening report as a universal property of the work.


11. THE EVIDENCE LADDER


ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT: Horizontal localization draws on interaural time, level and spectral cues; vertical and front/back judgments often rely strongly on individual pinna/head-related spectral information; altered outer-ear filtering can disrupt elevation judgments and support relearning; head motion can reduce front/back confusions; individualized virtual rendering can preserve much, but not all, free-field localization behavior [1–8].


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: A 3-kHz high/low-pass contrast produced significant differences in Butler and Humanski's stimuli; Middlebrooks' one-sixth-octave 6–12-kHz bands caused systematic frequency-specific mislocalizations; Wenzel's 12/16 result concerns a particular virtual-audio test after confusions were resolved. None is a KLAAT detection threshold, a guaranteed outcome for a frame drum, or a universal headphone-performance statistic.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: Listeners may report different spatial precision for Amastacia and Ankora; high-frequency attack and breath texture may contribute to direction judgments differently from the low drone; live room position, head movement and playback method may alter those reports without changing the performance.


SPECULATION: Ambiguous elevation and front/back cues could contribute to the sensation of an immense, unlocated, or archaeosonic presence. This could become one aspect of KLAAT's visual and sonic world. It is an aesthetic interpretation, not evidence of ancient ritual continuity, altered neural states or an objectively overhead source.


UNSUPPORTED: C# itself produces overhead localization; an elk-hide drum makes listeners hear sound from below; headphones automatically recreate the original vertical acoustic field; high frequencies always improve localization; the drone physically fills every height in the room; a reported vertical presence demonstrates entrainment, trance or bodily vibration; or KLAAT has been shown to cause any of these effects. No such KLAAT-specific test has been performed.


12. NOVELTY AND RESEARCH CONTINUITY


The current sequence has examined source size (109) and stationary apparent distance (110). Those are different dimensions from source elevation. Session 61 addressed physical spatial pressure variation across a room, not whether a listener infers ABOVE or BELOW from individualized ear filters. Session 80 addressed playback fidelity broadly, not the contradiction between high-frequency spectral detail and height mislocalization. Session 94 addressed phase/comb artifacts in a multi-mic sum, not pinna learning. Session 95 addressed unequal auditory access. Session 96 studied seeing the strike; Session 105 studied looming; Session 107 studied eyes open versus closed; Session 108 studied unnoticed change. The October 22 09:16 hourly note studied VISUAL capture of apparent horizontal sound location by an abstract projection; it did not isolate the spectral and sensorimotor constraints on auditory elevation. Other recent hourly notes addressed startle, air leaks, sound offsets, flanking transmission, ear-level exposure, cochlear implants, and group movement. No indexed dedicated elevation-localization session or recent hourly duplicate was found.


The strongest unresolved KLAAT question is not whether the sound can be engineered to seem overhead. It is whether an authentic recording or live performance elicits a stable, reproducible vertical-location report at all—and whether that report survives changes of listener, room and playback. The answer may be no. That is a useful result, not a failed aesthetic.


13. ONE OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — WHERE, IF ANYWHERE?


After one unchanged, comfortable-level KLAAT performance or recording, ask a consenting listener one open question before showing any visual: “Did the sound seem to come from any particular place, or not really?” Let them answer without a diagram or categories. Only afterward, if they spontaneously mention location, ask separately whether they mean a physical direction, a felt image, or a metaphor. Note whether they describe Amastacia and Ankora differently, and whether the sound seemed above/below, near/far, or simply enveloping. Record whether the listening was live, loudspeaker or headphone; the approximate physical geometry; whether the listener moved their head; and whether the instruments or artwork were visible. “No clear location” and “I don't know” are valid results. Do not ask anyone to change posture, use unsafe volume, or follow a score.


This observation does not require altering one note of KLAAT. It asks what a listener's perceptual system makes of the sound that already exists.


THE RETURN


Amastacia has a body. Ankora has a membrane. Their positions in a room are real. But the place a listener hears is assembled from the acoustics reaching two ears, from learned experience, and from the circumstances of listening. Sometimes that construction is precise. Sometimes it is wonderfully uncertain. KLAAT can welcome the uncertainty without misnaming it a measured physical force.


Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.
