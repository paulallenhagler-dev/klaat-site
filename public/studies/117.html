THE SKIN THAT DOES NOT HOLD STILL

Humidity, membrane tension, and the changing acoustic identity of Ankora

SESSION 117 / 164Body & PerformanceRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS A critical synthesis of primary experiments on animal-skin musical membranes, collagen hydration, and drumhead vibration. No KLAAT-specific measurements or listener outcomes are reported. The proposed application to Ankora is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated effect.

OPENING: THE SAME HAND, A DIFFERENT DRUM

Imagine that Paul plays Ankora one afternoon, sets it aside, and returns to it in another room or on another day. He does not deliberately alter his stroke. He has not changed the drumhead or written a new rhythmic passage. Nevertheless, the pulse seems softer, lower, less sharp, or perhaps more resonant. An easy explanation would be that his playing changed. Another would be that the recording changed. But Ankora has an additional, physically plausible source of variation: its elk-hide membrane is a biological material that exchanges moisture with its surroundings.

That is the focus of this session. The question is not whether weather controls the artistic direction of KLAAT, or whether one humidity level is ideal for trance. It is narrower and more useful: when an authentic performance sounds different, how much of the difference could come from a changing instrument rather than from a changed performance, microphone, or listener? A drum is not an invariant reference tone. In a natural-skin instrument, the sounding surface is a material state that can evolve while the musical identity remains continuous.

The practical significance is unusually direct. KLAAT's central instruments are Amastacia, the C# agave didgeridoo and continuous Drone, and Ankora, the elk-hide frame drum and Pulse. We have studied their interaction as rhythm, acoustic pressure, physical vibration, temporal expectation, and perception. We have also examined Ankora's membrane radiation, sympathetic response, and auditory–tactile frequency relationships. What has remained comparatively underdeveloped is the possibility that the material properties of Ankora itself vary between sessions. A difference in attack or decay need not be an intentional compositional event.

1. THE CENTRAL PHYSICAL RELATIONSHIP

For an idealized, uniformly stretched circular membrane, modal frequencies scale approximately with the square root of membrane tension per unit length divided by areal mass, and inversely with radius. Schematically:

frequency ∝ (1 / radius) × sqrt(tension / mass per area)

This is a physical model, not a method for tuning Ankora by formula. A real hide is not perfectly uniform. It has directional collagen fibers, variable thickness, boundary irregularities, bending and viscoelastic losses, possible coatings, and a frame that may itself respond to climate. A hand strike also excites multiple modes rather than one isolated frequency. Nevertheless, the ideal model identifies the key competing changes: less tension generally tends to lower membrane resonances; more moving mass generally tends to lower them too. Moisture may alter both, while also changing stiffness and damping.

The relationship also prevents a common interpretive error. If a drum's dominant spectral peak moves downward, it does not follow that Paul struck more slowly or with less force. Strike timing is a sequence of event onsets; membrane resonance is a property of the instrument's vibratory response. The two are independent enough that a rhythmic passage can keep approximately the same onset intervals while sounding markedly different.

The model does not say every measured spectral peak will move by the same proportion. Real drums have multiple modes, and their relative strengths depend on where and how the head is struck, where a microphone is placed, and how the head is supported. An apparent change in 'bass' could be a true mode shift, a change in damping, a redistribution of modal energy, or a recording artifact. These possibilities require different evidence.

2. DIRECT EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE: A GOATSKIN FRAME DRUM

Li, Gower, Destrade, and Yun (2022) developed an optical, nondestructive method for estimating stress and strain in thin membranes. One demonstration used the intact goatskin head of a bodhrán, a traditional Irish frame drum. This is especially relevant because the geometry and natural-skin material are closer to Ankora than, for example, a metal cymbal or a plastic snare head.

In the reported dry condition, the drum's fundamental resonance was approximately 84 Hz. After deliberate hydration, it was approximately 36 Hz. That is a large decrease in that particular instrument under that particular treatment—not a forecast that ordinary daily humidity will shift Ankora by 48 Hz. The authors also estimated a reduction in radial stretch from 0.28% to 0.20%, stress from 3.79 MPa to 1.31 MPa, and effective Young's modulus from approximately 680 MPa to 330 MPa. The skin thickness was 360 ± 30 micrometers.

These measurements matter for two separate reasons. First, they show that changing the water state of an animal-skin drumhead can coincide with major mechanical and acoustic changes. Second, the researchers measured properties of the membrane itself, rather than inferring all changes from a recording of a strike. The causal interpretation is therefore stronger than 'it sounded different on a humid day.'

There are limits. The experiment involved a single demonstration drum and a deliberately hydrated condition. It was not a randomized sample of elk-hide drums, not an extended KLAAT performance, and not a test of ordinary Kansas City indoor humidity. The authors explicitly note that their approximate stress analysis did not account for a possible change in mass density during hydration. They also discuss the difficulty of applying simple models to anisotropic biomaterials. The 84-to-36-Hz comparison should be treated as a demonstration of physical sensitivity, not a universal effect size or a practical target.

An important methodological detail: the authors recorded struck sound with a cellphone placed about 10 cm from the head for part of their characterization. A phone recording can therefore help detect a sufficiently large resonance change under controlled conditions. It does not, by itself, identify tension, stiffness, or moisture as the cause. This distinction is directly relevant to the archival phone videos Paul plans to assemble.

3. A SMALL CLIMATE-CHAMBER STUDY: EXPOSURE TIME MATTERS

Siswanto, Abdullah, and Darmawan (2018) examined three goatskin kompang drums of approximately 25 cm diameter in a climate chamber at 50%, 70%, and 90% relative humidity. They measured membrane tension and vibration frequency at eight-hour intervals over 48 hours.

Their reported reductions in tension after 48 hours were 6.2%, 13.1%, and 21.5%, respectively, across those conditions. Corresponding frequency reductions were 4.6%, 18.7%, and 26.4%. These are results for their particular samples, baseline, exposure protocol, and analysis. They are not a dose–response law for every hide drum. In particular, a reported reduction even in the 50% condition must be interpreted relative to that study's initial state; it does not mean that 50% RH universally 'lowers tuning' by 4.6%.

The study adds something the bodhrán demonstration does not: time. Moisture uptake and redistribution need not be instantaneous. The drum's state can depend on recent exposure, not just the humidity displayed on a meter at the moment a recording begins. A dry instrument entering a humid venue may not sound exactly like an instrument that has been in that venue for two days, even when the current air reading is identical.

But this was a small study, using goatskin rather than elk hide, a particular instrument geometry, and extreme conditions at the upper end. The highest humidity condition is not a recommended storage environment. Nor should the percentages be transferred to Ankora without direct measurement. What survives the limitations is a durable hypothesis: exposure history may matter alongside present conditions.

4. TEMPERATURE, HUMIDITY, AND AGING ARE NOT ONE VARIABLE

Suzuki and Miyamoto (2012) investigated resonance-frequency changes in two sizes of traditional Japanese nagado daiko, with drum diameters of approximately 42.4 and 48.5 cm. Their experimental comparisons included relative humidity values of 30%, 55%, and 80% at a fixed 20°C, and temperatures of 5, 20, and 35°C at fixed 55% RH. Their report describes the humidity influence as larger than the temperature influence over those tested ranges. They also considered changes over months after assembly.

This result is useful precisely because it prevents an over-simple story. 'The weather changed' is not a sufficient mechanism. Temperature, relative humidity, the instrument's recent exposure, age, construction, and use can interact. Relative humidity is itself temperature-dependent, so a reading without temperature is incomplete. The Japanese drums are also larger and structurally different from Ankora; the reported ranking of effects is a result for those drums and conditions, not a guarantee for every elk-hide frame drum.

There is another interpretive trap: even if Ankora's resonance changes between winter and summer, a phone microphone's automatic gain control, its distance and angle, the room's low-frequency modes, and the performer's strike position may change too. A single pair of casual recordings cannot isolate these variables. This is why 'different recording' and 'different drum state' must remain separate candidate explanations until the evidence discriminates them.

5. WHAT THE SKIN IS DOING

Animal hide contains collagen-rich fibrous structures. Water molecules can bind to, move through, and alter the mechanical behavior of such materials. Kudo and colleagues (2017) measured water adsorption in collagen films using infrared microspectroscopy and a quartz crystal microbalance under controlled relative humidity. Their study observed changing water-related spectral signatures and adsorption behavior as humidity rose, including nonlinear behavior at higher humidity. This is not a drum experiment. It establishes a material-level reason why moisture cannot be treated as a cosmetic variable for collagen.

A natural hide membrane may respond to water by changing dimensions, effective tension, stiffness, internal friction, and mass. The relative importance of these processes depends on how the hide was prepared, how it was mounted, whether it was treated or sealed, and its current mechanical loading. Elk hide is not interchangeable with the goatskin, python skin, or laboratory collagen films in the cited work. We do not know Ankora's exact hide thickness distribution, treatment history, mounting tension, or moisture sorption curve.

The distinction between tension and damping is especially important for listening. Tension strongly influences modal frequencies; damping influences how quickly a vibration dies away. A listener might describe a drum as 'darker,' 'thuddier,' 'tighter,' or 'more ringing' because of a change in spectral balance or decay even if the dominant resonance barely moves. Conversely, a clear downward shift in one mode may be hard to notice during an energetic, noise-rich improvisation. These are plausible acoustic and perceptual distinctions, not measured KLAAT findings.

6. NOT ALL MODES HAVE TO MOVE TOGETHER

Research on non-uniform drumhead tension provides another complication. In a 2010 Journal of the Acoustical Society of America study, investigators examined how departures from uniform tension split otherwise degenerate circular-membrane modes. The analysis used interferometry and finite-element modeling and showed why rim-tension differences can affect practical tuning and mode patterns.

This is relevant to a natural hide because it may not absorb or release moisture perfectly evenly, and its fiber structure is not uniform. The same average humidity could, in principle, coincide with different local tension patterns depending on exposure, handling, and material history. One part of the head could change differently from another. If so, a microphone spectrum could show not just an overall pitch shift but changes in the number, spacing, and decay of prominent peaks.

That possibility remains speculative for Ankora. The cited drumhead-mode study does not show that humidity caused the nonuniformity; it shows what nonuniform tension can do to vibration. We must not combine the studies into a claim that an elk-hide membrane necessarily develops humidity-induced mode splitting. It is a mechanism to test only if the recordings actually reveal stable, reproducible peak changes.

7. A MATERIAL CONTRAST, NOT AN ELK-HIDE RESULT

A 2024 experiment on the Chinese leiqin compared python-skin membranes with polycarbonate-based imitation membranes across 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90% RH. In that apparatus, the natural skin showed greater humidity sensitivity in sound-intensity measurements and moisture uptake, while the imitation material was comparatively unaffected. The study used four soundboxes and a 48-hour chamber protocol.

This comparison helps explain why 'drum' is too broad a category for predictions. Material composition matters. A synthetic drumhead may provide a more stable reference under some conditions, but it does not follow that synthetic material is artistically superior, or that Ankora should be replaced, treated, heated, tightened, or made more uniform. KLAAT's material irregularity is part of its character. A variable natural membrane is not a defect merely because it complicates measurement.

Nor is the 2024 result a clean demonstration that natural hide always sounds better or worse. It measured a particular soundbox design, materials, and endpoint. It did not compare elk hide with a Remo synthetic head, did not evaluate audience absorption, and did not measure perceived ritual intensity. Its strongest use here is as a warning against transferring calibration results across membrane materials.

8. THREE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS THAT MUST NOT COLLAPSE

A. MEASURED INSTRUMENT STATE

What is the room's temperature and relative humidity? How long has Ankora been there? Does its struck response exhibit different modal frequencies, relative amplitudes, or decay times? Are those differences repeatable when strike location, support, microphone placement, and recording processing are reasonably controlled?

B. MUSICAL PERFORMANCE

How does Paul respond instinctively to the drum that exists in that moment? A slightly less resonant head might invite stronger strikes, shorter figures, or different attention; alternatively he may not notice or respond at all. These are possible performer–instrument feedback loops, not instructions and not established KLAAT behavior. An unchanged rhythmic intention need not produce an unchanged waveform.

C. LISTENER EXPERIENCE

Does anyone actually hear the difference? Does it alter perceived warmth, physicality, energy, immersion, or enjoyment? A spectral change is not automatically a perceptually important change. Even if a listener notices, the report is not a measure of bodily vibration, neural entrainment, autonomic response, or trance. The relationship between material state and listener experience requires its own listening test.

These levels can be linked in future work, but they cannot be substituted for one another. An observed resonance shift is not a listener effect. A listener's description of 'deep vibration' is not a membrane-tension measurement. A musician's sense of flow is not proof that the drum has entered a special physical state.

9. WHY THIS MAY MATTER FOR THE KLAAT RECORDING ARCHIVE

Paul's recordings were made primarily for his own listening and technique development. They are valuable as records of real improvisation. They were not designed as controlled experiments. That is not a reason to discard them. It is a reason to label the claims they can support.

An archival video might reveal that Ankora's low resonance seems different across dates. Before attributing the difference to the hide, the analysis would need to consider microphone distance, angle, phone model, automatic gain control, room geometry, drum orientation, striking point, hand force, and whether the drum was held, suspended, or resting against the body. The most robust features of a phone recording may be onset timing and broad spectral patterns, not calibrated low-frequency amplitude or mechanical tension.

There is also a meaningful negative result: if several authentic recordings made under different conditions show similar decay and modal relationships, the instrument may be more stable in ordinary use than dramatic hydration experiments suggest. That would be useful knowledge. The science does not require Ankora to be unstable; it requires us to leave that question open until measured.

10. WHAT THIS DOES — AND DOES NOT — CHANGE ABOUT KLAAT

It changes our model of the source. We should stop treating Ankora as a perfectly fixed acoustic generator whose waveform depends only on the hand. The skin's mechanical state is a potentially changing part of the performance system. This is not a new rule for Paul. It is a refinement of how we explain and compare what he already makes.

It does not establish that humidity causes KLAAT trance, that wetter skin produces deeper bodily energy, or that one climate optimizes entrainment. It does not establish that elk hide behaves like goatskin by the same percentages. It does not establish a necessary change in pulse rate, a specific mood, or a superior performance technique. And it does not make the aesthetic 'ancient' because the material is natural; KLAAT's archaeological and ritual atmosphere is an artistic language, not a material-science conclusion.

The most interesting possibility is subtler. The apparent continuity of KLAAT's musical identity may coexist with physical variability in its instruments. The same two instruments can sound recognizably like themselves while never producing precisely the same acoustic field twice. That idea fits spontaneous performance without romanticizing unpredictability or turning it into an unfalsifiable claim. It is a measurable source of variation to keep distinct from improvisation.

11. EVIDENCE GRADING AND CONTRADICTIONS

ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT Animal-skin musical membranes can show substantial changes in resonance and mechanical stress with hydration or controlled humidity exposure. Controlled experiments on goatskin frame drums and other hide instruments support this. Collagen takes up water, and its material response is humidity-sensitive. Nonuniform tension can alter modal structure. These are findings about studied instruments and materials, not Ankora measurements.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT The direction and size of a measured change depend on membrane species, preparation, construction, baseline moisture, exposure duration, geometry, strike conditions, and measurement method. Humidity affected the tested Japanese drums more strongly than temperature over specified experimental ranges, but that ranking need not hold universally. A direct hydration experiment can produce a far larger effect than ordinary room variation. Some spectral differences arise from microphone placement or performance rather than the membrane.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT Ankora's elk-hide state may change with its moisture history, influencing modal frequency, decay, and the relative weight of transient attack and resonance. Some differences across Paul's existing recordings may reflect instrument state rather than technique. A controlled within-instrument comparison could establish whether the effect is audible and repeatable in ordinary use.

SPECULATION Environmental changes in Ankora's response might subtly alter Paul's spontaneous choices or the listener's impression of pulse weight and energy. There is no direct evidence yet that these interactions are large enough to matter in KLAAT, or that they improve absorption or activation.

UNSUPPORTED 'Ankora's resonance drops by 26% in humidity'; 'elk hide has the same wet/dry response as goatskin'; 'a lower drum resonance entrains the nervous system more strongly'; 'humid weather makes KLAAT more trance-inducing'; 'natural-skin variability proves a unique neurological effect.' None follows from the cited work.

12. THE OPEN QUESTION

What would count as a genuine KLAAT finding? Not a dramatic graph from another drum, and not a single phone clip that sounds deeper. A defensible result would show that Ankora's acoustic response changes systematically with documented environmental history under reasonably repeatable measurement conditions, and that the observed change exceeds ordinary strike-to-strike and recording variability. A separate listener comparison would be needed to establish perceptual significance. An even further step would be needed to connect that perceptual change to attention, bodily sensation, or physiological activity.

The research program has already learned to distinguish acoustic pressure from tactile vibration, rhythmic onset from perceived beat, performer flow from listener absorption, and source sound from phone processing. This session adds another necessary distinction: source identity from source material state. The drum can be the same instrument and still not be physically identical from day to day.

OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION — KRX-117: THE UNCHANGED STRIKE

On two ordinary days when you happen to pick up Ankora, without adjusting or conditioning it for an experiment, note the room temperature and relative humidity if those readings are conveniently available. Before or after playing naturally, make three comfortable, similar taps near the same spot with the phone resting in approximately the same place, and keep the unedited clips. Record whether the drum has just traveled or spent hours in that room. Do not heat, wet, overtighten, or otherwise alter the hide to provoke a result. Later we can compare the broad resonance and decay patterns, with the understanding that different strikes and phone processing may dominate. If you prefer not to do even that, simply preserve any existing clip that includes a clear isolated Ankora strike and the date/room information you remember. No change to your improvisation is required.
