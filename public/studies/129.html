THE SOUND THAT NEVER ENTERED THE LOOP

Why an authentic performance, a stored phrase, and the sound in the room are three different pieces of evidence

SESSION 129 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

CENTRAL QUESTION When Paul eventually amplifies or loops Amastacia's C# agave didgeridoo while continuing to improvise with Ankora's elk-hide frame drum, how can we tell whether a sound heard by the performer or audience actually exists in the stored loop? More fundamentally: if a loop sounds thin, hissy, unusually bass-heavy, strangely processed, or rhythmically different, can that observation be traced to the instrument, the playing, the recording chain, the loop buffer, or the playback environment?

This is not an argument for a particular looper, mixer, microphone, sound, or score. It is a way to protect a spontaneously created musical event from being misdescribed by the machinery used to preserve or project it. KLAAT is allowed to be rough, breathy, repetitive, irregular, and physically forceful. A measurement should tell us which of those properties were present in the performance and which were added, hidden, or changed downstream.

1. WHY THIS SESSION EXISTS: THE NOVELTY GATE The canonical Curriculum Index and Research Ledger were reviewed through Session 128, along with the two connected Session Corpora and the October 28 hourly notes through 15:15 CDT. Session 128 concerned unequal-length loop recurrence and the listener's interpretation of resulting rhythm; it did not establish what sound a loop buffer actually contains. Earlier Sessions 58, 75, 80, 94, 95, and 112 examined acquisition provenance, annotation, playback, summation, auditory access, and camera timing. These are necessary but not sufficient for a live looper.

Recent hourly notes isolated specific failure modes: overdub retention (04:16), speaker-to-microphone recapture (05:19), recording-boundary quantization (06:17), sound-triggered recording onset (07:18), undo versus save (09:20), input versus track effects (10:20), and microphone-level versus instrument-input compatibility (15:15). This session does not repeat any of those as its main question. It asks the new, higher-level inference question: given an audience recording or a looper output, WHICH STAGE OF THE SYSTEM IS IDENTIFIABLE, and what minimum evidence would distinguish the stored signal from the original acoustic act? That question requires the earlier notes but cannot be answered by any one of them.

No KLAAT looper, microphone, portable PA, or audio interface has been selected and no authentic KLAAT recording was analyzed in this session. The examples below are device specifications and explicitly hypothetical signal paths, not measurements of Paul or KLAAT.

2. FIVE DIFFERENT THINGS PEOPLE CALL 'THE SOUND' An improvisation can produce at least five nonidentical signals:

A. ACOUSTIC SOURCE: the pressure waves produced by Amastacia and Ankora in their immediate environment, including the room's response. B. MICROPHONE OUTPUT: the electrical signal generated at a particular microphone position, with its frequency response, directivity, sensitivity, and susceptibility to handling or airflow artifacts. C. STORED LOOP: the audio actually written to the looper's memory, after whichever gain stages, routing choices, effects, quantization or other processing precede the record buffer. D. MONITORED/REINFORCED OUTPUT: the live microphone path plus stored loop playback and any output processing, amplification, loudspeaker and room contributions. E. RECEIVED EXPERIENCE: what a listener hears and feels at a particular position and playback level, shaped by hearing, attention, expectation, and context.

These are related, but they are not interchangeable. The sound heard from the speaker can include a live input that never entered the recorded loop. A saved track can be dry while the live monitoring is wet. A microphone can capture more low-frequency energy at close range than an audience member receives. A loudspeaker can reproduce a loop with a different spectrum and level than the source recording. A listener may feel energized by that result without telling us which part of the chain caused the response.

The scientific danger is a reverse inference: 'I heard it at the output, therefore Paul played it that way.' That inference is not generally valid. Multiple different internal configurations can yield superficially similar outputs, and some transformations discard information. One output recording cannot automatically identify every upstream cause.

3. THE FIRST BOUNDARY: AN XLR PLUG IS NOT A PREAMPLIFIER A microphone produces a relatively small voltage. Shure's technical explanation places typical microphone signals around -60 to -40 dBu, although actual output depends on microphone sensitivity, acoustic sound pressure and distance. A microphone preamplifier supplies gain; changing connector shape alone does not. In the official BOSS RC-5 specifications, the 1/4-inch inputs have a nominal level of -20 dBu and input impedance of 1 megohm. The RC-505mkII, by contrast, lists dedicated balanced XLR microphone inputs nominally at -40 dBu, with 3-kilohm input impedance and phantom-power capability, alongside instrument inputs nominally at -10 dBu.

Use 0 dBu = 0.775 volts RMS. The illustrative voltages are: -60 dBu = 0.000775 V RMS = 0.775 mV; -40 dBu = 0.00775 V RMS = 7.75 mV; -20 dBu = 0.0775 V RMS = 77.5 mV; -10 dBu = 0.245 V RMS = 245 mV.

Thus a -60-to--40 dBu microphone signal is 20–40 dB below the RC-5's published nominal -20 dBu input level: approximately 10–100 times less voltage. This is a nominal-level comparison, NOT a prediction that the RC-5 will be silent, that it needs exactly 20–40 dB of gain, or that any particular microphone cannot be used with it. Input sensitivity, microphone output, adjustable gain, noise, and performance level matter. Nor does a high-impedance instrument input automatically damage a microphone; the practical problem is often the available gain, noise performance, and appropriate signal routing.

Why this matters: a weak loop from an improperly matched microphone is not evidence that Amastacia's low drone lacks energy, that Ankora's pulse is too soft, or that the artistic balance is wrong. Conversely, sending a hot mixer or preamp output into an input intended for a much weaker signal may overload it. A clean acoustic performance can become harsh for electrical reasons.

4. THE SECOND BOUNDARY: A LEVEL CONTROL CANNOT RECOVER INFORMATION LOST UPSTREAM Imagine a microphone path recorded so quietly that later gain also reveals substantial hiss. Turning up the stored track amplifies both the captured performance and the noise already present. It cannot recreate a signal-to-noise ratio that was never captured. Now imagine an analog input stage overloaded before analog-to-digital conversion. Reducing the level afterward makes the distorted waveform quieter; it does not restore the missing peaks.

The opposite error is equally possible. If a close microphone emphasizes Amastacia's breath articulation or Ankora's attack, the loop may sound extraordinarily detailed at low volume, while a distant audience hears a different balance. Those details can be legitimate parts of a close-microphone artistic recording. They are not automatically faithful estimates of the audience's unamplified acoustic exposure.

A useful distinction is between CREATIVE FIDELITY and EXPERIMENTAL PROVENANCE. Creative fidelity asks whether the resulting sound is artistically compelling and recognizably KLAAT. Experimental provenance asks what chain produced the measured or heard signal. A distorted or processed loop may be wonderful art while being poor evidence about unprocessed instrument acoustics. Both judgments can be true simultaneously.

5. THE THIRD BOUNDARY: WHAT THE DEVICE PLAYS IS NOT NECESSARILY WHAT IT STORES The RC-505mkII illustrates why the word 'record' requires care. Its official owner manual describes INPUT FX applied to sound as it is recorded and TRACK FX applied to already recorded tracks. The official Parameter Guide adds a routing qualification: with INPUT FX INSERT set to ALL, certain LOOP/TRACK input-routing states can prevent the processed input from being recorded with effects. That qualification is documented for this device and parameter-guide revision; it is not a universal property of loopers.

Consider a hypothetical Amastacia phrase with a noticeable input effect. Paul hears the effect while playing. After he stops, he hears a loop that sounds different. Three possible explanations are that (1) the effect was never written to the buffer; (2) it was written but playback effects or output routing changed; or (3) the microphone and loudspeaker recaptured or altered the signal. A single phone recording of the room cannot reliably separate these possibilities. A direct track export, a separate dry reference, and a documented output capture would each answer different questions.

The practical implication is not 'never use effects.' It is 'do not mistake a monitoring path for a record path.' KLAAT can choose to preserve raw drone texture, deliberately process it, or move between the two. The point is to know which one was captured before attributing an effect to breath, material resonance, rhythmic change, or listener physiology.

6. THE FOURTH BOUNDARY: LIVE SOUND CAN RE-ENTER THE MACHINE A future amplified KLAAT performance might involve a microphone near Amastacia, a speaker projecting a loop, and Ankora sounding acoustically nearby. If the microphone also picks up the speaker, some already-looped audio can re-enter the record path. This is a physical feedback or recapture route, not necessarily a malfunction and not the same thing as digital overdub retention inside the looper.

Such recapture can change level, spectrum, timing, reverberation, and stability. Its importance depends on microphone direction, loudspeaker position, gain, room geometry, whether recording is active, and the actual routing. It can be a deliberate musical resource, but it complicates claims about what was 'played once' versus what the system repeated or re-recorded. The 05:19 hourly note documented this separate issue; here it is one example of why output-to-source reverse inference fails.

If a future audience member says, 'That drone grew and grew,' the report is valuable as experience. But possible causes include Paul changing breath articulation, accumulated overdubs, changing output gain, acoustic feedback, room response, or a combination. A report of increasing intensity does not identify its cause.

7. A SIMPLE MATHEMATICAL MODEL OF THE EVIDENCE PROBLEM Let x(t) denote the acoustic event at a reference point. Let M denote microphone capture, G the input gain and conversion chain, R the routing and recording operation, B the stored buffer, P the playback processing, and H the loudspeaker/room transfer. A simplified audience signal is:

y(t) = H{ P[ B( R( G( M{x(t)} ) ) ) ] + L(t) },

where L(t) represents any simultaneously audible live path. This is schematic, not a measured transfer function; the real system may include nonlinearities, feedback, several microphones, multiple tracks, and listener-position effects.

The central result is that y(t) does not uniquely determine x(t). If R did not write an audible monitored effect, that effect is absent from B. If G clipped, some original waveform information is irretrievable from the distorted recording. If H filtered low bass, a weak bass response at the audience microphone does not prove the stored file lacked low bass. If L(t) includes live Ankora, an audience recording may contain drum attacks absent from the loop. Without independent observations of intermediate stages, these explanations can be observationally confounded.

This is an identifiability problem, not a claim that music must be reduced to mathematics. The mathematics protects the freedom of the performance by refusing to blame an unexplained output artifact on Paul's playing.

8. WHY 'I HEARD IT' IS STILL REAL DATA An audience member's description is not invalid just because the signal chain is uncertain. There are at least four legitimate kinds of evidence:

MEASURED ACOUSTIC/ELECTRICAL PROPERTY: waveform amplitude, frequency content, noise floor, loop duration, track routing, microphone position, or output level. Requires an appropriate instrument or file and documented settings. SUBJECTIVE REPORT: 'the drone felt bigger,' 'the pulse was energizing,' 'I lost track of time,' 'I wanted to move.' These are experiences, not direct measurements of their causes. PHYSIOLOGICAL OR NEURAL RESPONSE: respiration, movement, electrodermal activity, heart rate, EEG, or other recorded outcomes. None can be inferred from the audio alone, and none uniquely equals trance. CAUSAL HYPOTHESIS: 'the looper's output processing increased bodily salience,' or 'the acoustic drone caused absorption.' These require comparisons that isolate plausible causes and consider alternative explanations.

One person may report an intense physical sensation when the speaker has little deep-bass output, because rhythmic attack, expectation, and attention can influence bodily descriptions. Another may feel a powerful low-frequency sound in a room without reporting absorption. Neither observation establishes a special property of C#, neural entrainment, or a therapeutic mechanism. The experience matters; its causal interpretation remains open.

9. THE FALSE CHOICE BETWEEN 'PURE' AND 'PROCESSED' KLAAT is an artistic practice, not a laboratory source generator. Amastacia's C# drone and Ankora's attacks have identity partly because Paul manipulates them instinctively. A loop that preserves one freely played phrase may allow a longer continuous sonic field while the hands do something else. A microphone can reveal textures that a distant listener would not otherwise hear. A little amplification can help a performance reach people without turning the Westside neighborhood into a concert venue. These are artistic and practical possibilities.

The science does not demand an unprocessed aesthetic. It demands truthful labels. An unedited room recording is one kind of evidence. A close-mic recording is another. A loop track export is another. A PA recording is another. A mastered release is another. Calling all of them 'the original sound' erases useful distinctions. Calling the processed one 'fake' would erase the artistic point.

For future research, the unedited reference is especially valuable because it leaves more explanatory possibilities available. For future releases, Paul is free to choose the version that best communicates KLAAT. Neither decision determines the other.

10. A MINIMUM OBSERVABILITY MAP, NOT A PERFORMANCE SCORE If equipment becomes available, a compact setup record can distinguish four questions without interrupting improvisation:

SOURCE: Which instruments were sounding, where were they placed, and was the reference recorded continuously? INPUT: Which microphone, distance/orientation, preamp or mixer, nominal gain setting, and input connector were used? Was phantom power required and supplied appropriately? BUFFER: Which input was actually routed to the loop track? Were INPUT FX committed to the buffer? Was any overdub, quantization, auto-record, or save operation involved? OUTPUT: What was sent to the PA or headphones? Were TRACK FX, master FX, equalization, gain changes, or acoustic recapture present? Was the audience recording made from the room or from a direct output?

These are metadata questions, not musical instructions. They need not be answered while Paul is absorbed in playing; settings can be photographed beforehand and written down afterward. No tempo map, score, prescribed phrase length, or forced performance phase is required.

11. TWO DISTINCT QUESTIONS FOR A FUTURE LISTENER PILOT First ask the ecological question: 'What did this actual KLAAT playback make you notice, feel, or want to do?' A spontaneous answer can describe the received work, even if the system is processed. That is a valid audience-experience question.

Only later, and only if a comparison is justified, ask the attribution question: 'Would this same excerpt be experienced differently through a direct reference versus a loop/PA path?' That comparison needs level matching, counterbalanced order, identical source material where feasible, documentation of the signal paths, and preservation of the untouched original. Comparing two separately improvised takes cannot isolate the hardware because Paul will not play identically twice. A phone room recording cannot serve as a direct export of the looper's stored buffer. A listener's preference cannot establish which version is acoustically truer.

An 8–15-person pilot could reveal usability problems and interesting response patterns, but it would not prove a universal effect or a neurological mechanism. Session 125's small-sample uncertainty still applies. A later study might compare playback paths; the first useful step is simply to identify them correctly.

12. THE CONTRADICTION THAT MATTERS Manufacturer materials often emphasize 'high-quality recording' and extensive effects, while the detailed manuals expose conditional routing, nominal input levels, and persistence operations. These are not necessarily conflicting claims. High-resolution digital processing cannot make a microphone-level signal become instrument level without a suitable gain stage. Nor can floating-point processing repair distortion that occurred before conversion. A device can sound excellent in one configuration and misrepresent an acoustic source in another.

The RC-5 and RC-505mkII are especially instructive because their published input architectures differ. The smaller pedal's 1/4-inch nominal -20 dBu inputs do not establish the presence of an XLR microphone preamp. The tabletop model explicitly includes XLR microphone inputs and phantom power. Neither specification says which is artistically better for KLAAT, which Paul should buy, or what the resulting sound will be. They show why 'a looper' is not one universal electrical or recording architecture.

13. EVIDENCE GRADING ESTABLISHED TECHNICAL FACTS: A passive connector adapter does not supply microphone gain or phantom power. The cited RC-5 and RC-505mkII input specifications differ. The RC-505mkII has distinct INPUT FX and TRACK FX pathways and documented routing qualifications. A stored file, a live monitor feed, and an audience room recording are not definitionally identical. Electrical gain applied after an already noisy or clipped stage does not undo the upstream information loss.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: The audible consequence of a nominal-level mismatch depends on actual microphone output, input sensitivity, gain and noise performance. The effect of room/PA recapture depends on geometry, routing and level. Whether a processing difference is audible or artistically desirable depends on the material, listener and playback system.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: A future looper could make an authentic Amastacia phrase sound thinner, noisier, more processed, or differently timed without Paul changing the underlying improvisation. A multistage reference capture could identify some causes. No such test has yet been performed.

SPECULATIVE: An artistically chosen loop/PA path might increase immersion or activation for some listeners by maintaining a continuous field, altering attack contrast, or revealing close-mic detail. The direction, size and reliability of any effect are unknown.

UNSUPPORTED: KLAAT has demonstrated neurological trance, bodily resonance at a particular frequency, therapeutic effects, a beneficial 137- or 184-BPM neural clock, or an objectively superior looper signal chain. It is also unsupported that an audience recording can identify the unprocessed source without additional information, that an XLR-to-1/4-inch adapter always works adequately, that the RC-505mkII always prints audible input effects, or that Paul already owns the equipment discussed here.

14. WHAT WOULD COUNT AS A REAL ADVANCE The first genuine advance would not be a new effect preset or a more complicated loop. It would be one documented, authentic recording in which we can distinguish the acoustic reference, the actual loop buffer, and the audience output. A second advance would be to discover whether the differences matter to listeners after volume and presentation context are controlled. A null result would be informative: if a loop path changes measurable spectra but listeners do not report a consistent difference, the engineering distinction still matters for provenance but may not be a major artistic concern under those conditions.

KLAAT's research program becomes more credible when it can say, 'Here is the part of the signal chain we actually measured; here is the listener response we actually collected; here are the mechanisms we cannot yet separate.' That is more useful than attributing every intense experience to low-frequency physics or every imperfect recording to the performer.

15. CONCLUSION — THE MACHINE SHOULD NOT GET CREDIT FOR THE BREATH, OR BLAME FOR THE MUSIC The authentic event begins with Paul, Amastacia, Ankora, breath, skin, space, and spontaneous choice. A future microphone and looper can extend that event into a longer, more portable KLAAT performance. But the chain between instrument and listener is active, not transparent. Sometimes it preserves a phrase; sometimes it alters it; sometimes it plays something that was never written to memory at all.

The research rule is simple: do not infer the source from the final output when the intermediate path is unknown. The artistic rule is equally simple: the path may be used creatively, and it need not dictate what Paul plays. Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-129: THE THREE-CAPTURE CHECK Only when a looper and microphone are already available, make one disposable 15–30-second freely improvised phrase at comfortable level. If practical, preserve (1) an independent unprocessed reference, (2) the looper's stored-track export or closest documented record-path capture, and (3) a separate PA/headphone output capture. Note whether the live input is audible during playback, and turn off playback-only effects when checking the stored phrase. Do not use a valued performance for destructive tests, and do not force a tempo, phrase shape, or formal structure. If the equipment is not yet available, no action is needed: continue preserving authentic phone recordings and label their capture method accurately.
