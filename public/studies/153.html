THE PULSE INSIDE THE DRONE Why an amplitude-modulation spectrum is not a map of the beat a listener feels

Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH QUESTION

When Amastacia sustains a breath-shaped C# drone and Ankora adds a moving pattern of impacts, the recorded amplitude rises and falls on several timescales. Can a modulation-spectrum analysis distinguish recurring acoustic fluctuations from the beat, groove, or bodily activation experienced by a listener? The crucial answer is that it can characterize certain fluctuations, but it cannot, by itself, identify the perceptual beat or establish entrainment.

1. THREE DIFFERENT FREQUENCIES

Amastacia's audible drone has an acoustic fundamental and partials, measured in hertz. Its loudness and timbral energy can fluctuate much more slowly as breath pressure, mouth configuration and articulation change. Ankora's strikes add transients that can recur at an average rate but need not form a rigid grid. These three things—carrier frequency, amplitude-modulation rate and perceived beat—must not be conflated.

For a simple synthetic carrier x(t)=[1+m cos(2π f_m t)] cos(2π f_c t), f_c is the carrier frequency and f_m is its envelope modulation frequency; 0≤m≤1 is modulation depth. Expanding the expression yields a carrier at f_c and sidebands at f_c±f_m, each with amplitude m/2 relative to a unit carrier. This is a property of an ideal linear signal model, not evidence that Amastacia literally produces those components or that a listener will tap at f_m. A drum pulse is not necessarily equivalent to sinusoidal amplitude modulation: its sharp attack produces broad temporal and acoustic spectra.

A common practical approach rectifies a waveform or takes the magnitude of an analytic signal, smooths or low-pass filters that amplitude estimate, then calculates a Fourier transform or power spectrum of the envelope. The result describes how much envelope fluctuation occurs at different rates, given the analysis method. It is NOT the ordinary audio spectrum of the C# drone. A spectrogram of acoustic frequencies and a modulation spectrum of envelope fluctuations answer different questions.

2. A NUMERICAL EXAMPLE, WITH ITS LIMITS

If the Ankora attack pattern repeats four times per second, a perfectly periodic attack-envelope train has a fundamental repetition rate of 4 Hz, corresponding arithmetically to 240 events per minute. Its modulation spectrum can also contain multiples of 4 Hz because attacks are narrow and non-sinusoidal. If two alternating accent strengths form a two-strike cycle, the envelope may also show a 2-Hz periodicity even while there are four attacks per second. A listener might move at 2 Hz, 4 Hz, 1 Hz, or not at all. These are distinct observations.

A 20-second analysis window has nominal Fourier frequency-bin spacing of 1/20 = 0.05 Hz. That spacing is not an accuracy guarantee. If the tempo drifts or the pattern changes within the window, a narrow spectral peak can smear or split. Conversely, a 2-second window has 0.5-Hz bin spacing and poorer discrimination of nearby slow modulation rates, though it better localizes brief changes. Zero-padding can make a smoother display without restoring lost temporal information. These are deterministic engineering examples, not KLAAT recordings.

3. WHAT THE PRIMARY RESEARCH SUPPORTS

Nozaradan, Peretz, Missal and Mouraux (2011) presented an auditory rhythm and measured EEG steady-state responses at frequencies associated with the stimulus periodicities and metrical interpretation. Their work provides evidence that brain responses can emphasize rhythmic organization, including perceived meter, rather than merely mirroring every acoustic event. However, an EEG frequency peak is a measured neural response under a particular laboratory task, not a general diagnostic of trance or a proof of subjective groove. The study used designed rhythmic stimuli, not free Amastacia–Ankora improvisations.

Large and Jones (1999) developed a dynamic attending account in which attention can synchronize with temporal regularity. It offers a useful theoretical explanation for how a listener may anticipate events, but a model of attending is not itself direct physiological evidence that a KLAAT listener has synchronized to any particular envelope peak.

Grahn and Brett (2007) compared beat-related processing using functional MRI and found involvement of motor-related networks in beat perception, even without overt movement. This supports a broader distinction between perceived beat and simple acoustic timing, but it does not establish that KLAAT produces motor activation, and fMRI activation cannot be read as an individual's trance experience.

The strongest conclusion across these lines of work is modest: auditory timing can be represented at multiple levels, and the perceptually salient pulse need not be identical to the rate of individual attacks. A modulation spectrum is useful as one description of the sound entering that system, not a direct measurement of its subjective output.

4. WHY THE TWO INSTRUMENTS COMPLICATE ANALYSIS

Amastacia's breath articulation can create recurring amplitude swells without a corresponding Ankora strike. Ankora attacks can be partly masked by drone energy in a mono mix. Room reflections can smear the envelope; automatic gain control or compression can change modulation depth; microphone distance can shift the relative contribution of the two instruments. If the signal is normalized frame by frame, natural changes in envelope depth may be suppressed. Conversely, room noise or recording artifacts may create fluctuations unrelated to playing.

A full-band envelope combines everything. Band-limited envelopes can be informative: a lower acoustic-frequency band might track the drone more strongly, while a higher band might emphasize drum attack noise. But the division is not perfect, and no fixed crossover reliably isolates these instruments without validation. A spectral peak at 3 Hz in a combined recording could arise from breath, drum strikes, an interaction of both, a periodic processing artifact, or a combination. Source attribution requires independent evidence, such as video or separate microphones, and careful handling of the audiovisual-bias problem identified in Session 146.

The envelope's Fourier transform also sacrifices temporal order: two passages with similar modulation-power distributions can feel different because accents, grouping, onset timing, and transitions differ. Session 150's warning about local versus global rhythmic organization remains relevant. For changing improvisation, time-resolved envelope analysis or direct onset annotation may be more honest than a single whole-piece spectrum.

5. EVIDENCE LADDER

ESTABLISHED: Signal-processing definitions allow an amplitude envelope and its modulation-frequency distribution to be calculated from audio. Ideal periodic envelope fluctuations produce corresponding modulation peaks. Temporal window length and preprocessing affect the result. Beat perception can reflect organization beyond raw event rate.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Under controlled rhythmic stimulation, EEG and behavioral research show beat- and meter-related processing. Results depend on stimulus, listener, task, attention and analysis. Transfer to organic improvisation and everyday background listening is not automatic.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: Naturally recurring relationships between Amastacia breath accents and Ankora attacks may provide multiple competing or reinforcing periodicities. Some listeners may choose a slower embodied pulse than the attack rate; others may hear a faster subdivision or no stable beat. This could help explain why the same KLAAT passage works as an energizing soundtrack for one person and a contemplative texture for another.

SPECULATION: Particular combinations of slow breath-envelope cycles and rapid attacks might encourage unusual absorption or altered temporal awareness. This remains a question, not a result.

UNSUPPORTED: A modulation peak at 4 Hz proves a 4-Hz brain state; a 3-Hz envelope produces trance; a given tempo causes autonomic synchronization; KLAAT has demonstrated neurological entrainment or therapeutic effects. None follows from audio alone.

6. WHAT A USEFUL KLAAT TEST WOULD ACTUALLY ASK

Start with an existing, untouched recording. Keep the raw waveform, playback level, sample rate and any processing history documented. Plot a full-band envelope and one or two justified band-limited envelopes, alongside manually checked Ankora onset times. Inspect short time windows around naturally occurring shifts rather than forcing the whole performance into a single stable tempo. Report the exact envelope detector, smoothing cutoff, window length and frequency band.

Then ask listeners separately: Did you perceive a recurring pulse? If so, could you tap it, or describe it as slow, moderate, or fast? Did you feel energized, absorbed, unsettled, or indifferent? These are separate response dimensions. If recording taps, retain individual time series and compare them with possible subdivisions, rather than assigning the closest FFT peak as the 'true' beat. Record listening device and volume, since playback can affect transient salience. A small exploratory test could generate hypotheses; it would not prove causality or generalize to an audience.

A negative result matters. If an acoustic modulation peak is strong but no one hears a stable beat, that teaches us that modulation energy alone is insufficient. If listeners agree on a pulse that is weak in the envelope spectrum, the grouping or predictive context may be doing more perceptual work than a simple energy fluctuation. Either finding would sharpen the research without making the performance less spontaneous.

7. ARTISTIC IMPLICATION

The aim is not to turn KLAAT into an exercise in generating ideal modulation peaks. The most interesting acoustic evidence may arise precisely when Amastacia and Ankora are partly independent: one holds a breath-driven continuity while the other finds a different kinetic path. Scientific measurement should follow those emergent relations, not prescribe them. The aesthetic of dark ritual trance and the usefulness of KLAAT as a daily-life source of energy can coexist as listener-dependent possibilities, to be observed rather than advertised as proven effects.

OPTIONAL OBSERVATION

On one existing recording, listen without looking at the screen and tap whichever pulse feels natural for twenty seconds. Only afterward look at the drum attacks. Note whether your tap follows every strike, every other strike, a breath accent, or changes freely. One observation is enough; it is not a score and not a demand to play differently.
