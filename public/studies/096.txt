KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 96
THE STRIKE THE EYE CAN HEAR
Visual causality, Ankora's apparent decay, and the difference between listening to KLAAT and watching it happen
Published: October 17, 2026 — 3:13 PM CDT (America/Chicago)


GOVERNING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


STATUS: Research synthesis and an optional future observational comparison. No KLAAT-specific audiovisual, neurological, autonomic, or trance effect has been demonstrated.


CENTRAL QUESTION


Paul strikes Ankora while Amastacia's drone continues. The elk hide moves, the hand rebounds, and the drum's acoustic energy begins decaying. A person hearing the recording receives one set of cues. A person watching the same moment receives those cues plus a visible cause: a moving hand, a moment of contact, and the movement after contact. Could the viewer experience the drum as lasting longer, carrying more weight, or pulling the body more strongly into the pulse even though the audio signal has not changed?


This is not a question about whether KLAAT should become theatrical. It asks whether part of an authentic performance's perceived sound is constructed jointly by ear and eye. If so, audio-only KLAAT and visually witnessed KLAAT are not merely the same perceptual event with a picture added. They are different receiver conditions. That matters to a project that belongs both in a live room and in headphones during ordinary life.


THE CENTRAL DISTINCTION


MEASURED ACOUSTIC DECAY ≠ PERCEIVED SOUND DURATION.
VISIBLE CAUSAL GESTURE ≠ ADDED ACOUSTIC ENERGY.
PERCEIVED GROOVE ≠ OBSERVED MOVEMENT ≠ ABSORPTION OR TRANCE.

2. THE PERCUSSION EXPERIMENT THAT BREAKS AN INTUITIVE RULE


Schutz and Lipscomb (2007) recorded a marimba performer making gestures conventionally associated with long and short notes. They separated the videos from the recorded sounds and recombined auditory and visual components across conditions. Listeners were asked to judge the SOUND'S duration, even when made aware that audio and video had been recombined. Ratings depended strongly on which gesture was visible, despite the acoustic durations being comparable. In that controlled setting, a longer-looking gesture could make a note sound longer without lengthening the measured waveform [1].


The surprising result is not that people admire a flamboyant performer. It is that vision affected a judgment the experiment explicitly directed toward hearing. An impact gesture can contribute to the perceptual interpretation of the auditory event itself.


This is a primary experimental result from isolated marimba notes, not a demonstration about elk-hide frame drums, long improvisations, or trance. The original study's audiovisual manipulation was deliberately artificial, while its presentation was intended to look like a plausible musical action. We should not assume the magnitude or even the direction transfers unchanged to Ankora.


3. THE MECHANISM IS NOT 'VISION ALWAYS WINS'


Schutz and Kubovy (2009) ran four experiments to identify conditions under which the illusion appeared [2]. Their key findings were unusually diagnostic:


• Impact gestures changed perceived duration for tested PERCUSSIVE sounds, but not for tested SUSTAINED sounds.
• The effect could persist when a percussive sound followed the visible impact by as much as 700 milliseconds in the tested arrangement; it disappeared when the sound came before the apparent visual impact.
• Motion AFTER the apparent impact was the important part of the gesture for duration judgments.
• Substituting the words 'long' and 'short' for the gesture did not reproduce the same effect, weakening a simple verbal-response-bias explanation.


That 700-millisecond figure is a condition-specific experimental finding, not a universal audiovisual tolerance window and certainly not a recommended synchronization offset for KLAAT video. The more useful result is the asymmetry: the brain's interpretation of a physically plausible cause-and-effect sequence mattered. A hand appears to strike an object, and a sound follows. The eye may help determine how the ear organizes that event.


A further experiment by Armontrout, Schutz, and Kubovy (2009) reduced the visual gesture to its essential features. A visible sudden change of direction near impact and the duration of motion after impact were more important than several other movement descriptors such as distance, velocity, acceleration, and jerk [3]. A related schematic point-light demonstration could reproduce the effect with minimal visual information [4]. The causal-looking movement mattered more than elaborate imagery.


This gives KLAAT a precise distinction: the natural rebound of a hand after striking Ankora may be perceptually relevant even if the audience is not attending to the performer's face, costume, or ritual symbolism. The hypothesis is about event perception, not spectacle.


4. WHY ANKORA AND AMASTACIA MAY BE DIFFERENT CASES


Ankora is an impact source. Each strike has a visible action, an acoustic attack, membrane resonance, and decay. The visible action offers a plausible explanation for the sound. Amastacia is different: a sustained, continuously regenerated drone, with changes in vocal-tract shaping, lip oscillation, breath articulation, and noise. There is no isolated hand impact corresponding to every change in its spectrum.


The 2009 experiments suggest that the specific visual-duration illusion should be more plausible for Ankora's impact-like events than for an unchanged sustained Amastacia tone. They do NOT show that sustained musical sounds are immune to all visual influence. A viewer can see Paul's breath, posture, facial effort, or instrument movement and form other kinds of expectation. Those would require different experiments and should not be smuggled into the marimba result.


KLAAT adds a distinctive ecological difficulty: the two instruments coexist. A listener may use the continuous drone to hear an Ankora tail as part of one ongoing texture. The perceived duration of the drum may not be a single measurable boundary at all. 'I still felt the strike' could refer to membrane decay, room reverberation, expectation, attention, memory, or audiovisual grouping. Session 96 therefore proposes measuring perceived persistence as a listener response while keeping actual acoustic decay as a separate measurement.


5. THE SECOND PRIMARY RESULT: SEEING A DRUMMER CAN CHANGE GROOVE


Eaves and colleagues (2019 online; 2020 journal issue) tested 36 participants listening to popular-music drum patterns [5]. They manipulated audio groove level and the relationship between audio and a point-light display of the drummer. The display could correspond to the sound, remain static, be shifted by 0.5 seconds, or show a movement pattern incompatible with the audio. Participants rated perceived groove and desire to move in response to the audio on eight-point scales.


Both the audio groove level and the visual-display condition affected ratings. Fully corresponding drummer motion produced higher ratings than the incompatible display conditions. The experiment also showed that the effect of high versus low groove audio on desire to move was not uniform across visual conditions. This is especially relevant because it concerns a drummer, temporal movement cues, and a subjective motor invitation, rather than only isolated note duration.


Important boundaries: the participants rated their DESIRE to move; the experiment did not establish that they actually moved more. The 0.5-second shift was an experimental mismatch, not a KLAAT production parameter. Point-light representations and popular-music patterns are not equivalent to watching an elk-hide frame drum during a continuous didgeridoo improvisation. Nor do these ratings demonstrate altered consciousness, physiological entrainment, or healing.


Still, the result gives a new KLAAT question: does seeing Paul's naturally synchronized Ankora motion alter a listener's sense of rhythmic invitation when the exact same audio is heard? If yes, that would help explain why a live KLAAT encounter and an audio release may have different forms of intensity without treating either as incomplete.


6. THE CONTRADICTION: AUDIO CAN DOMINATE, AND VISUAL EFFECTS DEPEND ON TASK


A broader primary study by Thompson and colleagues (2023) had a pianist and violinist perform short Western tonal pieces with different intended emotions, then asked observers to rate emotion in audio-only, video-only, and audiovisual conditions [6]. The musicians' movements were captured and presented as point-light animations. Participants could extract some expressive information from either modality, but when audio was present, musical/compositional features—especially mode in this stimulus set—were more influential than the performers' intended expression. Ratings were also more consistent across listeners when audio was present than in video-only conditions.


This is not a refutation of the marimba illusion. It is a warning against an overly broad conclusion. A visual gesture may strongly affect a narrowly defined percussive-duration judgment while contributing much less to a judgment of emotional character in a different musical task. The stimulus, question, and available acoustic information all matter.


Nguyen and colleagues (2024) provide a second corrective [7]. In two experiments using auditory and bouncing-figure rhythms, musicians, dancers, and controls showed different patterns of audiovisual distraction during synchronization. In the finger-tapping condition, musicians were more influenced by auditory distractors while dancers were more influenced by visual distractors. In the whole-body movement condition, visual distractors exerted greater influence across groups. A second experiment included 60 participants, 20 per group. Thus the relative weight of sight and sound is not fixed; it can depend on training, the kind of movement, and the task.


For KLAAT, 'watching makes it stronger' would be an unjustified universal slogan. Some listeners may prefer to close their eyes. Some may feel more absorbed without visual events competing for attention. Others may find that seeing a strike clarifies the pulse or intensifies its apparent persistence. Audio-only daily-life listening is not a deficient version of a live ritual. The two contexts may recruit different useful information.


7. FOUR LEVELS OF CLAIM — KEEP THEM SEPARATE


ESTABLISHED IN CONTROLLED NON-KLAAT STUDIES


Visible, causally plausible impact gestures can change judgments of a percussive sound's duration without changing its recorded waveform [1–3]. Audiovisual drummer-motion correspondence can influence reported groove and desire to move in tested short drum-pattern stimuli [5]. Sight and sound do not have one universal dominance rule; task and stimulus type matter [2,6,7].


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE


The duration illusion depends on the impact-like character of the sound, the apparent causal relationship, and post-impact visual movement. The groove findings depend on visual congruence, musical material, training, and the response being measured. Findings from marimba, drum kit, piano, violin, and laboratory moving figures cannot be assigned an effect size for Amastacia and Ankora.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT


Seeing Ankora's natural strike and rebound may change perceived tail duration or pulse persistence when the identical audio is presented with versus without video. Corresponding motion may change groove or urge-to-move ratings. The effect may be stronger for Ankora attacks than for relatively stable Amastacia passages. Individual listeners may respond in opposite directions, especially when visual attention competes with immersion.


SPECULATION


The visible continuity of Paul's body across many strikes may help some listeners hear the pulse as one unfolding physical act rather than as separate drum sounds. That may contribute to KLAAT's 'ancient living sound' impression or its sense of embodied agency. These are artistic descriptions and hypotheses, not established perceptual mechanisms in KLAAT.


UNSUPPORTED


The visible gesture physically prolongs Ankora's recorded decay; video automatically increases absorption; watching KLAAT produces trance; visual causality proves neural entrainment; synchronized motion guarantees more actual audience movement; a 700-ms video offset is acceptable for KLAAT; choreographing longer strokes is a scientifically supported way to intensify KLAAT. None follows from the evidence.


8. A SOURCE–EVENT–RECEIVER MODEL


The useful research model has five distinct layers:


SOURCE ACTION: Paul's spontaneous hand strike, rebound, and body motion; simultaneous Amastacia playing.


PHYSICAL SOUND: Ankora's attack and decay, Amastacia's spectrum, room reflections, and any microphone/playback effects.


DELIVERED MEDIA: audio alone, synchronized audio plus natural video, or live performance with additional room/tactile/social cues.


PERCEIVED EVENT: when the listener believes a strike occurred, how long it seems to persist, how the sources bind, and whether the pulse feels clear or inviting.


EXPERIENCE/OUTCOME: enjoyment, absorption, activation, urge to move, actual movement, imagery, and any physiology measured separately.


A change at the fourth or fifth layer is not evidence that the second layer changed. In a controlled comparison using the same audio waveform, that separation becomes the point of the experiment. In a live-versus-recorded comparison, by contrast, playback, visual context, social presence, room position, and tactile delivery all change at once; a difference cannot be attributed to gesture alone.


9. WHAT THIS ADDS TO THE KLS-001 EMPIRICAL PROGRAM


KLS-001 currently prioritizes authentic baseline takes and blind audio-first listener reports. That order should remain intact. Introducing performance video before the first blind listening response would change the original research question and could bias the listener's independent acoustic segmentation. Do not silently alter the locked pilot protocol.


Instead, consider an optional separate KRX-096 follow-up AFTER the audio-only baseline. Keep the same audio file and playback setup in each condition; use synchronized, naturally captured video for the audiovisual condition. Randomize or counterbalance order across participants; where repeated exposure would contaminate the comparison, use separate listener groups or matched clips. Keep loudness, excerpt length, headphones/speakers, and audio processing identical. Use unprompted impressions first, then distinct ratings for perceived strike persistence, pulse clarity, groove/urge to move, enjoyment, and absorption. If actual movement is of interest, measure it separately. Store the video condition and timing offset as metadata.


A technical trap deserves emphasis: extracting naturally occurring strikes from a continuous KLAAT take may leave Amastacia sounding throughout the excerpt. A listener asked to judge 'how long the sound lasts' may simply answer 'it never stopped.' The study must identify the object of judgment as the particular drum impact/tail, and ideally check that listeners can recognize it without turning the question into a leading explanation. Audio-only and audiovisual versions must preserve the exact same sound. Video editing must not introduce different fades, compression, onset trimming, or level changes.


A further control could compare the full performance video with a version in which Ankora's striking hand is hidden while the audio remains unchanged. This is more ecologically natural than adding an artificial 500-ms mismatch. But masking the hand also changes visual composition and attention; it is not a perfect causal isolation. No one control removes every confound. A clean null is useful: KLAAT may be strong enough acoustically that seeing the gesture adds little to these specific judgments.


10. WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE ART, WITHOUT GOVERNING IT


KLAAT's archaeosonic identity is not simply a visual theme pasted onto a soundtrack. It is a performance made by a breathing human body interacting with agave, hide, air, and room. Seeing that process may reveal genuine causal information to the listener. This can be studied without adding stage movements, prescribing hand trajectories, or telling Paul to manufacture a particular visual effect.


The study also protects KLAAT's two legitimate futures. A person watching Paul live may receive meaningful visual information. A person driving, drawing, walking, or working while listening to an audio release may be energized by Ankora's pulse and Amastacia's continuous texture without seeing any action at all. Those are not competing artistic claims. They are different listening ecologies, and each deserves its own outcome measures.


The decisive question is no longer whether the live show is 'more powerful' than a recording. It is more precise: WHEN THE SOUND IS HELD FIXED, WHAT DOES SEEING THE ACT THAT MADE IT ADD TO THE LISTENER'S EXPERIENCE — AND WHAT DOES IT LEAVE UNCHANGED?


11. OPTIONAL KRX-096 — SAME AUDIO, TWO WAYS OF ENCOUNTERING IT


If Paul already has a video of an authentic improvised passage, select one short section with several clear Ankora strikes and continuous Amastacia. Keep the original audio exactly the same in two versions: audio-only, and that audio with the synchronized performance video. Ask a few willing listeners for their own description before giving any mechanism explanation. Then, if they are comfortable, ask whether individual drum strikes seemed to linger, whether the pulse invited movement, and whether the experience was absorbing. Compare responses without trying to make them agree. This is an exploratory observation, not a proof, and it requires no change to Paul's playing.


If no usable video exists, nothing needs to be staged or recorded specially for this session. The research question can wait for an authentic take.


FINAL POSITION


Ankora's strike is an acoustic event, but its perceived persistence may be partly an audiovisual event. The eye can supply a plausible cause for a sound, and in some tested situations that cause changes what the listener reports hearing. KLAAT may possess this additional layer of embodied perception in live or filmed form. Whether it does, and whether it affects absorption or activation, remains to be discovered from KLAAT itself.
