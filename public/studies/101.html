THE BREATH CHANGEOVER THAT NEED NOT BREAK THE DRONE

Circular breathing as a two-pump coordination skill, and the difference between acoustic continuity and musical continuity

SESSION 101 / 164Drone & BreathRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS: Literature-informed technical and perceptual analysis. No direct measurements of Amastacia or controlled KLAAT listener outcomes are claimed.

OPENING QUESTION

When Paul plays Amastacia, the sound is not simply the result of an instrument vibrating. It is the product of a changing human air system, a vibrating lip valve, a resonant agave bore, and a performer who is also attending to Ankora's pulse. Circular breathing lets a wind player sustain a tone while taking in fresh air. But what actually changes inside the mouth during the instant when the source of outward air pressure transfers from lungs to cheeks and back again? Can the sound remain perceptually continuous even if the mechanics—and the timbre—change briefly?

This session treats circular breathing as a technical freedom rather than an obligation to play uninterrupted. The purpose is not to remove the breath from KLAAT, conceal every transition, prescribe a tempo, or impose a score. The purpose is to make the changeover more available to Paul when his spontaneous performance calls for it, and to understand what a recording could reveal about the sound already emerging.

CENTRAL FINDING

Circular breathing is not literally simultaneous inhalation and exhalation through one continuous airway. It is a rapid, coordinated transfer between two sources of outward airflow: air delivered from the lungs and air temporarily pressurized in the mouth by tongue and cheek movement. Nasal inspiration can occur during the oral-pump interval. Imaging research demonstrates coordinated movement of the tongue and soft palate, while didgeridoo acoustics research shows that the shape of the player's vocal tract also changes the instrument's harmonic spectrum. Therefore a breath changeover can affect both airflow support and spectral color. Whether any such change is audible, noticeable, aesthetically desirable, or associated with listener absorption in KLAAT is unmeasured.

1. WHY THIS QUESTION IS NEW IN THE KLAAT CORPUS

Earlier KLAAT sessions have investigated auditory restoration across a masked interruption (Session 83), adaptation and habituation during repeated exposure (Session 84), differences among listeners (Session 95), how microphones change the recorded sound (Session 94), and the distinction between performance, physical delivery, and bodily experience (Sessions 98–99). None of those questions is the same as the performer's *internal pneumatic handoff* during circular breathing. Session 83 asked whether the auditory system can perceptually bridge an interruption. Session 101 asks how an actual uninterrupted or nearly uninterrupted drone is produced and what physical signatures its breath transitions might leave. The issue is simultaneously one of anatomical coordination, acoustic filtering, motor learning, and artistic agency.

The October 19 hourly notes concentrated on within-listener repeatability, wrist optical pulse artifacts, and active noise-cancellation playback state. Other recent notes covered jaw load, hearing-aid processing, free-response coding, and Ankora membrane pitch glides. This session does not repeat those topics. Its novelty is the two-pump changeover and its direct relation to Paul's wish for greater fluency on Amastacia while coordinating Ankora.

2. TWO PUMPS, NOT AN ENDLESS LUNG

In ordinary outward playing, respiratory muscles establish the pressure difference that drives airflow through the lips into the didgeridoo. During a circular-breathing changeover, the mouth can act as a short-lived reservoir. The tongue and cheeks pressurize that reservoir, sustaining outward airflow through the lips while the nasal airway admits fresh air. The respiratory supply then resumes before the oral reservoir is exhausted.

This is a coordination description, not a fixed recipe. The amount of air stored, the tongue's trajectory, the shape of the soft-palate seal, the timing of nasal inspiration, and the lip aperture can differ among performers, instruments, and individual cycles. The performer does not need to consciously narrate these events while playing. In skilled movement, useful coordination often becomes available without step-by-step conscious control.

It helps to distinguish three kinds of continuity:

(A) FLOW CONTINUITY: outward airflow through the lips does not stop. (B) ACOUSTIC CONTINUITY: the instrument continues producing measurable sound, although level, pitch, and spectrum may change. (C) PERCEPTUAL OR MUSICAL CONTINUITY: a listener experiences the passage as one continuing musical gesture, regardless of small physical changes.

A steady waveform is not necessary for a musically coherent drone. Conversely, an uninterrupted waveform does not guarantee that a listener hears a seamless breath transition. The three outcomes require different observations.

3. THE IMAGING STUDY: WHAT ACTUALLY MOVES

Peng, Phillips, Dyke and Stewart (2015, published online 2014) used cine magnetic-resonance imaging to observe eight experienced wind musicians: six professionals and two experienced amateurs, comprising two saxophonists, two trombonists and four trumpeters. Each sustained a note using circular breathing in a 3-T MRI scanner. The investigators tracked five anatomical distances and the area of the oral airway in midsagittal images.

All eight performers showed a broadly similar ordered sequence involving movement of the tongue and soft palate and a period of posterior oropharyngeal occlusion. Relative occlusion time was significantly longer in the two experienced amateurs than in the six professionals (reported P = .01). Absolute occlusion time showed a trend rather than a conventionally significant result (P = .08). A comparison of cycle times across instrument groups was not significant (reported P = .2), and the apparent trumpet-group difference changed when the amateurs were removed.

This is unusually direct anatomical evidence, but its limits are just as important. Eight people are too few to define a universal ideal. The two-amateur comparison is especially fragile; professional status is confounded with instrument, individual anatomy, training history and small sample size. Playing supine inside an MRI scanner is not playing a C# agave didgeridoo while striking an elk-hide drum. The imaging identifies a plausible mechanism and coordination sequence. It does not establish that a shorter closure is always better, that Paul should imitate one anatomy, or that any specific technique produces trance.

A separate conference MRI demonstration by Wiggins and Storey (2010) visualized circular-breathing mechanics during didgeridoo performance, while ultrasound demonstration footage from the Guenther laboratory shows the audible nasal sniff occurring alongside cheek deflation and forward tongue movement. These are valuable convergent observations but not controlled comparisons of KLAAT techniques or listeners. The demonstration should not be inflated into an outcome study.

4. WHY THE SAME BREATH CAN CHANGE THE SOUND'S COLOR

A didgeridoo's sound is shaped not only by its bore and vibrating lips, but also by the changing acoustic impedance of the player's vocal tract. In a 2005 Nature report and a more extensive 2006 Journal of the Acoustical Society of America experiment, Tarnopolsky and colleagues simultaneously measured the radiated didgeridoo sound and the player's vocal-tract impedance.

They found that pronounced spectral features of the output related systematically to impedance features of the tract near the lips. The more detailed 2006 experiment reported that when the tongue approached the hard palate, strong vocal-tract impedance maxima could occur around 1–3 kHz; those maxima tended to suppress corresponding upper-harmonic components, while lower-impedance regions allowed stronger components and formant-like spectral bands. High-speed lip imagery also showed that the lip valve could be closed for approximately half of each oscillation cycle in their recorded conditions, supporting the generation of upper harmonics.

These are instrument-and-player-specific acoustic observations, not an instruction to put the tongue at a certain spot or a claim that Amastacia has been measured at 1–3 kHz. They demonstrate an important principle: *the vocal tract is part of the sounding instrument.*

During a circular-breathing changeover, tongue, soft palate, cheeks and pressure supply are all changing. Even if Amastacia's underlying drone continues, its harmonic balance may briefly change. The listener might hear a tiny swell, narrowing of the upper harmonics, a breathy texture, a pressure wobble, a nasal inhalation, or nothing distinguishable at all. Some of these changes could be musically expressive. Others might be unintentional. The acoustic evidence does not rank them artistically.

The instrument's C# identity is not a biological trigger. In equal temperament, C#2 corresponds to about 69.3 Hz, but Amastacia's actual operating frequency and harmonic spectrum should be measured rather than assumed. The important mechanism here is the interaction among airflow, lips, tract and bore, not a mystical effect of the note name.

5. FOUR POSSIBLE CHANGEOVER SIGNATURES — AND WHY THEY MUST NOT BE CONFUSED

A useful future recording analysis could distinguish four physical possibilities.

FIRST, A LEVEL DIP: overall sound amplitude decreases during the handoff. This may indicate a pressure or lip-excitation change, but microphone placement, room reflections and gain processing can also cause apparent level variation.

SECOND, A PITCH PERTURBATION: the lip oscillation rate temporarily shifts. A pitch tracker may be unreliable during noisy or heavily modulated didgeridoo sound; its output should be checked against a spectrogram and listening.

THIRD, A TIMBRE SHIFT: the fundamental remains roughly stable while relative upper-harmonic energies change. This is consistent with vocal-tract filtering or changes in lip excitation, but neither cause is uniquely identified from a single microphone recording.

FOURTH, AN AUDIBLE NASAL OR AIR NOISE EVENT: a sniff, leak or breath texture occurs alongside the drone. That noise might be close to the performer's face but barely audible in the audience field, or it might become a distinctive part of KLAAT's texture. A close microphone can exaggerate the event relative to a distant listener.

A single changeover may exhibit several of these simultaneously. A recording with automatic gain control, heavy compression, denoising, or multi-microphone comb filtering may alter or conceal them. Session 94's recording-provenance caution therefore remains relevant. Importantly, the absence of a clear spectral discontinuity does not prove that nasal inspiration occurred, and a visible dip does not prove that circular breathing failed.

6. WHAT 'SEAMLESS' SHOULD MEAN — IF IT MEANS ANYTHING

There are at least three legitimate artistic outcomes. A breath transition can be acoustically unobtrusive. It can be clearly audible but experienced as part of Amastacia's living breath. Or it can coincide with a spontaneous gesture in Ankora that changes how the transition is heard. None is inherently superior.

The temptation is to convert research into a perfection rule: hide the breath, eliminate the noise, maintain an absolutely flat tone, and maximize the uninterrupted duration. That would be an unjustified leap. The research establishes the existence of a mechanical handoff and a tract-dependent timbre system; it does not establish that listeners prefer the least variable version. KLAAT's identity depends on breath, organic texture and the sense of a person actively making sound. Removing every sign of physical effort might actually remove information Paul values, but that aesthetic possibility is also not yet a listener-study result.

One genuinely useful technical goal is *choice*. If the handoff becomes reliable, Paul can allow it to disappear when he wants or let its audible trace become part of an improvisation. Technique expands the possible performance, rather than governing it.

7. WHY ADDING ANKORA IS A DIFFERENT MOTOR PROBLEM

The two-pump transfer already requires coordinated activity of respiratory, tongue, cheek, palatal and lip systems. Simultaneously playing Ankora introduces a separate limb-action system with its own timing, tactile feedback and impact mechanics. The question is not whether the brain can handle two activities in principle; skilled performers routinely coordinate multiple effectors. The question is how much attentional and motor interference occurs for *this performer, at this level of fluency, with these instruments*.

Three observations must remain separate:

(1) Paul's subjective sense of ease, effort or flow. (2) The actual sound of the drone and drum during a breath changeover. (3) Any listener's experience of pulse stability, absorption or activation.

Paul might feel a difficult breath handoff that is inaudible to a listener. Or he might feel deeply immersed while a recording reveals a brief level change. Neither outcome invalidates his flow state. Performer flow and passive-listener absorption are different constructs; neither can be inferred from the other.

Theoretical possibilities include temporary attentional competition, a learned coordination pattern, and shared timing cues between respiration and striking. However, a recording alone cannot identify neural motor programs, and no controlled KLAAT data currently show that Ankora is coupled to nasal inhalation or that such coupling affects listeners. There is no scientific justification for prescribing when a breath must occur relative to a drumbeat.

8. PRACTICE RESEARCH CONTAINS A USEFUL CONTRADICTION

A common motor-learning slogan says that interleaving difficult tasks is always better than practicing one component at a time. Music research is less tidy.

Mathias and Goldman (2024 online; Journal of Research in Music Education, volume 72 issue 4) recruited 20 advanced violinists; 19 complete datasets were analyzed. Each musician practiced short, demanding excerpts using blocked, increasingly interleaved, and randomized schedules. Acquisition was assessed immediately and retention 24 hours later. There was no statistically significant difference among schedules for immediate improvement (P = .791). At the delayed test, practice schedule did matter (F[2,36] = 5.16, P = .011, partial eta-squared = .22), and blocked practice showed *less deterioration* than either the increasing-interference or random conditions. The authors noted that the tasks were difficult, the practice methods constrained, and the result differed from some earlier contextual-interference findings.

That experiment was about violin excerpts, not circular breathing. Its relevance is methodological and limited: there is no universal evidence-based rule requiring Paul to combine every skill with the full performance from the first minute. A brief isolated skill exploration can be legitimate, as can returning to spontaneous whole-performance playing. Conversely, the violin study does not prove that blocked practice is best for didgeridoo, and the benefit of a technique should ultimately be judged by its transfer into actual music, not by how polished a drill sounds.

This contradiction reinforces the guiding principle: scientific findings may inform *how to learn a skill* without dictating *what music to make*.

9. MEASUREMENT DESIGN: THE CHANGEOVER-PROVENANCE GATE

If Paul ever wants to study the acoustic seam, the chain should be recorded explicitly:

A. PERFORMANCE: authentic spontaneous Amastacia playing, optionally with Ankora, without imposed phase or tempo. B. EVENT IDENTIFICATION: breath transitions marked only when independently observable or reliably identified by Paul; do not label every level dip as a nasal inhale. C. SOURCE CAPTURE: stable microphone position, fixed gain, unprocessed original audio, no automatic loudness normalization during comparison. D. ACOUSTIC DESCRIPTION: waveform envelope, short-time spectrum, harmonic ratios, apparent pitch, noise and time alignment; all with uncertainty. E. LISTENING: an independent listener reports whether they notice a change and how they describe it, without being primed to hear an error. F. INTERPRETATION: distinguish measured changes, perceived changes, and proposed mechanisms.

A short-time Fourier transform can make transient spectral changes visible, but window length creates a time-frequency tradeoff: a long window resolves nearby harmonics better while blurring the precise handoff; a short window localizes events better while making harmonic estimates coarser. A low C# drone is particularly vulnerable to overconfident instantaneous-pitch claims because low fundamentals require sufficient observation time. Comparing multiple window sizes and the raw audio is more informative than presenting one colorful spectrogram as proof.

If a listener notices no seam, the correct conclusion is not that the air supply was perfectly continuous. It is only that the tested listener did not report a seam under those listening conditions. If Paul hears a seam in headphones but not in the room, that could be a recording/playback effect or a genuine perceptual difference. Do not collapse the two.

10. EVIDENCE LADDER

ESTABLISHED, FROM PRIMARY RESEARCH: • Experienced wind players can use a coordinated tongue/soft-palate sequence during circular breathing; this has been directly visualized with cine MRI in eight musicians (Peng et al., 2015). • Didgeridoo vocal-tract impedance affects the relative strength of radiated harmonics; this was experimentally measured in didgeridoo players (Tarnopolsky et al., 2005/2006). • In one small study of advanced violinists, blocked practice yielded less 24-hour deterioration than random or increasing-interference practice; interleaving was not universally superior (Mathias & Goldman, 2024/2025 issue).

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: • The exact anatomical trajectory, duration and efficiency of circular breathing vary by person and instrument. Eight MRI participants do not define a universal standard. • The audibility of a changeover depends on actual spectral/level changes, room, microphone, masking, playback and listener. • Isolated versus combined skill practice may help differently depending on difficulty, prior fluency, fatigue and task transfer.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: • Amastacia may show brief spectral shifts at some breath handoffs without stopping its fundamental drone. • Greater ease in the two-pump transfer may free attention for Ankora, improvisational listening, and expressive decisions. • A listener may experience a transition as continuous even if a microphone reveals a measurable change, or may enjoy a transition that is clearly audible.

SPECULATION: • A distinctive micro-texture at the handoff might contribute to KLAAT's sense of organic presence or intimacy. There is no direct listener evidence for this. • Coordinated Ankora motion could sometimes perceptually mask a small Amastacia transition. This has not been experimentally tested in KLAAT.

UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS: • That KLAAT has demonstrated neural entrainment, trance induction, therapeutic respiratory effects, or an autonomic benefit from circular breathing. • That C# or approximately 137 BPM creates a special physiological response. • That a breathless, maximally smooth drone is scientifically superior or artistically required. • That the professional-amateur MRI difference establishes an ideal target for Paul's anatomy or a specific duration he must achieve.

11. IMPLICATION FOR KLAAT'S ARTISTIC IDENTITY

Amastacia is not an anonymous sine-wave generator. Her sound carries the changing relationship between breath, lips, tongue, agave and the surrounding space. Ankora is not a metronome; her pulse is made by a hand, hide and body in motion. The research does not ask Paul to suppress these physical traces or replace his instinct with a procedural sequence.

It does suggest a productive distinction between two kinds of refinement. Technical refinement gives Paul more freedom to sustain, interrupt, color, or redirect a drone when he chooses. Scientific refinement gives him better language for distinguishing an actual sound event from a perceived event and for knowing what an experiment would need to establish. Neither refinement dictates a composition.

KLAAT can remain a physical, energizing soundtrack for daily life and an immersive performance world. Sustained continuity may matter to some passages; in others, breath itself may be the event. The scientific literature cannot choose between them. Paul can.

12. THE UNRESOLVED QUESTION WORTH KEEPING

We still do not know what a typical Amastacia changeover sounds like under controlled capture, how variable it is across spontaneous passages, whether Paul perceives it as effortful, or whether an unfamiliar listener notices it. We do not know whether coordinating Ankora changes the mechanics or the sound. We have anatomical evidence from other instruments and acoustic evidence from other didgeridoos, not measurements of KLAAT.

That gap is narrow enough to test and useful enough to preserve. It is also an unusually good example of the project's guiding principle: begin with the lived performance; use science to make the artist more fluent and the observations more honest.

OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE KLAAT EXERCISE — KRX-101: THE UNFORCED CHANGEOVER

On a day when it feels comfortable, make one short, unedited Amastacia recording in which you play naturally and circular-breathe whenever you normally would. No prescribed tempo, duration, drone pattern, or phase structure; Ankora is optional. Afterward, note only one or two breath handoffs you remember. Listen to the raw recording at a comfortable level. For each, ask: Did the drone stop, change color, change loudness, include an audible inhale, or remain apparently continuous? Note what you actually hear, including 'not sure.' If you want a technical comparison, inspect the same short windows in a waveform and spectrogram, but do not force a breath event to match a visual feature. The goal is to learn what Amastacia already does and give your technique room to become more fluent. Stop if you feel lightheaded, strained or uncomfortable. This is an observation, not a requirement to perfect or hide the breath.
