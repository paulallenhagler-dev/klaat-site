THE PATTERN THAT FINDS ITS WAY BACK

How to recognize spontaneous recurrence in Amastacia and Ankora without inventing a score

SESSION 104 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS This is a critical synthesis of primary research in audio-structure analysis, musical pattern representation, and human annotation. No new KLAAT recording has been analyzed; no listener has been tested; no neural, physiological, trance, or therapeutic outcome has been measured. All numerical findings below describe the cited outside datasets, not Amastacia or Ankora. The purpose is to make spontaneous recurrence observable without turning it into a compositional rule.

CENTRAL QUESTION

When Paul plays an extended improvised KLAAT passage, something may return. It might not be the same sequence of drum hits. It might be a familiar density of Ankora attacks, a certain pressure and rasp in Amastacia, a breath gesture, or the way a handful of strikes seem to lean against a sustained tone. Perhaps the return happens ten minutes later, at a different speed, with different accents and a different room sound. Paul did not plan it; he may not notice it until listening afterward.

Is it meaningful to call that a recurring musical idea? What would count as evidence? And how do we prevent software from calling every persistent C# drone a repeated motif simply because the drone is present almost everywhere?

The central proposition is modest but useful: A PERFORMANCE CAN CONTAIN MEASURABLE SELF-SIMILARITY WITHOUT CONTAINING A FIXED FORM; A LISTENER CAN HEAR A RETURN THAT IS NOT SAMPLE-IDENTICAL; AND A COMPUTER CAN DETECT RESEMBLANCE THAT NO LISTENER EXPERIENCES AS A MUSICAL RETURN. These are three different outcomes.

1. NOVELTY GATE — WHAT THIS SESSION ADDS

The existing KLAAT corpus has already studied the difference between event density and beat, subjective segmentation, changes in predictive expectation, performer flow, rhythmic coordination, and retrospective memory. Session 87 asked where listeners hear chapters in an improvisation. Session 88 asked how a change revises what the ear expects. Session 97 asked how memory reconstructs the performance afterward. Session 103 separated walking vigor from beat synchronization. Recent hourly notes through October 20 at 07:20 CDT concern playback intermodulation, meter-detector time constants, time-varying room measurements, sound tolerance, camera rolling shutter, threshold gates, rhythm enculturation, and other distinct problems.

None of those is a dedicated study of LONG-RANGE WITHIN-TAKE RECURRENCE: whether a phrase-like acoustic configuration reappears, how to find it without assuming a stable metrical grid, and how to distinguish algorithmic recurrence from listener-recognized return. This is not another essay about the virtues of repetition. It is a method for finding out what actually recurs in a living, unscored performance.

2. FIVE THINGS THAT CAN LOOK LIKE REPETITION

First, there is SOURCE PERSISTENCE. Amastacia may remain present for minutes. A spectrogram can show similar low-frequency energy from one minute to the next. That does not mean Paul has repeated a phrase. It may mean only that the instrument's drone has not stopped.

Second, there is EVENT RECURRENCE. Ankora may produce repeated attacks with similar intervals or force. These events can be acoustically alike without constituting a recognizable multi-event figure.

Third, there is GESTURAL RECURRENCE. A rasp followed by a rounded overtone and a denser drum figure may return in an altered form. The order or contour may matter more than the absolute pitch, loudness, or duration.

Fourth, there is PERCEPTUAL RETURN. A listener hears something as 'that part again' even though its acoustic features are not identical. Familiarity, attention, memory, and grouping all influence this judgment. A listener can be right about their experience without identifying a sample-perfect recurrence.

Fifth, there is ALGORITHMIC SELF-SIMILARITY. A program marks two time windows as similar under a chosen feature representation and distance metric. This is a mathematical relationship, not a direct report of musical meaning. The algorithm may detect an unchanged room resonance, a repeated drum timbre, or an artifact of the microphone rather than a phrase.

A scientifically honest KLAAT study must name which of these it has measured.

3. WHAT A SELF-SIMILARITY MAP ACTUALLY SHOWS

A standard method converts an audio recording into a sequence of feature vectors. Each vector describes a short moment or window: spectral shape, energy distribution, transient activity, harmonic content, or another measurable property. Every moment is compared with every other moment. The result is a square time-by-time similarity matrix.

The main diagonal represents each moment compared with itself. That diagonal is trivial and tells us nothing about recurrence. Off-diagonal bright regions are more interesting. A bright diagonal stripe away from the main diagonal can indicate that a sequence of states reappears later in the recording. A broad bright block may indicate that two passages have similar overall timbre without repeating the same order of events. An isolated bright dot may be a coincidental match.

Jonathan Foote's 2000 work on audio novelty showed how local patterns in self-similarity can identify significant changes and segment boundaries without relying on one particular acoustic cue. This was a methodological contribution to audio segmentation, not proof that automatically detected boundaries correspond to the felt architecture of an improvisation. Foote's method can locate changes even without silence; it does not know whether Paul intended a threshold, a return, or no formal boundary at all.

A crucial distinction follows. A NOVELTY CURVE asks where the audio changes. A RECURRENCE MAP asks whether a configuration at one time resembles another. They answer different questions. KLAAT might have an important return with no sharp boundary, or a dramatic boundary into material that never recurs.

4. THE STRONGEST DIRECT COMPUTATIONAL TEST — AND ITS LIMITS

McFee and Ellis (2014) proposed a spectral-clustering approach that joins long-range repetition links with local temporal continuity. Their system produced multiple possible levels of structural organization instead of pretending that every recording has one uniquely correct number of sections. In one implementation they combined constant-Q harmonic features with timbral features, using graph relationships among time points to reveal nested forms.

They evaluated the method on 174 Beatles tracks and 735 SALAMI tracks. Their automatic selection procedure did not always choose the best available level of detail. On the Beatles corpus, its three-second-tolerance boundary F-measure was 0.579 (standard deviation 0.16), compared with 0.699 (0.16) for a named comparison method. On SALAMI functional annotations, the automatic method scored 0.455 (0.16) at the same tolerance, while the comparison method scored 0.550 (0.18). In the SALAMI case, an oracle that could select the best available output for each track reached 0.579 (0.15), showing that a plausible structure may exist among the candidate outputs even when the automated selector misses it.

Those figures are NOT KLAAT predictions. F-measure depends on annotation convention, allowed boundary tolerance, repertoire, feature extraction, and the selected evaluation target. The underlying tracks include song structures, tonal changes, and sections that often have verse/chorus-like functions. KLAAT's continuous drone and improvised frame drum may violate several assumptions at once.

One particularly important assumption is BEAT-SYNCHRONOUS ANALYSIS. McFee and Ellis's implementation aggregated features between detected beats. For conventional songs that can be useful. For a KLAAT passage in which Ankora stops, re-enters, accelerates locally, or supports several plausible metrical levels, a beat tracker may invent an unstable grid. If the feature windows depend on a false beat track, the recurrence map can inherit that error. The scientifically appropriate response is not to force Paul to play a clearer beat. It is to compare beat-synchronous results with clock-time windows and event-based descriptions.

5. WHY ONE FEATURE FAMILY IS NOT ENOUGH

A harmonic or chroma-like representation emphasizes pitch-class relationships. That is useful for music with chord changes. But Amastacia's nominal C# drone may make many unrelated KLAAT passages look harmonically identical. A feature that says 'still C#' cannot, by itself, distinguish a dry open drone from a rough, vocal-tract-shaped passage with Ankora's fast attacks underneath.

A timbral representation emphasizes spectral envelope and texture. It might detect returning breath color or a particular cluster of drum strikes. But it can also be fooled by a microphone moving, a changing room reflection, or a level-dependent playback effect. An onset-density representation may identify sparse-to-dense transitions but ignore the characteristic sound of the instruments. A pitch estimator can make octave errors; a transient detector can miss soft hand strokes or double-count noisy attacks.

Tralie and McFee (2019) tested a feature-fusion method for hierarchical music structure analysis. Their approach combined multiple time-series representations into a fused similarity network and reported better agreement with human structural annotations under their hierarchical L-measure evaluation than their compared pipeline. The significance for KLAAT is conceptual: a returning musical configuration can be represented across timbre, harmony, and rhythm rather than being reduced to one of them. The result does NOT establish which representation best captures a two-instrument, nonharmonically developing KLAAT improvisation. The model was not validated on Amastacia, Ankora, or a KLAAT listener panel.

For KLAAT, a reasonable future analysis would retain several separate maps before attempting any fusion: (a) Amastacia's evolving harmonic-envelope and noise texture, (b) Ankora's attack times and envelope characteristics, and (c) the combined recording's broader energy/texture contour. If separate stems are unavailable, source attribution must remain tentative. The aim is to ask whether different feature families agree, not to manufacture a single authoritative diagram.

6. THE REPEATED C# TRAP

Consider a hypothetical 25-minute KLAAT recording in which Amastacia plays continuously. Suppose a similarity algorithm uses short-time spectral energy concentrated near the drone's strongest harmonics. It might mark the entire performance as one repeating region. In one narrow sense the program is correct: the spectrum contains persistent components. But the map has failed to discriminate what makes the performance interesting.

Now imagine another algorithm looking only at drum attacks. It finds two stretches with similar average attack rate, perhaps six minutes apart. Yet in one stretch the strikes form a three-plus-two grouping and in the other they are evenly spaced. The same event count is not the same pattern.

A third algorithm identifies two passages with a similar order of roughness, breath articulation, and drum accents, even though the second is slower. This might be a more plausible candidate for a returning gesture. But it could also be a coincidence caused by using a feature representation too coarse to distinguish ordinary variation.

The defensible procedure is to include NEGATIVE CONTROLS: compare candidate matches with other randomly selected passages of equal length, avoid near-neighbor comparisons where all adjacent frames are naturally similar, and ask whether the candidate survives changes in window size, feature family, and playback condition. A visually striking similarity matrix is a hypothesis generator, not a verdict.

7. THE HUMAN ANNOTATION PROBLEM

The SALAMI project built a large dataset of structural annotations to evaluate music-analysis algorithms. Its original project report described more than 2,400 annotations of nearly 1,400 recordings. The existence of multiple annotations and levels of granularity is itself important: human listeners can disagree about how to partition a piece, even before an algorithm enters the picture.

McFee and Kinnaird (2019) showed that comparing hierarchical annotations is not as simple as checking whether two label strings match. Coarse and fine structural descriptions can refer to related organization while using different boundaries and labels. They investigated ways to expand annotations into comparable hierarchies, rather than assuming a single flat answer.

This matters even more for improvised KLAAT. One listener may recognize the return of a breath gesture; another may hear the return of a drum grouping; a third may report no return but a continuous change in energy. A listener who hears no motif is not necessarily inattentive. The music may support multiple organizations, or no robust recurrence at the chosen timescale.

The proper comparison is therefore not 'software says motif, listener says yes or no.' It is a three-way check: acoustically defined candidate recurrence, independently gathered listener judgments, and Paul's own retrospective description, collected without forcing any of the three to agree.

8. IMPROVISATION IS NOT A DEFECTIVE SONG

Cross and Goldman analyzed 435 four-four jazz solos from the Weimar Jazz Database to examine whether recurring melodic interval patterns were associated with particular metrical positions. Their result speaks to a general point: recurrence in improvisation can have systematic contextual properties. But it is not evidence that KLAAT has licks, a hidden four-four meter, or consciously reused phrases. The jazz corpus is transcribed, pitch-oriented, stylistically specific, and restricted to solos in four-four time. Its categories cannot be imposed on a didgeridoo-and-frame-drum performance that may be nonmelodic and metrically fluid.

The valuable lesson is narrower. Spontaneity and recurrence are compatible. A player can improvise while drawing on learned gestures, bodily habits, and evolving local expectations. The research question is not whether a recurring gesture makes KLAAT less authentic. It is whether a recurring gesture can be identified reliably and whether it matters perceptually.

In Paul's practice, the freedom to let the next sound emerge remains primary. If a pattern returns, the research can describe it afterward. If no clear pattern returns, the work is not incomplete.

9. WHAT A KLAAT-SPECIFIC TEST COULD MEASURE

A future study would begin with an actual, unedited KLAAT take. It should retain the original stereo master, recording chain information, playback level, and, if they already exist, isolated Amastacia and Ankora tracks. It should not require Paul to repeat a phrase on command.

The analyst would choose two or three window lengths before looking for exciting matches. For example, short windows might characterize individual textures; longer windows might characterize evolving gestures. These durations would be ANALYSIS SETTINGS, not prescribed performance units. The analyst would calculate separate feature maps for spectral texture, attack-event sequences, and broad dynamic contour, with explicit notes about normalization. Matching should exclude the trivial main diagonal and adjacent windows. Candidate matches should be checked against a distribution of nonmatches.

The most important validation would be perceptual. Without telling listeners that a passage is supposed to recur, play the intact recording and ask afterward whether any sound or gesture seemed to return. In a second, separate task, listeners could hear pairs of short passages and rate resemblance. The order should be randomized, volume matched when appropriate, and matched with control pairs of comparable duration and source balance. The two tasks answer different questions: natural recognition during the whole experience versus discriminability under an explicit comparison instruction.

Paul's own report would be a separate channel. He might say, 'I felt myself returning to that hand pattern,' or 'That rasp happened by accident both times,' or 'Those sections felt completely different.' These observations are valuable performer phenomenology, not a replacement for listener data. They should be recorded without revising the waveform to fit the explanation.

A possible analysis table would contain: candidate passage A/B timestamps; feature family; similarity score; score relative to nonmatch controls; sensitivity to window/normalization settings; blind listener agreement; Paul's retrospective impression; and known source/room artifacts. A missing value is reported as missing, not filled by interpretation.

10. FOUR EVIDENCE LEVELS AND ONE CAUSAL BOUNDARY

ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT Self-similarity and recurrence methods can identify repeated acoustic patterns and support structural analysis. Different representations emphasize different properties. Hierarchical descriptions and human annotations need not agree perfectly. Improvisation can exhibit recurring motifs or regularities without being fully precomposed.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT Reported structure-detection performance depends on the genre, dataset, annotation protocol, feature design, temporal resolution, beat-tracking reliability, and evaluation tolerance. Methods developed on songs with stable meter and tonal change may perform poorly on a continuous drone. The numerical results from Beatles, SALAMI, and jazz corpora cannot be transferred to KLAAT as effect sizes.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT Some unedited KLAAT takes may contain recognizable long-range returns of drum gesture, breath articulation, spectral texture, or their combination. Some of those returns may be perceived by listeners without prompting. Other candidates may be detectable only by algorithms, or only after a listener is asked to compare two excerpts.

SPECULATION The sense that KLAAT is an evolving world rather than an endlessly repeated loop might partly arise from intermittent reappearance of related gestures in altered contexts. This could give a listener familiarity without a fixed verse/chorus form. It is an artistic hypothesis, not a documented mechanism in KLAAT.

UNSUPPORTED A similarity matrix proves a conscious compositional plan; a recurring spectral pattern proves a recurring emotion; all listeners perceive the same motif; a detected recurrence causes trance, neural entrainment, autonomic regulation, or therapeutic benefit; C# or a particular BPM creates a universal motif; or Paul should engineer more repetitions because a computer found them.

CAUSAL BOUNDARY Even if a returning pattern correlates with reported absorption, correlation would not show that recurrence caused absorption. The same passage may also be louder, more novel, more personally meaningful, or more favorably placed in the listening sequence. A causal claim would require a carefully controlled comparison, ideally without losing the natural performance's ecological validity. An edited comparison might test a narrow mechanism but would no longer be the original KLAAT experience. Both facts must be acknowledged.

11. A CONTRADICTION WORTH KEEPING

The best automatic representation for matching musical passages need not be the representation that best predicts listener recognition. A harmonic model may score well on a pop-song dataset and fail to discriminate KLAAT's internal drone gestures. A timbral model may detect recurring rasp that listeners interpret as background texture rather than as a motif. A listener may hear an expressive return that changes in timbre and timing so substantially that simple acoustic similarity is low.

This is not an inconvenience to erase with a more complicated model. It is the central scientific opportunity. What recurs physically, what recurs perceptually, and what feels familiar are related but non-identical. A KLAAT research program becomes more credible by preserving those disagreements instead of calling them measurement failure.

12. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR KLAAT AS ART

KLAAT does not need a written architecture to possess identity. Amastacia and Ankora already provide a constrained material world: one breathing resonant body, one hand-struck membrane, two kinds of continuity, a particular relationship between pressure and release, and a human performer whose choices emerge in real time. Within those constraints, returns may arise from memory, habit, touch, instrument mechanics, or coincidence. Research can reveal which possibilities are supported in a recording. It cannot assign artistic necessity to them.

This is why the guiding principle matters. Experience-led creation means Paul remains free to follow the sound without listening for a recurrence graph in his head. Science-informed refinement means that afterward, when he wonders why a particular long take felt coherent or why a listener recognized something returning, there are better questions available than 'Was it all at the same BPM?'

The research may eventually help KLAAT document its own living vocabulary: not a score, not a ritual prescription, and not a claim of universal altered consciousness, but an evidence-aware description of gestures that sometimes reappear. If no such vocabulary is reliably measurable, that negative result would also be meaningful. KLAAT's aesthetic identity does not depend on proving a hidden form.

OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — KRX-104: THE UNPROMPTED RETURN

On a day when Paul already wants to listen back to an authentic take, mark one or two moments that simply feel familiar, as though a gesture has returned. Do not stop playback to analyze or change the playing. Later, compare those timestamps with an earlier passage and ask only: what actually resembles what—Ankora's timing, Amastacia's color, the joint texture, or just the feeling? If nothing clearly recurs, record that too. No additional performance, score, tempo target, or listener recruitment is required.
