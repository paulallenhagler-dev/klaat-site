THE PERFORMANCE YOU CAN REHEARSE WITHOUT PLAYING

Motor imagery, physical practice, and why imagined fluency is not yet an acoustic skill

SESSION 133 / 164Evidence & MethodRESEARCH SESSION

Published: October 29, 2026 — 19:11 CDT (America/Chicago) Governing principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH STATUS AND SCOPE This is a critical synthesis of controlled musician-learning studies and one recent percussion experiment. It reports no KLAAT-specific measurement, technique gain, listener response, neural state, autonomic change, or trance effect. Its purpose is to ask whether an optional, low-effort form of technical preparation could help Paul coordinate Amastacia and Ankora while leaving the actual music improvised. The answer is narrower than the familiar claim that “mental practice works”: imagery may complement actual practice for certain learnable actions, but it cannot reproduce the air pressure, lip vibration, skin contact, mechanical resistance, or live auditory error signals that define this particular instrument pair.

1. THE QUESTION THAT BELONGS TO KLAAT

There are at least two very different activities that someone might call “practicing KLAAT.” One is playing: Amastacia vibrates under Paul's breath and embouchure while Ankora supplies physical attacks, with the hands, trunk, mouth and respiratory system working together in real time. The other is anticipating or mentally reconstructing a familiar action without producing it. Paul might recall the sensation of a circular-breath changeover, imagine the next hand stroke, or hear an improvised phrase internally while sitting without the instruments.

These are not interchangeable. They might nevertheless interact. The question is not whether Paul should mentally compose passages, visualize a mandated four-phase form, or replace spontaneous playing with imagined scores. It is this: CAN OFF-INSTRUMENT REHEARSAL SOMETIMES HELP A TECHNICAL TRANSITION BECOME MORE AVAILABLE TO AN IMPROVISER, AND HOW WOULD WE KNOW?

This matters because KLAAT's skill burden is unusually cross-system. A drummer can rehearse a striking gesture without coordinating a lip-reed drone. A didgeridoo player can work on circular breathing without sustaining a second instrument's attack pattern. Paul has to make those movements coexist while continuing to listen and respond artistically. The difference between a rehearsed motor possibility and a predetermined musical phrase is crucial. Technique can be prepared; the music can still emerge.

2. WHAT MOTOR IMAGERY ACTUALLY MEANS

Motor imagery is the intentional mental simulation of an action without executing the complete action. Kinesthetic imagery emphasizes what movement would feel like; visual imagery emphasizes what it would look like. Auditory imagery concerns an internally represented sound. These can overlap, but they are not the same measurement or mechanism. Thinking about a phrase, recalling its sound, imagining a hand movement, and silently practicing a motor sequence may involve different processes.

For KLAAT, one could imagine the transition between cheek-reservoir pressure and lung airflow, or the feeling of Ankora's striking hand recovering from an accent. Such an image is a representation of an action, not a direct test of whether the physical action will work. There is no air-column oscillation during the imagined drone; no measured nasal airflow; no mouthpiece seal to maintain; no real rebound from Ankora's membrane. An imagined movement also does not automatically mean that the relevant muscles are silent, nor that the imagined duration matches actual performance. The category must be described carefully rather than used as a mystical shortcut.

An important additional distinction is between IMAGERY OF A KNOWN ACTION and IMAGERY OF AN ACTION NOT YET MASTERED. An experienced drummer can internally simulate familiar stroke mechanics with more useful sensory detail than a novice who has never felt them. Conversely, a person who repeatedly imagines an ineffective motion may become more confident in the wrong prediction. This last possibility is plausible, not demonstrated for KLAAT. It is a reason to keep real feedback central.

3. THE MOST DIRECT NEW EVIDENCE: TWENTY-EIGHT PERCUSSIONISTS

Loria and colleagues (2025, Journal of Motor Behavior, DOI 10.1080/00222895.2025.2501577) compared physical practice with combined motor-imagery/physical practice in 28 percussion majors learning a novel snare-drum excerpt. Motion capture quantified upper-limb movements. Temporal error was defined against the prescribed metronome-based note timing. Both groups improved their temporal error from pre- to post-training, and both showed changed stick positions and hand velocities. The published abstract does not establish that imagery alone was sufficient, or that one group became definitively superior. The combined group reported lower training effort and greater willingness to continue the approach.

An earlier 2023 conference abstract from the same project gives useful, explicitly preliminary detail: 40 physical trials for the physical-only group versus 20 physical plus 20 imagined trials for the combined group; aggregate timing error decreased from 51.4 to 37.9 milliseconds across groups. That is an illustrative within-study change of 13.5 milliseconds, approximately 26.3% of the initial mean, not a KLAAT improvement forecast. The conference abstract reports combined-group effort ratings of 3.8 versus 5.3 for physical-only practice, but the final journal abstract does not provide these exact numbers; therefore they should not be treated as independently reverified final-paper estimates. The key finding remains: a partly imagined practice schedule was compatible with improvement on a defined percussion task, while participants perceived it as less effortful.

Two limits are decisive. First, the endpoint was accuracy relative to a METRONOME and a NOVEL WRITTEN EXCERPT. KLAAT does not have an ideal score or an externally prescribed onset grid. A method that improves reproduction of a fixed pattern need not improve improvisational freedom, expressive flexibility, or coordination under continuous drone production. Second, “no detected between-group difference” is not a formal equivalence test. A study of 28 participants can fail to detect meaningful differences. Comparable observed improvement should not be inflated into proof that half the physical practice can always be replaced by imagery.

4. A WIND-INSTRUMENT TEST: FIFTY TRUMPET STUDENTS

Steenstrup and colleagues (2021, Frontiers in Psychology, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052) tested 50 classically trained trumpet students on five unfamiliar approximately 50-second etudes. Each participant encountered five practice conditions across a repeated-measures design: physical practice, auditory/motor imagery, overt singing, combined physical-plus-imagery-plus-singing, and no practice. The project produced 250 participant-condition sessions and 500 pre/post recordings. Three expert assessors evaluated several dimensions including pitch and rhythm accuracy, sound quality, intonation and expression.

Physical practice and combined practice improved overall performance and pitch accuracy relative to no practice. Only the combined strategy yielded a statistically significant improvement in musical expression in the reported comparisons. The combined strategy performed better than imagery-only or singing-only approaches in the authors' summary. This matters because it directly contradicts an appealing but overbroad claim: imagining a wind-instrument action is not a proven substitute for making the instrument sound. Combining mental, auditory and actual work can be useful, but the components do not all produce the same gains.

There is also an artistic complication. The students were learning fixed etudes with identifiable correct notes. Paul's improvisation may have no “correct next note” or prescribed timing. We cannot transfer an improvement in pitch accuracy on an unfamiliar trumpet exercise into a claim about spontaneous Amastacia harmonic articulation. We can transfer only a more modest idea: mental preparation may be a useful supplement when a musician is learning a specific physical or auditory skill and can later check the result against reality.

5. AN INSTRUCTIVE CONTRADICTION FROM WIND PRACTICE

Stambaugh (2013, Psychology of Music, DOI 10.1177/0305735612449505) studied 46 university woodwind and valved-brass students who practiced short seven-pitch tasks either in blocked/repetitive or random order. The retention results at 24 hours differed by instrument family. Woodwind players in the random-practice condition were faster and more even than those in blocked practice, with reported tests F(4,80)=4.448, p=.003, eta-squared=.15 for speed and F(4,80)=4.464, p=.003, eta-squared=.16 for evenness. In the brass group, blocked practice favored accuracy and speed, with F(4,64)=3.508, p=.012, eta-squared=.15 and F(4,64)=4.489, p=.003, eta-squared=.18, respectively.

This was NOT an imagery experiment. It is included because it undermines the premise that one efficient learning format must suit every instrument or movement. Even in conventional wind training, a schedule that helps one group can be less effective for another. The findings depend on the short experimental task, participant skill, and the way retention was tested. They do not authorize prescribing blocked practice for didgeridoo or random practice for frame drum. They do justify humility about importing a technique from snare drum or trumpet to the Amastacia–Ankora combination.

KLAAT's physical problem is not simply “wind plus percussion.” It is the specific coordination of lip vibration, breath changeover, left/right hand movement, torso stabilization, and listening under the constraints of the actual instruments. The right learning experiment must eventually measure that system, not a generic instrument-family label.

6. ANOTHER KIND OF COGNITIVE INTERVENTION IS NOT IMAGERY

Kan, Dibben and Wong (2024 issue; online 2023, Psychology of Music, DOI 10.1177/03057356231159194) randomly assigned 40 organists to a five-minute cognitive audio intervention (n=21) or rest control (n=19). The intervention used verbal self-talk intended to reduce unnecessary force in organ playing. Afterward, the intervention group showed lower forearm electromyographic activity during organ performance, while the controls did not show the same change.

That is a measured muscle-activity result, not a subjective claim about becoming “more relaxed,” and not evidence that imagination alone improves technique. It also illustrates how a musician can transfer a familiar but unnecessary movement strategy from one instrument to another: piano dynamics and organ dynamics are mechanically different. This is an important caution for Paul. An intuition learned on one drum or wind instrument might not be optimal for the exact resistance and geometry of Ankora or Amastacia. But the study does not prove that any particular KLAAT movement is wasteful or that Paul needs to reduce effort. Technique change should respond to a real problem, not to the assumption that visible effort is bad.

7. THE CENTRAL DISTINCTION: PREPARING A CAPABILITY VERSUS WRITING THE MUSIC

There are two ways imagery could enter an artistic practice. In one, the performer mentally rehearses a specific composition, including a fixed succession of notes, accents and entries. In the other, the performer explores the feel of a movement that may be used spontaneously in many musical contexts. KLAAT is interested primarily in the second possibility.

Consider a circular-breath transition. A technical aim could be to keep the air column vibrating while the source of pressure changes. Imagery might help Paul remember the relative sensation of cheeks, tongue, soft palate and nasal inhalation. But because the critical pressures and airway configuration are not reproduced during imagination, the test remains physical: did Amastacia continue, and did the transition feel more or less controllable when actually playing? The goal is not to conceal every breath sound. A natural audible breath may be part of KLAAT's expressive language. The skill aim is choice, not sterile perfection.

Consider an Ankora accent during a drone. A technical aim could be to recover the striking hand without disturbing lip contact or becoming stuck in a rigid motor plan. Imagery could prepare the feeling of the hand returning, but only actual contact with the hide reveals rebound, timing and resistance. The 2025 snare-drum study is relevant to hand motion, yet its measured stick trajectory does not tell us what happens to Amastacia when Paul plays both instruments. The most recent hourly research identified a plausible physical pathway from a large drum gesture through trunk/head movement to mouthpiece contact. That pathway remains unmeasured. Imagery cannot settle whether it occurs; it might at most prepare awareness of an action to inspect later.

Consider a spontaneous transition between rhythmic densities. Mental rehearsal might prime several possible movement options, but a preselected sequence would defeat the user's objective if it started to govern the improvisation. Nothing in the research shows that spontaneous creativity is enhanced by picturing a specific rhythm in advance. The best hypothesis is more modest: a broader repertoire of fluent physical options could reduce the need for conscious mechanical monitoring while playing. Whether that actually improves Paul's flow, freedom, or listener experience is unknown.

8. WHAT IMAGERY CANNOT SUPPLY

The missing information is physical and important. Imagined breath cannot reveal whether nasal airflow was adequate, whether the cheeks provided enough reservoir pressure, or whether the soft palate and tongue achieved a functional seal. Imagined lip movement cannot measure the pressure needed to sustain Amastacia's C# drone or the harmonic changes caused by vocal-tract configuration. Imagined striking cannot recreate Ankora's actual membrane stiffness, rebound, acoustic attack, or humidity-dependent response. Imagined coordination cannot reproduce the masking of a quiet drone detail by a loud drum attack, nor the spatial sound field in a room.

There is also a measurement asymmetry: a person may feel mentally prepared and nevertheless perform no better. Conversely, a person may show a small objective improvement without noticing it. Session 130 already distinguished subjective effort from measured fatigability. Session 124 questioned the universality of external attentional focus. This session adds a separate distinction: EASE OF MENTAL SIMULATION is not a demonstrated change in real acoustic execution. A successful imagery experience is not a successful physical trial until the skill is performed.

Nor does any of this support the idea that silently imagining KLAAT induces the same performer trance or bodily state as actually playing it. Motor imagery can be scientifically studied through behavioral, neuroimaging and physiological measures, but no such measurements exist for Paul or KLAAT. An imagined action, a performer's flow report, a listener's absorption report, and neural activity are different observations. One cannot substitute for another.

9. THREE HYPOTHESES WORTH KEEPING OPEN

H1 — COMPLEMENTARY SKILL ACCESS. For a familiar, narrowly identified motor transition, brief imagery between ordinary practice opportunities might help Paul recall a usable action, particularly if the real instruments are temporarily unavailable. The predicted outcome is not a new musical form but greater ease of initiating or recovering a movement when actual playing resumes. No KLAAT-specific evidence exists.

H2 — LESS PHYSICAL LOAD, NOT AUTOMATICALLY MORE SKILL. Imagery might reduce the amount of physical repetition required for some learning objectives, as suggested by the 2025 percussion comparison. But that result could depend on the task and participant expertise. Less perceived effort during practice does not prove equivalent long-term retention, endurance, musicality, or transfer to simultaneous didgeridoo and frame drum.

H3 — IMAGERY MAY BE MOST USEFUL AFTER REAL FEEDBACK. Because imagery has no live air pressure or membrane rebound, its content may become more accurate after Paul has actually experienced the movement and listened to the sound. A possible cycle is experience -> recall -> next experience -> correction, with the art remaining spontaneous. This is a plausible learning model, not an experimentally validated KLAAT protocol.

A null hypothesis belongs alongside all three: Paul may find imagery boring, distracting, inaccurate, or useless. It may add no measurable benefit over rest or casual listening. That outcome would not be a failure of KLAAT. It would simply mean this optional technique does not deserve space in his practice.

10. EVIDENCE LADDER — WHAT CAN BE SAID NOW

ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT: - In 28 trained percussionists, both physical-only and combined imagery/physical training were associated with pre/post improvements in a metronome-scored snare-drum task; the combined group reported lower effort. The study does not establish universal equivalence or imagery-only effectiveness. - In 50 trumpet students, physical and combined practice improved several judged outcomes over no practice; imagery-only was not the strongest strategy, and the combined strategy had an advantage for musical expression in that experiment. - A 46-person wind-practice experiment found opposite blocked/random-practice advantages across woodwind and brass tasks. Practice principles are context-dependent. - A randomized 40-person organist experiment found a brief verbal cognitive intervention changed measured forearm activity; this is not a motor-imagery result.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: - Short, score-based tasks with clear correctness criteria may be more sensitive to imagery-related gains than open improvisation. - Imagery vividness, existing skill, musical training, practice dose, task complexity and available sensory feedback may moderate outcomes; these were not quantified for KLAAT. - Subjective effort, timing error, expressive ratings and willingness to practice are distinct endpoints.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: - Imagery of a known breath transition or hand recovery could improve later access to that movement. - Complementary mental rehearsal might reduce physical repetitions for some subskills while preserving ordinary improvised sessions. - Imagery could be most useful when it follows real playing and is checked against real sound.

SPECULATIVE: - A repertoire of more accessible motor actions might indirectly increase spontaneous flow, allow more confident variation, or support longer performances without more conscious control. No direct KLAAT test has established any of these effects.

UNSUPPORTED — DO NOT CLAIM: - Imagery can replace actual circular-breath training; it produces real air pressure or lip-reed oscillation; it guarantees better improvisation; it creates a neurological trance; it improves listener absorption; it eliminates fatigue or injury risk; it makes a particular tempo or four-phase architecture necessary; the 2025 percussion result proves 50% physical practice is enough for KLAAT; or a mental image predicts how Ankora's hide will respond.

12. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR KLAAT AS ART

KLAAT's sonic identity is not an exam in rhythmic accuracy. Amastacia's living drone, Ankora's hide pulse, breath grain, accidental textures, repetition and changing physical intensity can be compelling precisely because they are enacted, not recited. The research should make it easier for Paul to choose what his body can do, not require him to choose what the music must become. Imagery is worth considering only as one possible aid to technical freedom.

The public language remains artistic: KLAAT is a resonance ritual, a practice of sonic ritualism and Archeosonic Ritualism. Nothing in this study requires marketing it as brain training, motor therapy, meditation technology, or an empirically demonstrated trance device. The scientific record should keep the distinction between what Paul experiences while playing, what the microphones capture, and what listeners independently report. A more fluent performer may eventually create stronger experiences, but that is a causal hypothesis requiring real KLAAT sound and listener data, not a conclusion from instrumental pedagogy.

OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE KRX-133 — REMEMBER ONE MOVEMENT, THEN LET IT GO

Only if Paul feels curious: after an ordinary improvised practice, identify ONE physical transition that actually occurred and that he wants to feel more available—perhaps a circular-breath changeover or Ankora hand recovery. On a separate quiet moment, spend 30–60 seconds recalling its kinesthetic feel without trying to perform, hold the breath, force respiratory muscles, or invent a composition. At the next ordinary free improvisation, do not insert the movement on command. Simply notice afterward whether it arose naturally and felt more accessible, unchanged, or less accessible. If useful, compare two naturally occurring instances in an untouched recording, noting obvious differences in tempo, context and recording quality. No success threshold, schedule, score, or required repetition is imposed. An unchanged or negative result is fully acceptable.
