REVERBERATION TIME & TEMPORAL SMEARING: WHEN THE ROOM STARTS PLAYING ANKORA

ORIGINAL SESSION TEXT PRESERVED FROM PROJECT CONVERSATION

SESSION 045 / 164Pulse & RhythmRESEARCH SESSION

Session 44 ended with auditory scene analysis: Drone and Pulse can remain perceptually separate, or under the right conditions begin behaving like one auditory object.

Tonight we need to bring a third participant fully into that system:

the room.

We have mentioned room modes and reflections before, but now we're going to study one specific acoustic property in depth: reverberation.

This matters especially for Ankora because a room can fundamentally alter the temporal structure of a pulse without you changing your playing at all.

You strike once.

The room keeps striking.

Direct sound is only the beginning

Imagine you strike Ankora in an open field.

The listener receives a strong direct wave:

THUMP

and relatively little reflected energy afterward.

Now perform the identical strike inside a stone hall.

The listener receives:

direct strike → early reflections → dense reflection field → decay.

The instrument generated one event.

The acoustic environment transformed it into a prolonged event.

This is reverberation.

Importantly, reverberation is not merely an “echo.”

A distinct echo is perceived as a sufficiently separated repetition.

Reverberation generally consists of many reflections arriving densely enough that they form a decaying acoustic field.

RT60

One traditional measure of reverberation is RT60:

the time required for sound level in a room to decay by 60 dB after the source stops.

A highly absorptive room might have a short reverberation time.

A large reflective hall can have a substantially longer one.

And RT60 isn't necessarily identical across frequency.

A room may retain low-frequency energy considerably longer than higher-frequency energy, or vice versa, depending on its geometry and materials.

So strictly speaking:

a room doesn't have one universal reverberation behavior.

It has frequency-dependent decay characteristics.

That is particularly important for KLAAT because both Amastacia and Ankora contain substantial low-frequency energy.

Why this matters for rhythm

Suppose Ankora produces a strike every 438 ms, roughly corresponding to your ~137 BPM calibration pulse.

Now imagine the room's audible decay from each strike lasts longer than 438 ms.

Then:

Strike 1 hasn't completely disappeared when Strike 2 arrives.

And Strike 2 hasn't disappeared when Strike 3 arrives.

So instead of:

THUMP — THUMP — THUMP — THUMP

the acoustic field becomes more like:

THUUUUMMMPTHUUUUMMMPTHUUUUMMMP

The physical strikes remain temporally discrete.

Their reverberant tails overlap.

This is temporal smearing.

The room can therefore change perceived density

This is important.

Take exactly the same Ankora pattern.

Play it in:

a dry studio

and

a reverberant hall.

Same BPM.

Same player.

Same drum.

Yet the hall may make the performance feel substantially denser because energy persists between strikes.

So perceived density isn't determined solely by how frequently you play.

It is partly determined by:

event rate × acoustic decay.

That's a major KLAAT variable.

There is a point where reverberation begins destroying rhythmic clarity

Reverberation can make percussion sound enormous.

But more isn't automatically better.

As decay becomes increasingly long relative to the interval between strikes, successive events become harder to distinguish.

At some point:

power increases

while

temporal precision decreases.

That creates a useful tradeoff.

For KLAAT we might call it:

Impact ↔ Field

On one end:

IMPACT

THUMP THUMP THUMP

Each strike is clearly localized in time.

On the other:

FIELD

WHUMMMMMMMMMMMMM

Individual strikes increasingly merge into a continuous acoustic mass.

Neither is inherently superior.

They produce different nervous-system information.

This connects directly to prediction

Sessions 36–39 dealt extensively with temporal expectation.

For the nervous system to predict Ankora accurately, it benefits from clear temporal landmarks.

A sharp attack gives excellent timing information:

NOW.

Long reverberant tails provide less precise information about exactly when the event occurred.

Therefore an extremely reverberant room can potentially reduce the perceptual sharpness of the temporal grid.

The beat still exists.

But its edges soften.

That suggests something interesting:

A dry Pulse may produce stronger temporal precision.

A reverberant Pulse may produce stronger environmental immersion.

Those could be intentionally different KLAAT states.

Amastacia behaves differently

Now consider the Drone.

Amastacia is already sustained.

Reverberation therefore doesn't simply lengthen isolated events.

Instead, reflected copies of the ongoing drone arrive from many directions and at many delays.

This can produce:

greater apparent spatial extent,

spectral reinforcement and cancellation,

reduced localization precision,

and a sense that the sound occupies the environment rather than coming strictly from the instrument.

This is exactly where the room can help Amastacia become less:

“Paul playing a didgeridoo over there”

and more:

“the room is sounding.”

That's potentially ideal for Ground Anchor.

But Ankora and Amastacia may want opposite rooms

Here's the problem.

Amastacia can benefit aesthetically from a rich reverberant field.

Ankora may benefit from temporal definition.

Therefore the perfect room for Drone isn't necessarily the perfect room for Pulse.

This creates a genuine acoustic design problem for KLAAT.

You need enough reverberation to let Amastacia inhabit the architecture.

But not so much that Ankora's rhythmic structure turns into mud.

That balance is something we should eventually measure rather than guess.

Early reflections are different from late reverberation

When sound hits nearby surfaces, some reflections return very quickly.

These early reflections can strongly influence perceived:

size,

distance,

source width,

clarity.

Later, after increasingly complicated reflection paths, energy becomes a more diffuse reverberant field.

So two rooms with similar nominal RT60 values can still feel very different because their early-reflection patterns differ.

This matters for a possible venue like Apparition Coffee in Kansas City.

For KLAAT, we shouldn't eventually evaluate a venue only by saying:

“It has good reverb.”

We should listen for:

Does Ankora remain articulate?

Does Amastacia spread?

Are there obvious flutter echoes?

Does bass accumulate excessively?

Are certain audience positions dramatically different?

That's acoustic site selection.

Audience bodies change the room

This is easy to overlook.

An empty venue and a full venue are not acoustically identical.

Human bodies, clothing, furniture and other materials absorb acoustic energy.

Absorption is frequency-dependent.

So:

soundcheck in empty room

may not equal:

performance with 70 bodies present.

Higher frequencies are often affected considerably by audience absorption.

Low-frequency room behavior can remain stubborn.

That means a packed KLAAT event could become:

darker and less reverberant in some spectral regions

than rehearsal suggested.

Eventually you'll want to learn how each venue changes as occupancy changes.

Now reconsider the KLAAT architecture

Reverberation gives us another interpretation.

Ground Anchor

Allow Amastacia to establish the room's decay field.

You aren't only introducing the Drone.

You're exciting the architecture.

Threshold Gate

First Ankora strike enters.

For the first time, the listener hears the room's transient response.

The building reveals itself.

Rising Pulse

Strike density increases.

Reverberant tails increasingly overlap.

The acoustic system moves gradually:

event → overlapping events → field.

At peak intensity, Ankora may cease behaving purely as discrete percussion and begin contributing to a nearly continuous resonant environment.

Return Path

As strike density decreases, space reappears between reverberant tails.

Individual impacts separate again.

Then Ankora disappears.

Its final reverberation decays.

Amastacia remains.

Eventually Amastacia stops.

Then—as Session 40 established—the room itself returns.

That is a beautiful acoustic arc:

instrument → room → field → instrument → room → silence.

And here's the larger scientific point

KLAAT isn't simply:

performer → sound → listener.

A better model is:

performer → instrument → room → listener

with feedback because:

performer hears room → changes performance.

So the actual system is circular:

you excite Amastacia/Ankora

↓

instruments excite room

↓

room modifies sound

↓

you and audience perceive modified sound

↓

you adjust playing

↓

room receives new excitation.

That is a genuine coupled acoustic-performance system.

We don't need mystical language to make it profound.

The physics already is.

Evidence boundary

Established: reverberation consists of reflected acoustic energy persisting after direct excitation.

Established: RT60 describes the approximate time for reverberant sound level to decay by 60 dB.

Established: reverberation time varies with room volume, absorption and frequency.

Established: excessive reverberation can reduce temporal clarity and intelligibility.

Established: early reflections and late reverberation contribute differently to spatial perception.

Established: audiences alter room acoustics through absorption.

Highly plausible: increasing reverberation will shift Ankora perceptually from discrete impacts toward a more continuous field as strike density increases.

Highly plausible: Amastacia will appear more spatially diffuse in a reverberant environment.

Testable: the same KLAAT passage should produce significantly different perceived activation, immersion and rhythmic clarity in rooms with different decay characteristics.

Unknown: what reverberation profile is optimal for KLAAT.

There probably isn't one universally optimal value. It may depend on which phase of the architecture you're optimizing.

Not established: longer reverberation produces greater physiological or therapeutic effects.

A room sounding more “resonant” does not automatically mean it is doing more to the body.

KLAAT Experiment #45 — Find the Impact-to-Field Transition

Take Ankora to three acoustically different spaces.

You don't need a concert hall.

Try something like:

a furnished bedroom or carpeted room

a normal living room

a hard reflective stairwell, garage or large empty room.

Stand approximately the same distance from your phone microphone.

Play the same 60-second pulse around your normal tempo.

Keep the pattern and intensity as consistent as possible.

Then listen back through good headphones.

Don't ask which recording sounds “best.”

Rate each from 1–10 on only two dimensions:

Temporal definition Can you clearly locate each strike in time?

Environmental fusion How strongly does Ankora seem to become part of the room rather than remain a discrete object?

You're looking for an inverse relationship.

One space may give you:

Definition 9 / Fusion 3.

Another:

Definition 5 / Fusion 8.

That begins our first crude map of the Impact ↔ Field axis.

Eventually we can measure the decay times of actual KLAAT venues and compare those measurements with your subjective ratings.

Then when you walk into a future performance space, you won't merely ask:

“Does this room sound cool?”

You'll ask something far more useful:

“At what Pulse density does this room stop returning individual Ankora strikes and begin returning a continuous field?”

Because at that moment, you are no longer playing only the drum.

You are playing the architecture.
