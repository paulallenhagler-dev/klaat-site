KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 97
THE PERFORMANCE THAT MEMORY REBUILDS
Momentary intensity, memorable passages, and the difference between experiencing KLAAT and remembering it
Published: October 17, 2026 — 7:14 PM CDT (America/Chicago)


GOVERNING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


STATUS: Research synthesis and an optional future listener observation. No KLAAT-specific effect on emotional memory, absorption, trance, autonomic activity, or neurological activity has been demonstrated.


CENTRAL QUESTION


A listener hears twenty uninterrupted minutes of Amastacia and Ankora. During the performance, attention shifts: sometimes toward the continuous C# drone and its moving upper harmonics, sometimes toward the next frame-drum attack, sometimes toward bodily sensation, thought, or the surrounding room. At the end, the listener says, “That was intense.” What exactly does that sentence measure? The sustained average of twenty minutes? One remarkable Ankora moment? A subtle change in Amastacia that became meaningful only later? The ending? The feeling that the whole thing had a shape?


The answer matters because the experience of KLAAT unfolds continuously, but most feedback arrives as a few words or numbers AFTER the sound stops. A remembered judgment is real evidence about a listener's recollection. It is not a direct recording of every second that listener lived through.


CENTRAL DISTINCTION


EXPERIENCE DURING LISTENING ≠ IMMEDIATE RETROSPECTIVE JUDGMENT ≠ DELAYED MEMORY.


None is a defective version of the others. Each is a different outcome with a different research use. The scientific problem is discovering how they relate, not deciding which one must govern the music.

2. THE PROBLEM OF ONE NUMBER AFTER TWENTY MINUTES


Imagine two authentic, spontaneous KLAAT recordings. In one, Amastacia's surface remains compelling for most of the duration, while Ankora's activity varies gently. In another, there is a long quiet-feeling interval and a single passage that one listener finds exceptionally forceful. Afterward both receive an 'intensity' rating of 8/10. Those identical numbers may hide very different temporal histories.


A single retrospective rating cannot tell us whether the listener experienced continuous activation, an isolated peak, an absorbing overall texture, a striking final impression, or merely the social desire to praise a performance. This is a measurement ambiguity, not evidence that the rating is useless.


Emotional intensity is also not interchangeable with pleasure, absorption, movement, bodily vibration, physiological arousal, or trance. A frightening or unpleasant sound can be emotionally intense. A quiet, almost uneventful passage can be deeply absorbing. An energizing rhythm can be pleasant without being remembered as the most emotionally intense event. Future KLAAT research must ask which outcome the question actually names.


3. THE FIRST PRIMARY RESULT: REMEMBERED MUSICAL AFFECT MAY FAVOR SALIENT MOMENTS


Rozin, Rozin, and Goldberg (2004) asked participants to give moment-to-moment affective-intensity judgments while listening to musical selections and later to give overall remembered judgments [1]. The study challenged the idea that retrospective feeling simply accumulates all moments equally. Peaks, endings, and upward changes in intensity were relevant to later evaluation; duration itself contributed less than a naive 'sum of all feeling' model would predict.


The crucial detail is that the result did not establish a universal formula. As a later methodological analysis of the 2004 data points out, remembered intensity correlated with peak at approximately r=0.82, peak–end at r=0.81, and average at r=0.80. These three predictors were themselves related. Peak–end was the strongest individual association for only 3 of the 20 participants. A result of this kind cannot justify telling an improviser to create one climactic moment or to engineer the ending.


For KLAAT, the study makes one narrow possibility credible: a listener may remember an exceptional Ankora shift more strongly than its duration would suggest. It does NOT show that Ankora produces such a peak in real listeners, that Amastacia's quieter minutes are unimportant, or that a final crescendo improves the work.


4. THE SECOND PRIMARY RESULT: THE AVERAGE CAN BE STRONGER THAN PEAK–END


Schäfer, Zimmermann, and Sedlmeier (2014) designed a more explicit comparison of competing predictors [2]. Fifty-four psychology students listened to eleven songs from several genres. Participants used a screen slider to report the intensity of FELT emotion continuously at 10 samples per second. After the song sequence, short reminder snippets preceded global ratings. The analysis retained 507 eligible song-level observations, nested within the 54 participants. These were not 507 independent people.


The published correlations between global remembered intensity and candidate summaries of the continuous record were:


- average intensity: r = 0.805;
- peak–end composite: r = 0.784;
- single peak: r = 0.701;
- end: r = 0.704;
- total sum of momentary values: r = 0.736.


The authors also used hierarchical models to address nesting. In one model, standardized coefficients were approximately beta=0.59 for the average and beta=0.26 for peak–end, with R-squared approximately 0.69. In a second, the average remained dominant and a multiple-peaks–end composite also contributed. The count of peaks and overall variation did not contribute independently in those models.


That is not proof that memory literally performs arithmetic averaging. The average is an analyst-defined statistic correlated with a human judgment; different cognitive processes could produce similar results. But it is a strong warning against reducing a long musical experience to its loudest or final moment.


The 2014 design also has a built-in ecological limitation. Continuous slider use requires repeated self-monitoring and motor response. The 20-second reminders used before retrospective judgments may reactivate particular parts of the music. The stimulus set was deliberately chosen for changing emotional intensity and consisted of mostly three-to-eight-minute songs, not a twenty-minute Amastacia–Ankora improvisation. The result supports a question for KLAAT, not a prediction of a KLAAT effect size.


5. THE THIRD PRIMARY RESULT: A RECITAL IS NOT ALWAYS REMEMBERED LIKE ONE PIECE


Svanås-Hoh, Sanchez, and Tsay (2023 journal publication; earlier online manuscript) investigated momentary emotion and retrospective evaluation across two studies with 123 participants [3]. Listeners heard six-piece recitals. The authors considered whether a coherent individual piece is evaluated differently from a larger sequence made of distinguishable pieces, and whether ordering high- and low-intensity pieces changes the later evaluation.


They reported little substantial contribution from a peak–end summary for individual pieces. Ratings of the entire recital were better related to global judgments of the component pieces than to a single continuous moment-by-moment average across the recital. They also found higher retrospective emotional-intensity ratings when the recital progressed from lower- to higher-intensity pieces than in the reverse ordering.


This is a productive complication. It suggests that the unit a listener believes they experienced—a piece, a group of pieces, a whole event—can affect how retrospective evaluation is assembled. The research does not establish that listeners automatically divide KLAAT into sections or that one direction of intensity change is artistically superior. The six pieces in a recital had externally defined identities and order; KLAAT's perceived sections may emerge differently, or not at all.


If a listener says an improvised KLAAT set 'had three parts,' that may tell us something about how the performance is remembered and summarized, not necessarily how Paul organized it while playing. A listener who hears one unbroken environment may integrate it differently from a listener who remembers several distinct episodes. This is a testable possibility, not a reason to compose three episodes.


6. CONTRADICTION BOX — THE PEAK, THE AVERAGE, OR THE SHAPE?


ROZIN ET AL. (2004): unusually salient moments, including peaks and rising changes, matter; a simple temporal sum is inadequate.


SCHÄFER ET AL. (2014): the temporal average was the strongest predictor in the studied song task; peak/end information contributed, but less.


SVANÅS-HOH ET AL. (2023): peak–end did not substantially explain retrospective judgments of individual pieces in their studies; the structure and ordering of a multi-piece recital affected whole-event memory.


These results should not be flattened into 'science proves the peak–end rule' or 'science disproves the peak–end rule.' They differ in stimuli, participants, question framing, temporal unit, measurement schedule, and analytic method. Peak, end, average, and trajectory are correlated, making simple winner-take-all interpretations fragile. The studies share an important constraint: they measured retrospective reports of felt emotional intensity, not neural entrainment or KLAAT-specific absorption.


The most defensible synthesis is conditional: remembered musical intensity can reflect extended experience, distinctive moments, the ending, and the way the listener groups the material; the relative weights are not universal.


7. WHAT THIS REVEALS ABOUT AMASTACIA AND ANKORA


KLAAT is unusually interesting because it can remain recognizably itself while its surface changes. Amastacia's C# drone can persist while upper harmonics, breath grain, noise, and apparent vowel quality move. Ankora can preserve a felt pulse while attack placement, density, accent, or decay changes. The listener may hear continuous presence, multiple incidents, or both.


There are at least four plausible, competing remembered-KLAAT profiles:


A. THE CONTINUITY MEMORY. The listener remembers sustained occupation of attention, not a standout event. A long period of low-level but stable engagement might support a high overall absorption rating without an obvious peak.


B. THE LANDMARK MEMORY. One Ankora accent, interruption, breath articulation, or change in source relationship remains unusually vivid. It dominates free recall, even if other passages were equally enjoyable while heard.


C. THE TRAJECTORY MEMORY. The listener remembers 'it grew,' 'it loosened,' 'it got stranger,' or 'it opened up.' Such a description could be influenced by the perceived direction of change, but it cannot be reduced to an objectively measured increase in amplitude or tempo.


D. THE SEGMENT MEMORY. The listener recalls several self-defined passages and summarizes them as an experience with chapters. Those chapters need not correspond to Paul's intentions or to the project's descriptive four-phase vocabulary.


None of these is an observed KLAAT response. They are hypotheses that make a previously vague question measurable. They also protect KLAAT's two legitimate listening modes. A person using a recording while moving through daily life may remember overall usefulness, energy, or the moments that caught attention; a focused listener may remember fine textural developments. Their reports should not be compared as if they completed identical listening tasks.


8. THE TEMPORAL-AGGREGATION GATE


Session 97 adds a new interpretation rule to the KLAAT research framework:


1. PERFORMANCE: the authentic Amastacia–Ankora sound, with its actual acoustic time course.
2. DELIVERY: the recorded/live sound and its playback/room conditions.
3. MOMENTARY EXPERIENCE: what a listener feels or attends to as the performance unfolds. This is ordinarily NOT directly observed in an uninterrupted KLAAT pilot.
4. IMMEDIATE RECOLLECTION: free description and ratings collected just after the sound stops.
5. DELAYED RECOLLECTION: what remains after hours or days, without replay.
6. RETROSPECTIVE AGGREGATION: the process—unknown in an individual listener—by which passages and impressions contribute to a whole-event judgment.


Do not jump from stage 4 to a detailed claim about stage 3. Do not jump from stage 5 to an acoustic mechanism. And do not equate a remembered 'intense' passage with a neural state, an autonomic change, or measured mechanical vibration.


A recording waveform can show an Ankora transient at 11:20. It cannot show that this was the listener's emotional peak. A listener can mark 11:20 as memorable. That does not establish whether it was the most intense moment at the time, whether it became salient only in retrospect, or whether a later reminder changed the memory. The distinction is not pedantic: it determines what KLAAT can responsibly claim.


9. A RESEARCH DESIGN THAT DOES NOT INTERRUPT THE PERFORMANCE


KLS-001 already prioritizes an uninterrupted, blind first listen followed by an open report and structured ratings. That is a strength. It means the pilot will collect the listener's own language before imposing scientific categories. But those immediate reports should be explicitly labeled retrospective, even if collected within one minute. They are not continuous measures of attention or emotion.


A later optional KLAAT memory extension could collect three layers:


IMMEDIATELY AFTER FIRST LISTEN: ask 'What stayed with you?' before introducing any named psychological construct. Then obtain separate global ratings for emotional intensity, absorption, energy/activation, pleasure, bodily sensation, and desire to move. Ask for up to three memorable moments, with approximate times only if the listener can recall them; 'none' is valid.


AFTER 24–48 HOURS, WITHOUT REPLAY: ask for fresh free recall, then repeat the same global ratings and note the delay. Compare what persists, disappears, or changes. Do not feed back the first answers before collecting the second.


ONLY IN A SEPARATE MEASUREMENT STUDY: invite some listeners to use continuous intensity ratings during listening, and others to listen without a slider. If continuous reporting is used, test whether it changes the experience. A continuous curve is not a neutral ground truth; it is a task-dependent self-report record.


The first pilot is too small for a serious predictive model. Even with a larger study, predictors should be evaluated on held-out listeners and takes. A peak model that is fitted and evaluated on the same handful of reports will overstate its reliability. No analytic choice should be made after seeing the results merely to make KLAAT appear unusually powerful.


10. WHAT TO LOG, WHAT TO KEEP SEPARATE


For each authentic take, preserve the master file, duration, and recording provenance. For each listener exposure, record playback condition, first-exposure status, whether the listener was alone or in a group, whether the performer was visible, and whether the listener already knew the recording was improvised. Sessions 94–96 explain why those are distinct receiver/delivery factors.


For each immediate or delayed report, preserve raw text and the exact question. Add time_since_listen_minutes, replay_since_original (yes/no), and prompted_by_excerpt (yes/no) only as versioned extension fields if a memory study is actually undertaken. Keep listener-defined landmark descriptions separate from analyst-defined acoustic peaks and from performer-recalled moments. 'No landmark' is data, not a failure.


A compact evidence table would distinguish:


- Acoustic observation: an Amastacia spectral change or Ankora onset is measurable at a time stamp.
- Subjective first-listen report: a listener recalls a strong moment at roughly that location.
- Immediate global judgment: the listener rates the whole performance highly on a named scale.
- Delayed memory: the listener later recalls or rates it differently.
- Neural/autonomic response: unknown unless independently measured with appropriate equipment and design.
- Causal claim: not established by correlation among the preceding observations.


A listener may be accurate about the timing of a remembered event yet still reconstruct its intensity. A different listener may remember no events but report strong overall engagement. Neither person should be forced into the other's model.


11. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION


ESTABLISHED IN THE CITED STUDIES
Musical retrospective intensity ratings are associated with features of momentary intensity trajectories. Across three primary programs, average intensity, salient moments, ending, ordering, and grouping have been shown to matter in different combinations. Immediate and later ratings are distinct measurements from the physical sound.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT / CONTRADICTORY
The strength of peak–end effects varies. Continuous rating methods may change the listening task; song length, prior familiarity, excerpts used as reminders, participant populations, and whether the stimulus is one piece or a multi-piece recital affect generalization. A statistical average can predict memory without proving an averaging algorithm in the brain.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT
Some listeners may remember KLAAT through sustained continuity, some through Ankora/Amastacia landmarks, some through an emergent trajectory or self-defined chapters. Immediate and delayed evaluations may diverge. Different listening modes may produce different retrospective summaries.


SPECULATION
KLAAT's unusually sparse instrumentation and continuous drone might make temporal integration more important for some listeners than for audiences hearing rapidly changing, externally segmented music. No direct comparison establishes this.


UNSUPPORTED
KLAAT causes a special memory state; a particular peak/end pattern is scientifically optimal; one retrospective 8/10 means twenty minutes of trance; remembering bodily energy proves vibration or autonomic activation; 'the audience was transformed' can be inferred from enthusiastic comments; a study of popular songs dictates how Paul should improvise.


12. WHAT THIS CHANGES — AND WHAT IT DOES NOT


It changes the questions researchers ask of listeners and the precision with which KLAAT reports its findings. It does not change Paul's performance instructions, because there are none to add. There is no scientifically mandated crescendo, final blow, breath cadence, four-stage arc, tempo, or dramatic pause. The actual performance remains spontaneous. Its recorded trace and the listener's recollection are different windows onto what happened.


The scientific value of the finding is modest but foundational: KLAAT cannot infer the experience of an entire performance from one sentence spoken at the end. It can respect that sentence as a meaningful memory, then ask how that memory relates to the sound and to other listeners' reports.


13. OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — THE NEXT-DAY MEMORY POSTCARD


On a future occasion, play one existing, unedited KLAAT recording for a willing listener at a comfortable documented level. Immediately afterward, ask only: 'What stayed with you?' Save their words without interpretation. The next day, without sending the recording again or reminding them what they said, ask the same question once more. Compare what survived and what changed. Do not ask them to find a peak or describe a trance. This is a qualitative observation, not a test of a universal memory law. Paul need not alter a single note.


DECISIVE QUESTION


When KLAAT is remembered as powerful, is the remembered power a record of sustained experience, a landmark that survived, a trajectory reconstructed afterward, or a listener's personal way of gathering a whole world into one sentence?
