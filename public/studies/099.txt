KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 99
THE SKIN THAT RISES WITHOUT BEING SHAKEN
Musical chills, objective goosebumps, reward, and the difference between an emotional shiver and physical vibration
Published: October 18, 2026 — 3:20 PM CDT (America/Chicago)


GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH STATUS: This session reports evidence from music-perception and psychophysiology research conducted on other music and listeners. No KLAAT-specific chill, piloerection, dopamine, autonomic, trance, or tactile response has been demonstrated. The optional test is exploratory and does not alter Paul's improvisation.


THE QUESTION


A person listens to Amastacia's continuous C# drone and Ankora's pulse. At some moment, they describe a shiver up the spine, goosebumps, a rush, a sense of weight, or the feeling that the music is vibrating inside them. Are these interchangeable descriptions of one physical phenomenon? No. One of the most useful scientific distinctions for KLAAT is that a person can experience a bodily-feeling emotional event even when the relevant skin is not being mechanically shaken by the sound. Conversely, a floor or chair can vibrate measurably without anybody experiencing a pleasurable emotional chill.


That is the focus of this session: the difference between sound-driven mechanical sensation and a listener's music-evoked chill or frisson. The distinction matters precisely because KLAAT is sparse, bodily, sometimes trance-like in Paul's own performance, and intentionally capable of being energizing without requiring a ceremonial setting. It also matters because music science has an unusually instructive disagreement here: studies can measure neural reward responses, sweat-gland activity, visible goosebumps, and self-reported chills, yet these measurements are not equivalent, do not always occur together, and do not reveal a universal acoustic recipe.


THE CENTRAL PROPOSITION


A CHILL IS A POSSIBLE RESPONSE TO LISTENING, NOT A FREQUENCY DELIVERED BY THE INSTRUMENT.


1. FIVE EVENTS THAT SHOULD NEVER BE COLLAPSED


Consider five distinct observations during an authentic KLAAT recording.


A. Acoustic input: a microphone records Amastacia's fundamental, harmonics, breath noise, and Ankora's transients. This describes sound pressure at the microphone, subject to the recording chain.


B. Mechanical input: an accelerometer measures chair, floor, or skin-surface motion. This describes movement at that sensor, in its calibrated frequency and amplitude range. It does not measure the listener's feelings.


C. Subjective bodily report: the listener says, 'I felt it in my chest,' 'my skin tingled,' or 'a shiver went up my neck.' The report is genuine as an account of experience, but it does not by itself identify a mechanical pathway or autonomic mechanism.


D. Autonomic/skin response: electrodermal activity reflects sympathetic sweat-gland-related activity; objective piloerection is the raising of hair follicles, often visible as goosebumps. Neither is an acoustic frequency and neither alone establishes enjoyment.


E. Neural/reward finding: a neuroimaging experiment detects a blood-flow, BOLD, or radioligand-binding change in a research participant. That is not a portable signature that can be inferred from someone's description of a KLAAT performance.


All five may be scientifically interesting. The category error is to use evidence for one as if it proved the others. Session 92 asked what bass physically reaches the listener. Session 98 asked how Ankora radiates. This session asks something different: what if the most dramatic 'physical' response is partly an emotional/autonomic event rather than the mechanical transmission of low-frequency energy?


2. WHAT THE EARLY REWARD EXPERIMENTS ACTUALLY SHOWED


Blood and Zatorre (2001) used positron emission tomography with TEN musically trained participants, five women and five men, aged 20–30. These participants were specifically selected because they could reliably experience intense chills to self-chosen music. Their selected pieces were classical, and other participants' selections served as comparison music. The researchers found that more intense reported chills were associated with regional cerebral-blood-flow changes in structures implicated in reward, motivation, emotion, and arousal. Heart rate, muscle activity, and respiration were also monitored.


This is striking evidence that highly pleasurable music listening can recruit systems not restricted to ordinary acoustic feature analysis. But it is not a random sample of ten typical listeners, a test of didgeridoo, or a demonstration that a particular beat rate generates a specific brain state. The selection procedure enriched the sample for people who already knew they had chills. That improves sensitivity to the phenomenon and sharply limits prevalence claims.


Salimpoor and colleagues (2011) extended the question using PET with a dopamine-sensitive radioligand, alongside fMRI and autonomic measurements. In their selected listeners, results were consistent with dopamine release in the striatum during intense musical pleasure; temporal analyses associated anticipation more with the caudate and peak pleasure more with the nucleus accumbens. This is often compressed into a catchy but misleading slogan: 'music releases dopamine.' The more precise claim is that under the tested conditions, intensely pleasurable, participant-selected music produced measurable evidence of striatal dopaminergic involvement. PET is an indirect method, fMRI does not measure dopamine directly, and neither study establishes what happens during KLAAT.


The important implication is conceptual, not compositional. A sound can become meaningful and rewarding through a listener's expectations, memories, attention, and personal history. A sustained C# drone cannot be assumed to release dopamine merely because it is low, ancient-sounding, or repetitive. Equally, the absence of a traditional melody does not make KLAAT incapable of emotional salience. Both questions require evidence from actual listeners.


3. GOOSEBUMPS ARE NOT THE SAME THING AS REPORTED CHILLS


The term 'musical chills' is used inconsistently across studies and ordinary conversation. A listener may mean tingling, a shiver, goosebumps, a sudden emotional swell, or a pleasurable rush. Piloerection is narrower: the small muscles attached to hair follicles contract, raising hairs and changing skin appearance. This can be observed with sufficiently sensitive imaging, but it is not present in every self-reported chill.


Craig (2005) tested 32 students, including 16 musicians and 16 nonmusicians, with familiar and unfamiliar music. Reported chills corresponded to changes in galvanic skin response and were accompanied by observable piloerection in some instances, but skin temperature did not show a significant change. That is a useful early demonstration of partial, not universal, agreement among measures.


Benedek and Kaernbach (2011) used objective piloerection assessment while participants encountered music and film audio. Their work supported the value of observing goosebumps directly rather than relying solely on a verbal label, and linked the response to emotionally moving experiences. The later systematic review by de Fleurian and Pearce (2021) reported that objective piloerection appeared in only a subset of participants who reported music-evoked chills across multiple laboratory studies: examples included 57% in Craig's study and about 40% in Benedek and Kaernbach's. These percentages describe particular study samples and detection methods, not a population probability that any KLAAT listener will get goosebumps.


A camera showing goosebumps cannot tell us why they occurred. Temperature, startle, other sensory stimulation, anticipation, and emotional meaning are candidate influences. Grewe and colleagues (2011) demonstrated chills in response to auditory, visual, tactile, and even gustatory stimulation, as well as mentally generated experiences. This directly undermines the claim that a shiver requires a specific acoustic resonance.


For KLAAT the measurement rule is simple: write 'reported chill' when a person reports one; write 'visible piloerection' only if it was actually observed; write 'mechanical vibration' only when a relevant surface was measured. Never convert one label into another.


4. THE STRONGEST CONTRADICTION: THE MOST MOVING MOMENT MAY NOT BE THE LOUDEST


It is tempting to assume that more bass, more drums, more volume, and a larger crescendo will produce stronger chills. The evidence does not support a universal rule. Nagel and colleagues (2008) compared short chill-associated passages with passages not associated with chills and reported increased loudness in a particular perceptual-frequency range (approximately 920–4,400 Hz) in chill-associated excerpts. That is a context-bound acoustic correlation; it is not a universal low-frequency prescription and does not establish causation.


A much larger computational analysis by de Fleurian and Pearce (2021) examined 988 tracks that listeners had previously reported as chill-inducing, compared with matched tracks from the same artists. The chill-associated tracks were, on average, rated sadder by the analyzed audio-feature system; exploratory comparisons also indicated slower, less intense, and more instrumental characteristics. These are TRACK-LEVEL ASSOCIATIONS derived from an existing selection of reported chill pieces, not experimentally verified chill responses from 988 independent people in a controlled room. The feature model, genre, listener self-selection, and matching criteria constrain interpretation. Still, the findings complicate the story that an energetic or high-intensity surface is necessary for a profound response.


Mori and Iwanaga (2017) also separated two kinds of peak music emotion. Their analyzed experiment included 66 university students selected for a tendency toward chills or tears. Self-reported chills were associated with increased electrodermal activity and subjective arousal, whereas music-evoked tears were associated with slower breathing alongside heart-rate acceleration; both groups reported pleasure. A powerful response, therefore, need not be one uniform 'activation' pattern. Nor does a reported chill mean a listener became physiologically calm.


These studies do not tell Paul to play more softly, more loudly, more slowly, or more dramatically. They tell the research program to stop using acoustic intensity as a substitute for listener experience. KLAAT's sustained minimalism might create space for highly salient small changes, or it might produce no chills for some listeners while still supporting groove, focus, movement, or deep absorption. Either result is artistically legitimate.


5. CAUSAL EXPERIMENTS HELP, BUT THEY DO NOT PRODUCE A UNIVERSAL FORMULA


Bannister and Eerola (2018) recruited 24 participants who regularly experienced musical chills. They presented original versions of three chill-associated pieces and manipulated versions in which previously identified chill sections had been removed. Chills were significantly less frequent after the manipulations across the three pieces. Continuous chill-intensity reports were higher in identified chill sections than in controls, with partly corresponding electrodermal findings. Loudness, brightness, and roughness were correlated with response in two of the three pieces, not universally in all three.


This study offers stronger evidence than a simple playlist correlation that the local musical context can matter. But removing a whole section changes more than one acoustic feature. It changes continuity, expectation, meaning, duration, and what the listener has already heard. The experiment does not establish that adding a crescendo, a drum fill, or a roughness peak to KLAAT would cause chills. A controlled manipulation can show that a passage matters without identifying which component mattered or why.


There is a valuable methodological paradox here: manipulating music can improve causal inference, but manipulation can also remove the improvisational relationship that makes the music meaningful. For KLAAT, the sensible first stage is observation of authentic recordings. Only if a later causal question is worth the artistic compromise should researchers consider an explicitly separate comparison version. The original performance remains the reference, not a failed draft waiting to be optimized.


6. WHY CHILLS MAY BE ESPECIALLY DIFFICULT TO INTERPRET IN KLAAT


KLAAT has several plausible routes to a strong bodily description, none yet measured as a chill mechanism:


— Amastacia can create an extended, spectrally changing drone. The listener may become absorbed, notice small changes more intensely, or feel the passage as one continuous field.
— Ankora supplies transients and rhythmic landmarks. A particular strike may become salient because of prior repetition, unexpected accent, or the moment the listener notices it.
— Breath articulation can sound unmistakably human. The listener may interpret a change as effort, voice, vulnerability, tension, or release.
— The room and playback system can deliver different amounts of low-frequency sound and vibration. This is physical delivery, not evidence of reward chemistry.
— KLAAT's ancient/ritual visual language may shape meaning, expectancy, and emotional framing when it is present. That is a context variable, not an acoustic mechanism.
— The listener's familiarity, preferences, memory, social situation, and momentary state may influence whether any of the above becomes moving or uncomfortable.


The sound may feel bodily because it is mechanically coupled, because it invites motor readiness, because the listener has a chill, because attention has turned inward, or because several mechanisms overlap. A phrase such as 'I felt the drum in my spine' does not settle which one. This is why the new question deserves its own study rather than being folded into the existing low-frequency vibration chapters.


7. THE CHILL–VIBRATION DISSOCIATION GATE


Use a simple five-column logic before interpreting any KLAAT report:


(1) SOURCE: What actually occurred in the unedited performance? Document recording identity, acoustic features, and event times without assuming a formal score.
(2) DELIVERY: What sound pressure and mechanical movement reached the listener's position? Measure if necessary; otherwise mark unknown.
(3) EXPERIENCE: What did the listener say, in their own words, without being told to expect goosebumps or trance?
(4) PHYSIOLOGY: Was anything actually measured? Skin conductance, piloerection, breathing, heart rate, and neural activity require distinct instruments and analysis. 'Not measured' is a valid result field.
(5) INTERPRETATION: What explanations remain possible, and which have been excluded? A chill report alone cannot prove a particular frequency, dopamine release, neural entrainment, or physical vibration.


The most revealing outcomes may be disagreements among columns. A listener could report goosebumps while no chair vibration is detected. Another could receive measurable chair vibration yet report nothing unusual. Someone might describe 'energy' without pleasure, or pleasure without shivering. A musician could feel powerful flow while an audience member feels a steady daily-life groove. These are not failed KLAAT experiences. They are different outcomes that a serious research program should preserve.


8. MEASUREMENT PITFALLS THAT COULD MAKE THE DATA LOOK MORE CONVINCING THAN IT IS


A. Prompt contamination. Asking 'Did the bass give you goosebumps?' teaches the listener the answer category. Begin with an open description and only afterward offer neutral optional categories.


B. Recall bias. A listener may remember one intense moment and report the entire excerpt as powerful. Session 97 established why momentary experience and remembered experience should not be collapsed.


C. Event timing. A listener pressing a button after noticing a chill introduces reaction time and reporting delay. Skin-conductance responses also evolve over seconds. Exact alignment to an Ankora strike is not automatically causal.


D. Instrument artifacts. Motion, room temperature, dry skin, electrode placement, and camera lighting can distort peripheral measurements. A consumer smartwatch is not a validated detector of emotional chills or dopamine release.


E. Selection bias. Chills are easier to elicit in people recruited because they already experience them frequently. Such a sample may be useful for mechanism exploration but cannot estimate prevalence in the wider KLAAT audience.


F. Acoustic confounding. Comparing a live PA with headphones changes room field, sound level, low-frequency delivery, social context, and visible action. Any difference in chills would have many possible causes.


G. Missing chills as a false negative. Absorption, movement desire, alertness, meaning, or pleasure can occur without goosebumps. No-chill does not equal no-effect.


H. Arousal as a false synonym. Electrodermal changes can reflect sympathetic activation without identifying whether the experience was pleasurable, unpleasant, surprising, or effortful.


9. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION


ESTABLISHED IN OTHER MUSIC-LISTENING CONTEXTS
Some people experience music-evoked chills or shivers. In selected research participants, these events have been associated with autonomic changes, measurable piloerection in a subset, and activity in reward-related neural systems. Musical chills are not reducible to a single measured acoustic frequency or to mechanical vibration of the skin. Subjective chills and objective goosebumps are overlapping but nonidentical outcomes.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT / MIXED
The likelihood of chills depends on listener, music, familiarity, emotional meaning, experimental instructions, and context. Associations with loudness, roughness, valence, or musical structure differ across datasets and methods. Chills and tears can have different physiological profiles. Neuroimaging studies often recruit highly responsive listeners and do not generalize to every musical style or person.


PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT
An authentic Amastacia–Ankora performance may sometimes elicit self-reported chills that are separable from low-frequency mechanical vibration. A sparse passage might produce a strong emotional event without a large SPL increase. Different listeners may report bodily weight, groove, shiver, pleasure, or absorption at different times in the same recording. These possibilities are hypotheses until measured in KLAAT listeners.


SPECULATIVE
The combination of a persistent drone, human breath noise, and intermittent drum landmarks might allow an unusually intimate relationship between expectation, attention, and emotional peaks. A ritual aesthetic might change the likelihood or interpretation of a chill for some listeners. No existing KLAAT dataset establishes these pathways or their direction.


UNSUPPORTED / PROHIBITED CLAIMS
'KLAAT causes dopamine release'; 'C# produces goosebumps'; '137 BPM induces frisson'; 'the audience's spine is physically resonating because they shivered'; 'a chill proves trance or neural entrainment'; 'goosebumps prove healing'; 'more bass or volume necessarily produces a stronger emotional response'; or 'a listener without chills was not activated.' None follows from the evidence reviewed here.


10. THE ARTISTIC CONSEQUENCE IS FREEDOM, NOT A NEW RULE


The strongest implication is not that Paul should chase chills. It is that the existing improvised sound can be investigated without confusing different kinds of intensity. KLAAT is not obligated to manufacture a dramatic emotional peak every few minutes. It can remain useful while someone drives, draws, works, or moves through a normal day; it can also become deeply absorbing when heard attentively. A person may experience a chill once, never, or often. That variation is scientifically interesting but not a ranking of artistic success.


Amastacia and Ankora are not variables to be adjusted until a biomarker lights up. They are instruments within a personal artistic language. Science can help identify what a listener actually experienced, which explanations remain credible, and whether recording or presentation preserves the original acoustic world. It should not prescribe the world.


11. OPTIONAL KRX-099 — THE UNPROMPTED BODY-WORD TEST


Use one existing, unedited KLAAT excerpt that you already like. Invite two or three willing listeners to hear it at a comfortable level in a quiet setting; participation and stopping are always optional. Do not mention goosebumps, vibration, trance, or dopamine beforehand. Immediately afterward ask only, 'What, if anything, stood out to you?' Record their exact words. If they spontaneously mention a bodily sensation, ask one neutral follow-up: 'Can you describe what that felt like and when you noticed it?' Preserve 'none' and 'not sure' as valid answers. Optionally note playback type, approximate level, and whether the person already knows KLAAT.


Do not interpret the result as physiological proof. Do not touch participants, film their skin without consent, or raise the volume to try to provoke a response. If one person says 'goosebumps,' another says 'a heavy pulse,' and another says 'good background music,' the result is already useful: it demonstrates why those reports must be kept distinct. No score, tempo, phase change, or new recording is required.


FINAL POSITION


The skin can seem to rise because music is emotionally moving, even when a surface is not measurably shaking. And sound can physically shake a surface without producing any emotional chill. KLAAT's research gains precision when it treats these as different possible paths from sound to experience. The purpose is not to turn Amastacia and Ankora into a neurological control system. The purpose is to understand what their real, spontaneously made sound does and does not do to real listeners.
