KLAAT / RESONANCE RESEARCH / SESSION 147

THE BODY HEARS A DIFFERENT MIX

Bone conduction, self-produced sound, and why the performer’s experience cannot stand in for the audience’s

SESSION 147 / 164Body & PerformanceRESEARCH SESSION

RESEARCH QUESTION When Paul plays Amastacia while simultaneously striking Ankora, is the sound he experiences physically and perceptually the same sound a nearby listener hears? In particular, how much of the performer’s experience comes from air-conducted sound, mechanical vibration through contact, bone-conducted sound, breathing effort, and predictive motor signals? This question is distinct from asking whether the audience feels trance, and from asking whether the recording accurately preserves the drum: it asks whether performer and listener are receiving the same sensory stimulus in the first place.

WHY THIS IS A LIVE KLAAT QUESTION KLAAT is built around simultaneous acoustic actions. The mouth is coupled to the agave tube, hands to the frame drum, and breath is both the engine of the drone and an intimate physiological sensation. Paul may feel a sustained, powerful physical continuity even when a distant microphone captures an uneven balance between the drone and the drum. A listener, in turn, may experience a sharp rhythmic attack that feels much more prominent than it did to the performer. Neither experience is invalid. They are different observations.

A central hazard in the research program is treating the performer’s report of deep flow as if it predicted the listener’s absorption. Flow can be genuine and artistically decisive without establishing any particular audience effect. Before interpreting differences psychologically, we should identify differences in sensory access.

ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE: MULTIPLE PATHS TO HEARING Ordinary listening involves sound pressure reaching the external ear and driving the middle and inner ear. Bone-conducted vibration can also stimulate the cochlea through several physical pathways. The precise contribution depends on frequency, coupling, anatomy and vibration location. The performer has access to additional mechanical paths because his mouth and hands contact vibrating instruments. A recording made in the room cannot capture all of those paths simply by placing a microphone in the air.

This does not mean the player literally hears a completely separate song, or that bone conduction automatically amplifies the deepest bass. The magnitude and frequency response of these contributions are not measured for KLAAT. Nor does the presence of tactile vibration prove that an audience receives comparable vibration: floor, chair, room construction, distance and footwear matter.

ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE: SELF-GENERATED SOUND IS PERCEPTUALLY SPECIAL Auditory perception is shaped by action. Experimental research on self-generated versus externally generated sounds shows that predictable sensory consequences of voluntary actions can be processed differently. The literature includes auditory attenuation and motor-related predictive effects, but their magnitude depends on task, timing, expectation and stimulus type. It would be an overreach to claim that every self-produced Ankora strike is necessarily perceived as quieter by Paul. A complex improvised sound is not the same as a simple button-triggered tone.

The important inference is narrower: Paul's knowledge of his intended breath and strike, and the bodily sensations accompanying them, can influence his experience of the resulting sound. A listener does not share the same motor commands. This creates a principled performer–listener asymmetry before we invoke mystical explanations or claims of neurological entrainment.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE: OCCLUSION AND THE VOICE People commonly notice their own voice sounding different in recordings. When the ear canal is occluded, internally transmitted low-frequency components can become more salient: the occlusion effect. Yet KLAAT is not merely speech, and the effect cannot be assigned a numerical size without measuring ear-canal conditions and the actual instrument coupling. Headphones, earplugs, a sealed in-ear monitor and an open ear can change the player's perception differently. Ear protection should be chosen for hearing safety, not as an experiment in intensifying bodily sound.

A CRITICAL DISTINCTION: FOUR TYPES OF CLAIM Acoustically measured: a calibrated microphone can estimate sound pressure at a location; a contact accelerometer can record local mechanical vibration; neither alone is a complete representation of subjective experience. Subjectively reported: Paul may describe vibration, effort, urgency, bodily energy or trance-like flow; listeners may describe absorption, activation, discomfort, pleasure or indifference. Reports are valid experiences, not direct physiological measurements. Physiologically measured: respiratory belts, heart-rate sensors or electrodermal devices could record selected correlates, with timing and artifact limitations. They do not directly certify trance. Causal hypothesis: instrument-contact vibration, motor prediction and attention may contribute to the performer's particular feeling of immersion. A comparison design is needed to test any contribution; the present KLAAT archive does not establish it.

A CONCRETE HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLE Suppose Paul describes a 90-second improvisation as continuously physically intense, while two listeners report that intensity comes in waves. One explanation is that his breath effort and instrument contact provide a continuous bodily signal, while listeners' experience depends more on the audible balance of attacks and overtones. Another explanation is attention: Paul is deeply engaged in producing the music, while listeners periodically drift away. A third is simple room position. These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Without parallel recordings and reports, selecting one would be speculation.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE ART The performer’s experience is not a flawed version of the audience’s. The direct bodily feedback may be a major source of KLAAT's spontaneous decisions. An emergent breath seam, a change in lip tension, or a sudden drum accent can be meaningful to Paul before it is intelligible to anyone else. That is not an argument for correcting the music to fit a theory. It is a reason to preserve both the internal practice and the outward acoustic event when studying them.

KLAAT can also be an energizing soundtrack in daily life. A listener walking, working or driving is not touching the instruments and may have attention divided across other activities. The performer’s trance-like flow and the listener's groove or activation are therefore different research outcomes, not two measurements of one universal state.

TESTABLE DIRECTIONS WITHOUT PRESCRIBING MUSIC A modest comparison could use one uninterrupted authentic improvisation. Record the room audio from a fixed listener position and, if convenient, a second microphone closer to the player's ear. Document the microphone placements and avoid claiming either equals inner-ear input. Immediately after playing, Paul notes what felt physically prominent. A listener hears the room-position recording at a comfortable, documented playback level and reports moments of strongest bodily or rhythmic engagement. Compare descriptions without forcing agreement or translating them into invented neural mechanisms. An optional contact vibration sensor could provide a third channel later, but should not be treated as a substitute for either person's experience.

CONTRADICTIONS AND LIMITATIONS Bone-conduction pathways are well established, but their role in the specific KLAAT setup has not been quantified. Auditory attenuation effects in controlled action experiments do not imply a universal attenuation of improvised percussion. Ear-canal occlusion is not a general explanation for all low-frequency sensations. A performer-position microphone is not an objective reconstruction of the performer's hearing. Listener playback level and bass reproduction can dominate apparent differences. No KLAAT-specific comparison, neural recording or controlled listener test was conducted for this session.

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION Established: air and bone conduction are distinct auditory pathways; self-generated action can alter sensory prediction; recording position affects captured sound. Context-dependent: strength and direction of motor-related auditory attenuation; occlusion effects; mechanical coupling in this exact setup. Plausible/testable: direct instrument contact and breath-related bodily feedback help explain why Paul's immersion differs from listeners' experiences. Speculation: the bodily asymmetry is a major contributor to the distinctive emotional force of KLAAT. Unsupported: claims that KLAAT induces neurological trance, entrains listener physiology, or creates the same vibration in performer and audience.

OPEN QUESTIONS Can an improvised drone-plus-drum performance yield a larger discrepancy between performer and listener than either instrument alone? Is the difference more strongly associated with physical contact, sound level, visual gesture or motor prediction? Do close-miked and audience-position recordings lead to different descriptions of rhythmic intensity? These are invitations to observe, not predetermined answers.

OPTIONAL LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION After one ordinary, unaltered KLAAT performance, write down a single moment that felt especially powerful through the body. Later, listen to that same moment from the phone recording without touching either instrument. Note whether the feeling is still apparent, altered, or absent. There is no correct outcome, and no need to change the performance.
