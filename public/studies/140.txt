KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 140
THE PATTERN THAT APPEARED AFTER THE SEARCH
When discovery and confirmation use the same improvised sound, the same listeners, and the same decisions
Published: 2026-11-01, 08:15 CST (America/Chicago; UTC−06:00)


GOVERNING PRINCIPLE
Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.


RESEARCH QUESTION
Suppose an authentic KLAAT recording contains a passage where Amastacia's harmonic texture changes while Ankora's pulse grows more insistent. Several listeners describe a surge of energy. An analysis finds that one combination of spectral-flux window, pulse-density estimate, listener rating, and time lag predicts those reports. Has KLAAT discovered an acoustic mechanism of activation—or merely discovered the particular analysis that makes this small collection of observations look convincing?


This question is about the research process, not a proposed score. Paul does not need to play differently, choose a fixed tempo, add a staged crescendo, or suppress the spontaneous, bodily logic of Amastacia and Ankora. The work is to keep the difference between an interesting discovery and a tested conclusion visible.

2. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A FINDING AND A SEARCH RESULT


An audio file is not a single number. From the same sixty-six seconds, an analyst could compute amplitude in 100-ms, 250-ms, 500-ms, one-second, or two-second windows; detect drum onsets with several thresholds; quantify spectral flux with different frequency bands; compare the beginning with the middle, the middle with the end, or a hand-picked 'transition'; and align these measures with listener judgments using zero-, one-, two-, or five-second lags. Each choice may be defensible for some purpose.


A listener response is not a single number either. Someone may report energy, absorption, bodily pressure, enjoyment, irritation, imagined movement, perceived speed, or loss of time. A result can look strong for one outcome and weak for another. Selecting the strongest outcome after examining all of them is not the same as testing one outcome chosen beforehand.


There is nothing illegitimate about searching. Discovery is the proper function of exploratory research. The mistake occurs when the final selected relationship is described as though it was the one relationship specified before the evidence was examined. The result has effectively been asked to discover itself and validate itself.


Three different statements must remain separate:
(A) DESCRIPTIVE: In this recorded passage, measured spectral flux rose while three listeners independently described heightened energy.
(B) EXPLORATORY: Among the feature/lag combinations examined, this one aligned most closely with energy ratings.
(C) CONFIRMATORY: A feature/lag relationship specified before inspecting an independent evaluation set predicted the prespecified outcome in that set, with the stated uncertainty.


A and B can be useful and publishable as observations. Neither automatically licenses C. Even C does not establish a causal neural or autonomic mechanism; it establishes predictive performance under defined conditions.


3. PRIMARY EXPERIMENT: HOW ORDINARY FLEXIBILITY CHANGES FALSE-POSITIVE RISK


Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) studied researcher flexibility using simulated data with no true experimental effect. Their baseline design had twenty observations per condition. With one prespecified comparison and a conventional p < .05 threshold, the nominal false-positive rate was about five percent under the simulation's assumptions. Allowing analysts to choose among two correlated outcomes and their average produced 9.5% false positives. Allowing an optional additional ten observations per group yielded 7.7%. Selecting among subsets of three conditions yielded 12.6%. When four flexibility mechanisms were combined—outcome choice, optional sampling, covariate choice, and condition selection—the false-positive rate in their simulated search was 60.7% (15,000 simulated datasets per scenario).


That 60.7% is not a universal probability that music research is wrong, and certainly not a measured probability that KLAAT findings are false. It belongs to that study's particular null model and menu of analysis decisions. Its force is conceptual: a collection of individually reasonable-looking decisions can turn a nominal five-percent test into something very different if the decisions are selected after looking at the results.


For an even simpler illustration, suppose twenty truly independent null tests each have a five-percent false-positive probability. The probability of at least one nominally significant result is 1 − (0.95)^20 = approximately 64.2%. If tests are correlated, the calculation changes; if one test is genuinely non-null, the all-null illustration no longer applies. The point is not that every acoustic analysis is a lottery. It is that the number of opportunities to discover an apparently exceptional result matters.


KLAAT's possible choices are unusually numerous: attack threshold, harmonic-band boundary, loudness normalization, excerpt length, response lag, listener subgroup, listening device, first versus repeated hearing, and definition of 'activation.' If those choices are tuned to maximize the observed effect, the final p-value does not reflect the full search unless the procedure accounts for it.


4. THE ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY WARNING: A PEAK CHOSEN BECAUSE IT WAS HIGH


Luck and Gaspelin (2017) used simulations and reanalyses of event-related potential (ERP) data to show how choosing time windows and electrode locations from the same data used to test an effect can yield high rates of statistically significant but bogus findings. In many of the experimental designs they examined, the chance of at least one such result exceeded fifty percent.


An ERP study is not a KLAAT experiment, and an ERP component is not the same thing as a perceived beat or an audio amplitude envelope. The transfer is about analytic logic. Imagine recording EEG during KLAAT, looking across many frequencies, scalp channels, and moments until one striking peak appears, and then reporting a significance test at precisely that peak as though its location was specified independently. That is the risk. A striking graph can be the result of selection, not proof of neural entrainment.


A related but less technical version can occur with an ordinary phone recording. Suppose an analyst first listens to identify the moment that feels most intense, then measures several audio descriptors there, and finally reports that this moment has an unusually high descriptor value. If the same descriptor influenced the moment's selection, the comparison is partly circular. If the selection was purely subjective, the report may still be an interesting account of a moment, but it is not a blind, independent acoustic test.


5. DOUBLE DIPPING IS NOT A SYNONYM FOR ALL REUSE


Kriegeskorte and colleagues (2009) demonstrated that using the same noisy measurements both to select data and to estimate/test an effect can produce spurious apparent effects. They illustrated the problem with noise-only data in neuroimaging. Their important qualification is that selection is damaging when the statistic being reported is not independent of the selection rule under the relevant null hypothesis. Reusing data is not automatically forbidden. It matters what is selected, how, and what claim follows.


For KLAAT, these are different situations:
- Selecting an Ankora strike by visible hand contact, without seeing its acoustic spectrum, then describing the spectrum: potentially a defensible observational selection, though video timing and recording biases remain.
- Selecting the strike because it has the largest measured low-frequency energy, then estimating the 'typical' energy of KLAAT strikes from that selected strike: biased by design.
- Selecting the passage with the largest listener-rated energy and then saying that passage has the largest listener-rated energy: tautological, not an independent finding.
- Selecting a promising acoustic feature from one set of listeners, then testing its relationship with a separately recruited set hearing an unchanged recording: a possible route to genuine out-of-sample evidence, provided decisions were frozen before the new responses were inspected.


There are still complications. Different listeners hearing the same take provide a test of generalization across listeners, not independent replication across performances. Different excerpts from the same improvisation provide neither independent listeners nor independent performances. The unit of replication must match the claim.


6. SEVENTY ANALYST TEAMS, ONE DATASET


Botvinik-Nezer and colleagues (2020) asked seventy independent teams to test nine previously specified hypotheses using the same functional-MRI dataset. No two teams used identical analysis workflows. Their statistical conclusions varied substantially, even when intermediate maps looked similar. Aggregating across teams also yielded areas of consensus, so the study does not imply that analysis is hopeless or that every conclusion is arbitrary. It shows that reasonable methodological decisions can materially change conclusions.


A related study by Silberzahn and colleagues (2018) assigned the same observational soccer-refereeing dataset and question to twenty-nine analysis teams. Twenty teams reported a statistically significant positive association and nine did not. Their estimated odds ratios ranged from 0.89 to 2.93. The research question and data were shared; analytic choices differed. Those numbers concern refereeing data, not music or KLAAT, and the study does not establish which analysis was correct. It demonstrates how an analyst's choices can be part of the outcome.


This matters when the KLAAT project eventually uses software to extract features from raw recordings. Two competent analysts could use different onset detectors, band limits, smoothing kernels, loudness normalization, or rules for ambiguous breath sounds and produce different numbers. The scientifically useful response is to retain the settings, expose the alternatives, and test whether the substantive conclusion survives them.


7. FOUR MULTIPLICITIES THAT SHOULD NOT BE COLLAPSED


FIRST: MULTIPLE QUESTIONS. Asking whether KLAAT increases absorption, enjoyment, alertness, and bodily sensation gives four outcomes. These may be correlated, but they are not interchangeable. A 'positive on at least one' claim has different error behavior from one specified primary outcome.


SECOND: MULTIPLE DEFINITIONS OF ONE QUANTITY. Spectral flux can depend on window length, overlap, frequency weighting, and normalization. Pulse density can mean acoustic peaks, independently initiated hand gestures, or listener-inferred beats; Session 139 and the recent hourly note on multi-contact strikes remind us not to confuse distinct observables.


THIRD: MULTIPLE CHANCES TO LOOK. If results are inspected after each new listener and recruitment stops when a favorable p-value appears, ordinary fixed-sample inference is not automatically valid. Sequential designs can be legitimate, but their stopping and error-control rules must be specified.


FOURTH: MULTIPLE UNITS. A thousand overlapping audio frames are not a thousand independent performances. Ten clips from one take do not equal ten independent takes. Ten listeners rating one take may provide information about listener variability for that take, but not the full distribution of KLAAT improvisations. This is dependence and sampling, not merely multiple comparisons.


Treating all four as one problem leads to bad remedies. Adjusting p-values does not fix a mislabeled gesture, a phone microphone's automatic gain control, an unrepresentative listener sample, or a failure to replicate across independent performances.


8. WHAT CORRECTION CAN AND CANNOT DO


One possible response to multiple statistical tests is to control a family-wise error rate or a false discovery rate. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (1995), for example, provides a way to control the expected proportion of false discoveries among rejected hypotheses under its stated assumptions, including relevant dependence conditions. It is not a universal filter for truth. It does not turn correlation into causation, restore a missing experimental control, or remove selection bias created before the tested family was defined.


Nor should every exploratory KLAAT note be burdened with formal hypothesis testing. If Paul records an extraordinary five-minute passage and someone describes it as 'I felt it in my chest,' the first responsibility is to preserve the audio, the exact language, and the listening circumstances. A single sincere response is an observation; it does not need a p-value to exist. It only needs a p-value if a particular statistical claim is being made, and then the design must justify the number.


9. THE DISCOVERY / VERIFICATION SPLIT


The practical advance is to give KLAAT two explicitly different research modes.


DISCOVERY MODE is open. Listen, notice, compare, annotate, and generate ideas. Try different onset algorithms, look at unexpected spectral changes, identify moments that listeners independently remember, and keep all the surprising nulls. The original recordings are not altered; the performer is not constrained. Label resulting relationships 'exploratory.' Record what was tried, even imperfectly. The goal is learning what may be worth a more careful test.


VERIFICATION MODE is narrower. Before inspecting the evaluation evidence, specify the exact question; which original recordings and listeners will count; how acoustic features and outcomes will be measured; what preprocessing, exclusions, and lags are allowed; and what result would count as support, contradiction, or uncertainty. Then apply the frozen plan to evidence not used to select it.


These are not two styles of music. They are two labels for how knowledge is produced. Spontaneous improvisation remains the source of both.


A crucial nuance: if the same listeners hear the same passage twice, the second presentation is not automatically independent confirmation. Familiarity, demand characteristics, carryover, and changed attention may matter. If the new test uses different listeners but only one take, it supports a narrower listener-generalization claim. If it uses new takes but the same listener, it supports a different generalization. The strongest design depends on what question KLAAT actually asks.


10. AN AUTHENTIC KLAAT EXAMPLE, WITHOUT PRETENDING WE HAVE DATA


Imagine Paul selects a naturally occurring thirty-second passage from a phone recording because it contains a striking combination of Amastacia rasp and Ankora pulse. The passage is not fabricated or edited. Four friends hear it. Two say 'energizing,' one says 'almost hypnotic,' and one says 'too intense.' An analyst calculates ten acoustic features at five window sizes and tries four lag choices against several coded listener outcomes. One association looks exceptionally strong.


What is measured? There are acoustic feature values for this one recorded passage, plus four genuine subjective reports. What is not measured? There is no independently replicated KLAAT activation effect, no validated neural coupling, no known causal role for spectral flux, and no population-level probability of trance. With four people and a single selected passage, the analysis is hypothesis-generating at best.


A reasonable next step, if ever wanted, would be to retain the same unchanged recording, specify one feature/outcome relationship, and test it on independently collected listener reports. Better still, for claims about KLAAT generally, include multiple independently improvised takes sampled by a documented rule rather than choosing only the most persuasive excerpt. There is no requirement that Paul produce particular musical gestures for the test.


Suppose the new listeners do not show the predicted association. That does not mean the first friends lied, the music is ineffective, or the artistic experience was illusory. It means the specific generalizing prediction was not supported under the tested conditions. A failed prediction is information; it can redirect research toward listener differences, listening context, measurement validity, or the possibility that the original relationship was chance.


11. THE AESTHETIC BENEFIT OF NOT OVERFITTING THE ART


A statistical trap can become an artistic trap if KLAAT begins chasing whichever acoustic feature happened to correlate with a favorable rating in a tiny sample. An analyst might say, 'Increase spectral flux here,' or 'hold this BPM because it scored higher,' or 'repeat this transition because it produced the biggest autonomic peak.' None of those prescriptions follows from an exploratory association. Worse, they would reverse KLAAT's stated hierarchy by making a fragile analysis govern a long-evolving improvisational practice.


The alternative is not anti-scientific. Technical improvement can make spontaneous playing more fluent; better microphones can preserve more of the actual acoustic event; carefully collected listening reports can reveal real differences between people and settings. Science can refine technique, capture, interpretation, and test design without dictating the composition.


This distinction is especially important for KLAAT's everyday energetic identity. 'People sometimes use this sound while moving, working, or concentrating' is a meaningful observation. 'This acoustic configuration reliably improves productivity or induces a specific brain state' is a much stronger claim and requires a different standard of evidence. Do not convert a useful soundtrack into a neurological claim simply because a waveform has attractive regularity.


12. WHAT COUNTS AS A STRONG RESULT?


ESTABLISHED OUTSIDE KLAAT:
- Choosing among many tests and reporting only favorable ones can inflate nominal false-positive rates; the size depends on the choices and data-generating process.
- Selection and testing on non-independent data can create circular inference.
- Different defensible analysis pipelines can yield different conclusions from the same dataset.
- Independent evaluation and transparent reporting reduce particular kinds of overfitting and concealment; neither eliminates every bias.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT:
- The appropriate unit of independent evidence depends on whether the claim concerns listeners, takes, venues, or specific acoustic moments.
- The best feature window and lag depend on the measurement target, instrument acoustics, and the time scale of the listener response. No universal KLAAT window or 'correct' feature is known.
- Statistical adjustments depend on the defined family of hypotheses and dependence assumptions; they do not fix poor measurement or confounding.
- Preregistration is valuable for confirmatory claims, but a badly chosen prespecified test can still be uninformative.


PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES:
- Some correlations between Ankora attack density, Amastacia texture, and listener activation will weaken when tested on independent listeners or independent takes.
- A subset of relationships may prove robust across reasonable feature definitions and playback contexts.
- Individual listener preferences may moderate effects more strongly than any single acoustic descriptor.
- Clear separation of exploratory and confirmatory results will improve the research project's ability to distinguish durable findings from interesting one-offs.


SPECULATION:
- KLAAT may eventually identify stable families of perceptual response rather than one universal 'resonance score.' No data yet establish those families.
- Improvisational variability might help reveal which relationships are robust across natural performance changes, but only if the sampling design is documented.


UNSUPPORTED:
- KLAAT already causes neural entrainment, trance, autonomic synchronization, therapeutic effects, or a special C# physiological response.
- A single significant correlation proves an acoustic cause of a listener's experience.
- Twenty time windows or twenty listeners from one take constitute twenty independent KLAAT performances.
- A favorable machine-learning prediction tested on the same data used to tune it is independently validated.
- Preregistration, correction, or a sophisticated sensor alone guarantees truth.


13. A DURABLE CLAIM-LABELING RULE FOR THE RESEARCH LEDGER


Every future KLAAT feature-to-response claim should be accompanied by four small labels:


OBSERVATION: What was actually measured or said, and by whom? Example: 'Ankora onset detections per second increased in this recording; a listener described greater energy.'
SELECTION: Was the passage, feature, lag, or outcome chosen before or after looking at the same result? Example: 'This window was selected after exploratory review.'
REPLICATION UNIT: What is independently sampled—listener, performance, recording, session, venue, or none? Example: 'One take; four listeners; no independent performance replication.'
CLAIM STATUS: Descriptive, exploratory, prespecified test, externally replicated, or still unknown. Example: 'Exploratory association; not a causal or physiological finding.'


This is a reporting discipline, not a new rule for how Paul should play. It makes a rich, imperfect archive scientifically usable without rewriting its history.


14. OPTIONAL, LOW-PRESSURE KLAAT OBSERVATION


Take one existing unedited recording that Paul already likes. Without asking anyone to listen yet, write down ONE question it raises—perhaps, 'Do other listeners independently describe this section as energizing?' Preserve the exact audio and the wording of that question. If Paul later shares it, invite the listener's own description before mentioning the prediction. Whatever they say, keep the original wording and label the exercise exploratory unless a genuine independent test plan was established. Nothing about the music needs to change.


The research advance is not a magic threshold, a preferred BPM, or a command to improvise scientifically. It is a clear boundary: what we noticed can inspire what we test; what we noticed is not already the test.
