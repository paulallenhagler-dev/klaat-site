KLAAT RESONANCE RESEARCH — SESSION 89
THE PULSE BETWEEN THE INSTRUMENTS
Rhythmic roles, motor invitation, and why groove is not the same as movement


Published: October 15, 2026 — 8:18 AM CDT (America/Chicago)
Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.
Status: Canonical published study. KLAAT-specific effects remain untested unless explicitly identified otherwise.


CENTRAL FINDING


Session 88 asked whether a spontaneous musical change simply surprises the listener or changes the expectation governing what follows. A stronger unresolved question lies underneath that distinction: when the ear begins expecting Ankora's pulse, what makes the body want to join it? Is it the number of strikes, the irregularity of their timing, their low-frequency weight, the interplay with Amastacia, the listener's learned meter, or some combination? And can someone experience a pleasurable urge to move while remaining physically still and deeply absorbed?


Newer primary experiments complicate a familiar story that “moderate syncopation makes music groove.” A 2025 study found that the relation between syncopation and the pleasurable urge to move depended strongly on the number and identity of rhythmic layers. Another 2025 study found that the often-reported moderate-complexity preference depended on whether Western listeners heard familiar 4/4 meter or less familiar meters. A 2024 neurophysiological study found rhythmic attention in EEG and pupil measurements without a corresponding change in recorded calf-muscle activity. These findings converge on a more useful research principle: a listener's rhythmic experience is an interaction among the acoustic signal, the perceived temporal reference, learned expectations, and the listener's body—not a simple function of how busy the drum is.


For KLAAT this is especially promising, because its sound is neither a conventional drum kit nor a fixed metrical score. Ankora's repeated attacks coexist with Amastacia's sustained C# drone, breath accents, overtone changes, noise bursts, and the variable relationship between the two. The central opportunity is to investigate that naturally occurring relationship without adding instruments, prescribing syncopation, forcing a four-beat meter, or telling Paul to “build groove” according to a laboratory optimum.


GOVERNING PROPOSITION: A RHYTHM CAN INVITE MOVEMENT WITHOUT PRODUCING MOVEMENT; A CONTINUOUS DRONE CAN SHAPE RHYTHMIC EXPECTATION WITHOUT BECOMING A DRUM; AND NEITHER EFFECT IS PROOF OF TRANCE.


1. WHAT “GROOVE” MEANS IN THIS RESEARCH


The word groove is used in several incompatible ways. Musicians may mean a compelling ensemble feel, a particular rhythmic pattern, or a quality of timing. Experimental psychologists often operationalize groove as the pleasurable urge to move to music: a rating given after hearing a stimulus. We will use “urge to move” when referring to that specific outcome and reserve “groove” for the broader perceptual and artistic phenomenon.


Ankora's sound can provide an audible temporal reference, but four measurements must be kept apart. First, the recording can contain measurable attack times and inter-onset intervals. Second, a listener can report a beat or an easy-to-follow pulse. Third, a listener can report wanting to tap, sway, walk, or move. Fourth, a listener may actually move in ways that can be observed. These four observations need not covary. Pleasure and absorption form two additional dimensions; neither can be inferred from a foot tap or a waveform.


This matters for KLAAT's two legitimate listening contexts. During attentive listening, a person may be still yet feel a strong internal pulse. During drawing, housework, walking, or another ordinary activity, Ankora may support an energizing sense of motion without becoming the primary object of attention. Neither context should be treated as a failed version of a ceremonial listening experience.


2. THE CLASSIC INVERTED U: USEFUL, BUT NOT A COMPOSITIONAL LAW


Witek and colleagues (2014) asked participants to rate funk drum breaks that varied in syncopation. Their online experiment found the strongest reported pleasure and desire to move at intermediate rather than minimal or maximal syncopation. A key detail is that the relationship was stronger among people who enjoyed dancing. The study also reported that a simple audio-entropy measure was a poor predictor of the responses. It therefore does not license a universal equation in which “more entropy” or “more off-beats” automatically produces a better experience. [S1]


Matthews and colleagues (2019) extended this work by varying rhythmic and harmonic complexity. They again found an inverted-U relationship for rhythmic complexity, but high harmonic complexity weakened the effect of rhythm on pleasure. Musical training also altered the pattern. In other words, the apparent influence of rhythm depended on what else was heard and on the listener. [S2]


The distinction between syncopation and sheer density is important. Syncopation concerns a listener's inferred metrical expectations: an event may arrive at a weaker position or displace an expected strong-position event. Attack density merely counts how often something happens. A rapid Ankora passage can be dense but predictable. A relatively sparse passage can be surprising if a well-established strike is omitted or displaced. A pulse that feels heavy or inevitable can be produced without conventional syncopation at all.


Nothing in these experiments establishes an optimal KLAAT drum pattern. The source material was mostly Western groove-oriented music, often with fixed instrumentation, short clips, and explicit rating tasks. Paul's free improvisations and the sustained acoustic presence of Amastacia were not tested.


3. THE 2025 RESULT: RHYTHMIC LAYERS CHANGE THE COMPLEXITY EFFECT


Seeberg, Matthews, Højlund, Vuust, and Petersen (2025) tested the effect of rhythmic instrumentation directly in two online studies (




n = 108 and n = 46). In each study, listeners rated twelve rhythmic patterns presented in four instrument configurations, for 48 stimuli per study. The configurations varied whether the rhythm contained a single instrument, pairs of drum-kit elements, or the full bass-drum/snare/hi-hat arrangement. The investigators compared degrees of syncopation while asking separately about pleasure and desire to move. [S3]


The fully instrumented rhythms showed the strongest inverted-U relationship between syncopation and urge to move. Single-layer versions showed a much flatter relationship. Adding a hi-hat could provide a recurring temporal reference; bass-drum components appeared especially consequential in the tested materials. The effects were not identical across the two studies, and the second sample was smaller. These results support an interaction among rhythm, timbre, and the roles played by different sound sources. They do not demonstrate that three instruments are inherently better than one or two.


This is the strongest new connection to KLAAT. Amastacia and Ankora are two physical instruments, but the listener may encounter more than two perceptually meaningful streams. Ankora may supply a recurring low attack and variable higher-frequency slap or edge accents. Amastacia may contribute a nearly continuous drone, overtone fluctuations, breath-driven noise, and occasional emphatic articulations. The room may extend or blur some attacks. A listener might use Ankora's pulse as the reference against which Amastacia's irregular breath accents are heard, or sometimes the reverse: an emphatic, repeating Amastacia gesture may make a drum accent feel newly placed.


This is a hypothesis about perceptual grouping, not a declaration that every breath articulation is an independent rhythmic layer. We would need to identify which acoustic components are actually separable, whether listeners hear them as separate streams, and whether those streams contribute to beat clarity or bodily engagement. Crucially, the result does not suggest that Paul should add a hi-hat, force extra subdivisions, or change KLAAT's minimalism. The existing sounds already provide the research material.


4. A SECOND 2025 RESULT: THE METER A LISTENER BRINGS MATTERS


Spiech, Câmara, Fuhrer, and Penhune (published December 16, 2025) tested Western listeners across three behavioral experiments with samples of 143, 120, and 120 people, respectively. They used 63 short real-music clips spanning familiar 4/4 and less familiar meters, with rhythmic complexity estimated using pulse entropy and validated against listener judgments. They found that moderate complexity yielded the greatest urge to move in common 4/4, while simpler rhythms tended to yield the greatest urge in the non-4/4 set. [S4]


The result is a direct warning against exporting one complexity curve to every rhythm. It suggests that groove depends partly on the listener's learned metrical model. The same apparent amount of complexity may be engaging in a familiar framework and difficult to follow in an unfamiliar one. The researchers also noted that a waveform-based pulse-entropy model can mischaracterize music whose perceptually important low-frequency and percussion layers are simpler than its overall onset pattern.


Several caveats limit transfer. Participants were recruited from Western countries. The stimuli were short, typically at 110–130 BPM, with at least three musical layers and no vocals. The meter labels were musicological judgments, and listeners may hear multiple possible groupings. The studies were not preregistered. They do not establish that 4/4 is intrinsically better, that other cultures respond identically, or that Ankora's provisional approximately 137 BPM reference has special psychological value.


For KLAAT, the point is not to label every passage with a time signature. A listener may perceive a stable beat while being unsure of its higher-level grouping; another may feel a slower pulse or different grouping in the same Ankora pattern. We should treat metrical interpretation as an observation to ask about, not an objective score to impose on an improvisation.


5. THE 2024 NEUROPHYSIOLOGY: RHYTHMIC ATTENTION IS NOT OBSERVED DANCING


A 2024 study titled “Oscillatory attention in groove” recorded pupil size and scalp EEG while participants listened to three drumbeats varying in rhythmic complexity. The researchers found that beat-related pupil and EEG phase consistency generally weakened as the rhythm became more complex. Sustained pupil dilation was largest for the medium-complexity rhythm. One reported comparison found F(2,56) = 9.417, p < .001 for pupil dilation across the three complexity conditions after exclusion of an outlier. [S5]


The revealing counterpoint is that the study did not detect corresponding rhythmic-complexity effects in the calf-muscle electrical activity it measured. The authors explicitly cautioned that their neural and pupil findings should be interpreted as lower-level perceptual processes rather than evidence of a higher-order motor response. Participants were constrained in a laboratory posture and repeated the same three rhythms many times, so lack of calf activity does not mean no one ever wants to move to music. But it does mean that a rhythmic neural or pupil signature cannot be casually described as actual movement.


Zalta, Large, Schön, and Morillon (2024) used magnetoencephalography while people listened to melodies with manipulated syncopation and rated groove. They reported beat-related 2-Hz activity and groove-associated dynamics at approximately 1.4 Hz and 20–30 Hz in auditory–motor networks. Their interpretation concerns predictive timing and motor-system engagement. It is not a demonstration that every listener physically moves or enters trance, and these rates are specific to the experimental stimuli and analysis. [S6]


A useful methodological warning follows. Neural oscillation frequency is not the same as Amastacia's acoustic fundamental, Ankora's strike rate, amplitude-modulation rate, or the frequency of floor vibration. A 2-Hz neural correlate in a melody experiment cannot be mapped onto a 137-BPM drum performance simply because both involve repeated events. In particular, neither EEG nor MEG findings from other music justify claiming that KLAAT induces theta, synchronizes brainwaves, or causes altered consciousness.


6. CONTRADICTORY EVIDENCE: HIGH COMPLEXITY CAN WORK IN THE RIGHT CONTEXT


Duncan and Orgs (2024) tested 179 participants with twenty novel house-music clips that manipulated low-frequency amplitude and syncopation. Groove ratings were highest when both were high, and low-frequency amplitude made a substantial difference. This does not fit a simple universal “medium complexity is always best” rule. Instead it reinforces the importance of musical genre, sound spectrum, and listener preferences. [S7]


A separate audio–tactile study by Hove and colleagues (2020) tested 22 listeners in a car/subwoofer setup and 18 using a vibrotactile backpack. Adding bodily vibration increased some groove and enjoyment ratings, tapping force, and—in the first experiment—observed spontaneous movement. But that result depends on deliberately applied tactile stimulation, not merely the existence of a low note. [S8]


The combined evidence is more useful than either headline alone. The perceived urge to move can be influenced by metrical expectations, sound-source roles, rhythmic complexity, and physical delivery. The direction of influence can change with context. A loud or low-frequency Ankora attack is not interchangeable with a synchronized vibration delivered to the body. An increase in urge-to-move ratings is not the same as a measured increase in movement, and neither necessarily implies stronger musical absorption.


7. WHAT WE CAN ACTUALLY SAY ABOUT AMASTACIA AND ANKORA


We know that Amastacia is a C# agave didgeridoo and that Ankora is an elk-hide frame drum. A prior 66-second reference was provisionally estimated to contain an Ankora pulse around 137 BPM, but that is not a measured constant for all KLAAT recordings. We know from Paul's practice description that the music is breath-led, repetitive, often physically demanding, and substantially improvised. These are project observations, not laboratory measurements.


We do not yet have a controlled comparison of actual KLAAT recordings that isolates beat clarity, breath accents, groove ratings, bodily movement, or listener absorption. We therefore cannot say which sound layer is “responsible” for activation. We cannot say whether listeners perceive Ankora as 4/4, another meter, a loose recurring pulse, or a shifting collection of events. Nor can we claim that Paul's personal flow transfers into the listener's motor system.


There are, however, several concrete possibilities worth investigating retrospectively. Ankora's repeated attacks may create a stable temporal reference even when accent placement changes. Amastacia's breath articulation may sometimes act as a secondary rhythmic cue, but in other moments may simply enrich the texture without functioning as a beat. A listener might feel compelled to move when both instruments are strongly differentiated, or might prefer a more unified, blended acoustic mass. A listener could experience high absorption with little movement urge, high movement urge with modest absorption, or both simultaneously.


These possibilities are not a reason to change the sound. They are reasons to preserve the real sound accurately and ask better questions about it.


8. TECHNIQUE WITHOUT COMPOSITIONAL CONTROL


The science offers one indirect, useful implication for practice: greater physical fluency can make naturally occurring relationships more legible without prescribing those relationships. Better circular breathing may allow Paul to sustain Amastacia's drone while retaining freedom to articulate breath and harmonics. Greater independence between Ankora's striking hand and Amastacia's breath may reduce accidental disruptions that Paul does not intend, while leaving deliberate roughness, asymmetry, and spontaneous changes untouched.


But technique should not be confused with sterilization. A slightly uneven strike, a rough breath edge, or a momentary collision between drum and drone may be artistically central. The test of technical improvement is whether Paul has more choices and less unwanted effort, not whether he performs a mathematically uniform pulse. The same principle applies to transitions: fluency should allow an instinctive transition to emerge cleanly when it emerges, not require it to occur at a prescribed point.


KLAAT's ancient, ritual, archaeosonic identity is also not reducible to rhythmic complexity. The visual world, material character of the instruments, sound texture, performance context, and duration can shape expectation and meaning. The cited groove studies do not isolate these elements. Their value is explanatory, not governing.


9. A BETTER CAUSAL MAP


The simplest claim—“Ankora has more subdivisions, therefore listeners are activated”—skips too many links. A more defensible map is:


Acoustic event pattern and timbre -> listener's inferred beat, grouping, and source roles -> perceived predictability/complexity -> reported urge to move and pleasure -> possible actual movement, depending on posture, context, preference, and task.


Amastacia's drone and breath articulation can influence several of these links without functioning as a conventional beat. The playback chain can alter low-frequency weight and attack clarity. Repetition can alter the inferred model. Attention can shift between the music and a daily activity. None of these arrows is yet a demonstrated KLAAT-specific causal relationship; the map identifies variables and plausible pathways for future testing.


Even the word “activation” needs care. It might mean an energized subjective feeling, increased urge to move, observable movement, a change in heart rate, skin conductance, pupil size, or some other physiological measurement. These are not interchangeable. KLAAT's goal of activation need not imply maximum arousal, continuous dancing, or uniform response across listeners. An energizing everyday soundtrack can succeed without making the listener consciously analyze its meter.


10. EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION


ESTABLISHED IN THE CITED STUDY CONDITIONS


Intermediate syncopation often increases reported pleasure and urge to move in familiar groove-oriented stimuli. The effect depends on musical context and listener characteristics. The number and timbral roles of rhythmic layers can change the syncopation–urge relationship. Familiar versus unfamiliar meter can change which rhythmic complexity produces the highest groove ratings among Western listeners. EEG, MEG, and pupillometry can track rhythmic processing in experiments without proving actual movement. Adding physical vibration can affect some movement and engagement outcomes.


CONTEXT-DEPENDENT OR CONTRADICTORY


The inverted-U effect is not universal. High syncopation paired with strong low-frequency energy increased groove ratings in a house-music study. Meter familiarity and instrumentation modify the response. Short online clips, drum-kit stimuli, head-fixed laboratory listening, and real concert experiences are not equivalent to a long KLAAT improvisation. Observed movement, urge to move, pleasure, and absorption can dissociate. All claims about bodily effects depend on sound level, device, room, posture, and whether mechanical vibration actually reaches the listener.


PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESES


H1. Listeners may report a clearer temporal reference during naturally occurring passages in which Ankora's pulse remains audible beneath Amastacia's evolving timbre.
H2. Some Amastacia breath articulations may be perceived as independent rhythmic cues, while others may be perceived as texture only.
H3. Listener-rated urge to move may differ from rated absorption and from visible movement; one need not predict the others.
H4. Familiarity with a KLAAT recording may change how listeners group Ankora's accents without any acoustic change in the file.
H5. The relationship between instruments, not Ankora's attack density alone, may predict which moments feel energizing.
H6. Focused listening and everyday-activity listening may produce different relationships among groove, absorption, and enjoyment.


SPECULATION


The tension between a sustained human-breath drone and a recurring hand-struck pulse may create a distinctive “living clock” experience. Its power may come from recognizably human irregularities within a stable sonic identity. The archaeosonic aesthetic may influence how listeners interpret those irregularities as ancient, bodily, or ritualistic. These are artistic hypotheses; they have not been validated by KLAAT-specific listener studies.


UNSUPPORTED CLAIMS


There is no evidence that KLAAT's C# drone, approximately 137-BPM reference, a particular syncopation ratio, or a certain number of rhythmic layers uniquely induces trance, theta activity, autonomic regulation, or healing. A groove rating is not proof of motor entrainment; EEG phase consistency is not proof of bodily movement; a subjective sense of vibration is not proof of measured tissue resonance. No scientifically established “optimal KLAAT groove” exists.


11. METHODOLOGY ADVANCEMENT — THE FOUR-OUTCOME MOTOR GATE


Session 89 adds a research safeguard to the existing Before/Change/After and Listener-Defined Chapter frameworks. When studying a KLAAT passage, keep four outcomes separate:


A. Perceived beat or pulse clarity: can the listener find a recurring temporal reference?
B. Pleasurable urge to move: does the music invite tapping, walking, swaying, or another movement?
C. Actual movement: did the listener move, and how was that observed?
D. Absorption or felt energy: did the listener become inwardly engaged or energized, whether or not they moved?


Add enjoyment as a separate rating rather than assuming it equals any of the above. Record listening context, playback system, volume comfort, familiarity, and whether movement was constrained by the task. Where possible, preserve the free description before structured questions. Acoustic measurements—attack times, inter-onset variability, RMS level, spectral features, and audible breath gestures—belong in a different data column from listener reports.


Do not call a passage “syncopated” solely because its attack timings are irregular; syncopation presupposes a listener-relevant metrical framework. Do not treat a listener's chosen tap rate as the one true tempo. And do not collapse multiple outcomes into one KLAAT trance or activation score.


This gate is an interpretive tool for research after performance, not a demand for new layers, a prescribed tempo, or a score. Ground Anchor, Threshold Gate, Rising Pulse, and Return Path remain optional artistic descriptions of what happened—not instructions for what must happen.


12. KRX-089 — THE PULSE YOU FEEL WITHOUT MOVING (OPTIONAL)


Choose one existing, unedited KLAAT recording with Amastacia and Ankora. Use two naturally different 45–90-second excerpts from it—one where Ankora seems especially distinct against the drone, another where the sounds feel more blended. Do not create new musical versions or manipulate the audio. If no such contrast is obvious, simply use one excerpt; the observation is still useful.


Invite four to eight listeners to hear the excerpts at comfortable volume, preferably on the same playback system. Present the order alternately across listeners if using two excerpts. Before any ratings, ask for a spontaneous description: “What did this passage make you want to do, if anything?” Then ask separate, simple 0–10 ratings for pulse clarity, urge to move, absorption, felt energy, and enjoyment. Ask whether they actually moved, but do not require or discourage movement. If the listener was sitting still, that is not a zero for urge to move. Record whether they knew the music beforehand.


Compare individual patterns rather than forcing a group winner. A passage might produce strong urge to move but modest absorption; another might produce intense absorption without movement. If listeners disagree, preserve the disagreement. A sample this small is exploratory and cannot establish a population effect or causal mechanism. No musical revision follows automatically from the result.
