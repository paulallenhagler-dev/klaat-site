THE PEAK BETWEEN THE SAMPLES

2026-11-07 15:16 CST

SESSION 156 / 164Recording & PlaybackRESEARCH SESSION

GUIDING PRINCIPLE: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.

RESEARCH QUESTION Can a faithful digital capture of Amastacia and Ankora contain no samples at or above digital full scale, yet produce an inter-sample peak during reconstruction or export that exceeds the nominal digital ceiling? What does that imply for preserving the force and texture of spontaneous strikes?

WHY THIS QUESTION NOW Sessions 148–155 addressed recorder-clock drift, membrane modes, timing variability, low-frequency weighting, hearing-aid processing, envelope rhythm, background listening and spectral averaging. The Nov 7 13:15 hourly note isolates a separate unresolved issue: reconstruction peaks after sampling, not simply digital sample clipping or microphone overload. It offers a mathematically testable example. The 14:17 note instead concerns temperature-dependent microphone sensitivity; the 12:15 note concerns misleading correlations. This session selects the reconstruction question because it directly bears on fidelity of existing recordings and is not another statistical description of musical structure. The hourly note remains intact as provenance.

1. WHAT A DIGITAL SAMPLE PEAK ACTUALLY TELLS US A PCM file is a sequence of discrete numbers. Its sample peak is the largest absolute stored sample, conventionally expressed in dBFS relative to full scale. A maximum of -1 dBFS means no stored sample reaches the nominal full-scale amplitude. It does NOT guarantee that the smooth waveform produced by a reconstruction filter will remain below that threshold between samples. An interpolated waveform can have a larger maximum than its stored samples. This is a mathematical feature of sampling and reconstruction, not evidence of poor playing or a mystical property of bass.

A true-peak meter estimates the reconstructed waveform's maximum using oversampling and filtering, reporting dBTP. A true-peak estimate is not the same as a measured acoustic sound-pressure level, and it is not an exact measurement of the voltage produced by every possible converter. True peak is a useful risk indicator for processing and playback headroom. The meter's filter, oversampling factor and algorithm matter.

2. A QUANTITATIVE EXAMPLE THAT CAN BE CHECKED Consider the hypothetical sinusoid x(t) = A cos(2πft + φ), sampled at fs, with f = fs/4 and φ = π/4. The sample values are ±A/√2; their largest magnitude is 0.7071068 A. The continuous-time waveform reaches A between sampling instants. The difference is 20 log10(A/(A/√2)) = 20 log10(√2) = 3.0103 dB. If the stored sample peak were -2.0 dBFS under this idealized construction, the reconstructed peak would reach approximately +1.01 dB relative to the same nominal ceiling.

This is an intentionally constructed, phase-dependent example, NOT a typical overshoot estimate, NOT an analysis of any KLAAT recording, and NOT a prediction that a real DAC must audibly clip. Real musical signals are broadband, time-varying and filtered. The illustrative sine lies within the Nyquist band, but practical reconstruction, codecs and processing can produce different results. The number 3.01 dB is not a universal inter-sample ceiling.

3. WHY ANKORA AND AMASTACIA ARE RELEVANT Ankora produces abrupt attacks with evolving decay and spectral content; Amastacia produces a sustained drone with breath-shaped harmonic and noisy fluctuations. Their combination can create local peaks that a sample-peak-only inspection misses. The fact that a drum attack is sharp does not by itself prove it will create a large inter-sample overshoot. The shape and phase of the bandlimited waveform, capture filters and subsequent processing determine the risk. Amastacia's continuous energy also changes the combined waveform's instantaneous peaks: two components can add or partially cancel. A strikingly loud-looking transient in a waveform display is not proof of audible clipping; conversely a display showing no flat-topped samples does not rule out a later reconstruction overshoot.

This is particularly important if a short phone video is edited, converted to compressed audio, normalized for publication or played on a different device. Sample-rate conversion, lossy encoding, equalization and limiting may change reconstructed peaks. The original performance may be entirely sound while a derived export distorts. We should not blame breath support, striking force or rhythmic coordination for a defect introduced downstream.

4. THREE DIFFERENT FAILURE POINTS First, analog front-end overload: a microphone capsule, preamplifier or input circuit can distort before conversion. Lowering a digital file's level afterward cannot restore lost waveform detail. Second, sample clipping: the converter or digital processing creates values at the digital ceiling, potentially flattening stored peaks. Third, inter-sample or reconstruction overs: the stored samples remain below full scale, but an oversampled estimate or downstream conversion crosses a nominal ceiling. These problems can coexist but should never be treated as synonyms. Phone automatic gain control, compression and noise reduction introduce still more confounds. A clean true-peak reading cannot certify that the original microphone never overloaded.

5. ESTABLISHED, CONDITIONAL, HYPOTHETICAL AND UNSUPPORTED ESTABLISHED ENGINEERING: sample maxima and reconstructed maxima need not coincide; oversampled true-peak estimators address this distinction. ITU-R BS.1770 defines a standardized framework for programme loudness and true-peak estimation; BS.1771 addresses meter requirements. The mathematical example above follows directly from sinusoidal sampling.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: the magnitude and audibility of reconstruction overs depend on signal, filters, resampling, codec, gain staging and playback headroom. Not all dBTP values above zero cause audible distortion on all systems. Some software and devices accommodate overs without clipping; others do not. Audible artifacts should be evaluated with matched-level listening and, where possible, measurements of the actual output path.

PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESIS: certain naturally occurring Ankora attacks in a phone recording might acquire higher reconstructed peaks after conversion than the original sample-peak reading suggests. A separate hypothesis is that an export could alter perceived attack roughness or punch while the original improvisation remains unchanged. Both require actual KLAAT files and controlled comparisons.

SPECULATION: very short bursts of reconstruction distortion might change the felt 'grit' or apparent impact of a KLAAT passage. This is not established, and listeners might not hear any difference.

UNSUPPORTED: that KLAAT recordings already contain inter-sample overs; that overs cause trance or autonomic activation; that the C# drone has a unique susceptibility; that every dBTP reading above zero is audible; or that true-peak measurements reveal Paul's intentional expressive force.

6. A FIDELITY-FIRST INVESTIGATION, NOT A COMPOSITION RULE When original audio is available, preserve a byte-for-byte untouched copy and note sample rate, bit depth or encoding, channels, app and export history. On a working copy, calculate sample peak in dBFS and true peak in dBTP using a documented meter, including its oversampling method. Repeat for the intended encoded/exported version. Keep the files at their native levels for measurement, and avoid normalizing one before comparing their reported peaks. If a discrepancy appears, inspect the relevant passage and compare the original and export at a comfortable matched listening level. Ideally also check whether a different decoder or resampler changes the result. The correct unit for an estimated digital reconstructed peak is dBTP; do not report it as dBA or acoustic SPL.

This analysis should be conducted on KLAAT as it was played. There is no reason to impose a slower beat, softer strike, score, phase structure, or 'scientifically optimized' gesture. If a delivery format requires headroom, an export-level adjustment or appropriate mastering decision is distinct from changing the art. Any adjustment should preserve the untouched original.

7. THE BIGGER METHODOLOGICAL LESSON A recording is evidence of a performance only through a capture and playback chain. A sharp Ankora event may be acoustically real, digitally altered, perceptually salient or some combination. To understand why listeners describe 'impact,' 'pressure,' 'movement' or 'trance,' we must avoid confusing file measurements with bodily or psychological outcomes. A true-peak meter measures none of those experiences. Listener ratings, audio analysis and any physiology belong in separate columns of a future KLAAT evidence record.

An especially useful negative finding would be that original and export true peaks differ but listeners cannot reliably tell them apart. That would narrow an engineering concern without invalidating the music. Likewise, a listener hearing a rougher attack would not alone identify true-peak overs as its cause; codec artifacts, loudness differences and expectation would need consideration.

8. OPEN QUESTIONS Does the phone's video audio path apply automatic compression before saving? Are peaks higher after the platform's codec transcode? Does true-peak excess cluster near Ankora onsets or also occur during sustained Amastacia passages? Are any differences audible when loudness is matched and order is blinded? Would the same finding recur across playback devices? These are empirical questions, not instructions to alter the improvisation.

OPTIONAL LOW-PRESSURE OBSERVATION If an original KLAAT recording and one exported copy are available, simply compare their sample-peak and true-peak readings with the same meter. Note any difference; no need to replay, retake or modify the performance. If the files are unavailable, nothing needs to be done.
