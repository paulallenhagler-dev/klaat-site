THE INSTRUMENT BIGGER THAN ITS BODY

Acoustic scale, imagined source size, and the difference between sounding enormous and moving the body

SESSION 109 / 164Body & PerformanceRESEARCH SESSION

GOVERNING PRINCIPLE Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.

RESEARCH STATUS Evidence synthesis from seven primary experimental studies, plus KLAAT's existing research corpus. No new recording, acoustic measurement, listener trial, physiological measurement, or neural observation was collected. Any statement about how KLAAT listeners might experience apparent size is an explicit hypothesis, not an observed result.

THE QUESTION

Amastacia is an agave didgeridoo, not a giant machine. Ankora is a hand-struck elk-hide frame drum, not a distant mountain being struck by a hammer. Yet it is entirely plausible for a sparse acoustic performance to suggest something much larger than the visible instruments: a hollow chamber, a vast resonator, an animal-scale voice, a heavy object, or an apparently immense field of sound. Where does that impression come from? And if a listener describes KLAAT as “huge,” what exactly has been measured?

The new question is not whether bass can be heard or felt, whether a room amplifies low frequencies, or whether an instrument radiates equally in all directions. Those matters have already been addressed. It is whether the ear uses pitch, the spacing of resonances, the envelope of an impact, and learned associations to infer the size and material of a source—and whether those inferences can diverge from the actual physical object. This is an especially apt question for an artistic world that feels ancient and physically grounded without depending on a literal historical ritual or an electronically constructed fantasy.

A vital distinction will govern the whole session: ACOUSTIC SOURCE DIMENSIONS, PERCEIVED SOURCE SIZE, APPARENT SOUND-FIELD EXTENT, AND BODILY VIBRATION ARE FOUR DIFFERENT THINGS. A listener can hear something as massive without being exposed to exceptional sound pressure, receiving measurable vibration through a chair, or accurately identifying the instrument that produced it.

1. THE EAR IS TRYING TO EXPLAIN AN EVENT

Listening is not a passive inspection of frequencies. Sound ordinarily comes from objects and actions. A struck surface rings; a hollow body filters a buzz; a rolling object produces repeated contacts; a voice carries clues about a vocal tract. Listeners learn statistical relationships between sounds and causes. Those relationships are useful but not infallible.

For Amastacia, at least three physically distinguishable features may contribute to such judgments. The repetition rate of the lip-driven excitation helps establish a pitch. The frequency-dependent filtering of the bore, mouth, and vocal tract shapes an envelope of prominent resonances. Breath noise, turbulence, attack articulation, and fluctuations add temporal texture. An unedited mixed recording cannot automatically assign every spectral feature to one physical cause; the instrument, performer, room, microphone and playback system all contribute.

For Ankora, the onset, spectral distribution, resonant modes, decay, and mechanical damping of the membrane/frame system may suggest the kind of thing that has been struck. But an acoustic event is a coupled system: hand, membrane, frame, room, and microphone. The listener may infer a larger or heavier struck object than is physically present, just as a plate can make a small ball sound larger. That is a perceptual inference, not an error in the listener's sincerity.

This differs from the common phrase “low notes sound big.” Pitch is only one cue. The central primary literature deliberately separates the repetition rate of a source from the scale of its spectral resonances. That separation makes the topic testable instead of merely metaphorical.

2. THE TWO FREQUENCY MAPS: PULSE RATE AND RESONANCE SCALE

Van Dinther and Patterson (2006) investigated acoustic-scale perception in musical instrument sounds [1]. They manipulated two dimensions of recorded, sustained instrumental notes. PULSE RATE (PR) refers to the repetition rate of excitation and strongly influences perceived pitch. RESONANCE SCALE (RS) concerns the positions of the resonant peaks in the spectral envelope, related to the physical scale of a resonating system. These are not interchangeable: a resonator can have a different spectral envelope while an excitation maintains the same nominal pitch.

Their first experiment measured how much a resonance-scale change listeners needed to discriminate. The just-noticeable differences varied markedly with instrument and condition: roughly 3–10% for the baritone-voice conditions, around 7% for French horn, around 12% for saxophone, and roughly 10–20% for cello in the reported configurations. These are psychophysical thresholds under carefully manipulated, comparative listening conditions, not thresholds for recognizing an actual didgeridoo, and not percentages of KLAAT listeners who would feel an instrument grow.

Their second experiment found that listeners could still identify instrumental families when pitch and resonance scale were moved outside familiar combinations. Across the study's 16-instrument identification matrix, the reported overall correct identification was about 68% across manipulated conditions, averaged over four listeners. This is useful but also a methodological warning: small psychophysical panels and resynthesized isolated notes do not directly represent a long, mixed, improvised KLAAT performance. The study establishes that the auditory system can use resonance scale as a meaningful perceptual dimension; it does not prove that Amastacia sounds like a physically larger instrument in any particular take.

A resonance-scale change is not identical to turning down the treble. Broad equalization can alter brightness without preserving the relative formant-like relationships required by the original scale manipulation. Nor is a larger apparent source necessarily a louder source. The experiment's conceptual contribution is to show that an acoustic object can carry separate information about its excitation rate and its resonating structure.

3. WHY THE VOICE LITERATURE MATTERS—AND WHERE IT STOPS

Smith and Patterson (2005) varied glottal-pulse rate and estimated vocal-tract length in resynthesized vowels, asking listeners to judge apparent speaker size and category [2]. The results showed a strong influence of vocal-tract-length cues on judged size; pulse rate and vocal-tract length jointly influenced judgments of age/sex categories within ordinary combinations, while implausible combinations could shift the weighting toward vocal-tract cues. In other words, a low voice is not simply the same perceptual object as a large resonator.

Pisanski and colleagues' later experiment (2014) sharpened the distinction [3]. Participants made above-chance relative body-size judgments from voiced speech, whispered speech, and three-formant sine-wave speech. The authors reported that low pitch could improve the use of size information even though pitch and actual height were unrelated within the male sample; a reported 70–80% accuracy figure applied to particular low-pitch conditions, not to a universal ability to infer body size. That is a striking example of a perceptual cue being useful in a task without being a straightforward anatomical ruler.

Why bring human voices into KLAAT? Amastacia is excited by the performer's lips, and the player's oral and vocal-tract configuration can shape its sound. Listeners may hear voice-like resonances in a drone even when no words are present. The resemblance provides a plausible path for size or agency imagery. But the physics is not identical: a didgeridoo's bore and the player's vocal tract form a coupled wind-instrument system; one cannot simply map speech formant frequencies to the length of the agave body or to a fictional speaker's stature. The 2014 speech results say nothing about KLAAT's C# being uniquely “large,” and nothing about trance.

A further subtlety: source-size judgments may be partly categorical. A listener might hear “large voice,” “large hollow tube,” “machine,” or “cavern,” all while assigning different actual physical dimensions to those categories. An unprompted metaphor and a forced numerical size estimate are not the same response. KLAAT should not collapse them.

4. THE STRUCK OBJECT THAT MISLEADS THE EAR

Grassi (2005) used a deceptively simple apparatus: balls dropped on plates of different sizes [4]. Participants were asked to estimate ball size without being told the numbers or materials of the balls and plates. Their size estimates were meaningfully related to the ball, but also shifted with the plate: the same kind of ball was judged larger when it landed on a larger plate. Most of the sound energy came from the plate, not the ball.

This matters because it reveals a genuine attribution problem. The listener hears the outcome of an interaction and assigns a property to one participating object. The acoustic event does not arrive with labels identifying which object contributed which resonance. The investigators also found that listeners generally struggled to identify materials or the number of plates in some conditions. Size estimation could be partly successful while causal understanding was incomplete.

Ankora creates a different interaction—hand on hide, membrane/frame modes, and room radiation—but the inference problem is analogous. A listener may call a strike “heavy” because of a lower spectral balance, a long decay, a conspicuous onset, or the room's response. That does not identify the actual force of Paul's hand, the drum's diameter, or the acceleration delivered to a listener's body. Session 98 asked how the two-sided membrane radiates. Session 109 asks what object the listener imagines when that radiated sound reaches the ear. These questions can have different answers.

The most important caution from Grassi is not that listeners are bad at hearing. It is that the sound of a collision belongs to a coupled event. In KLAAT, a room or playback speaker may become part of that event from the listener's standpoint. Acoustic source attribution must remain conditional unless the components have been independently measured.

5. SIZE CAN OVERRIDE MOTION CUES

Houben, Kohlrausch, and Hermes (2005) studied recorded wooden balls rolling over a surface [5]. They created 16 hybrid stimuli by recombining spectral information and temporal characteristics from four original recordings, then asked listeners to compare ball size and speed. For judgments of which ball was larger, listeners favored the sound with the spectral content of a larger ball. For speed judgments, the spectral content of a smaller ball exerted an influence too; temporal information was comparatively weak over the specific stimulus range tested.

The contradiction is productive. A naive model might assign temporal cues to speed and spectral cues to size, independently. The actual judgments crossed those categories. A spectral feature that suggests “small object” can also bias what seems fast. This is a reminder that perceptual descriptors such as heavy, fast, powerful, close, ancient, or enormous need not correspond one-to-one with a measured acoustic variable.

For KLAAT, Ankora's repeated attacks and Amastacia's continuous field can invite both source and motion impressions. A denser subdivision may sound like a faster event; a darker resonant balance may sound like a larger object; and their combination may change the perceived character of motion. None of these links is established for Paul's recording. More importantly, no inference here says he should make the drum darker, enlarge the instrument, change tempo, or shape a crescendo. The research is for describing what the authentic sound already suggests.

6. THE MATERIAL THE EAR THINKS IT HEARS

Giordano and McAdams (2006) asked listeners to identify materials of struck plates made of steel, glass, wood, and plexiglass while varying plate size [6]. Listeners were excellent at separating the broad steel/glass group from the wood/plexiglass group under the experimental conditions, but they frequently confused the materials within each broad group. The researchers found that damping-related measures explained broad categories, yet other descriptors such as signal duration could explain the results comparably well. Within-category judgments depended strongly on frequency and object size. A single “ringing time equals material” explanation failed.

This is directly relevant to Ankora's elk hide. A listener might describe a sound as wood, stone, bone, metal, leather, or something unrecognizable. Those words may communicate an aesthetic impression, not a correct material identification. The physical instrument can be authentic elk hide while the acoustic impression suggests a different material. There is no scientific requirement that the listener correctly guess the object in order for the sound to be musically meaningful.

It is also a caution for the archaeosonic visual language. The artwork may evoke excavated diagrams, minerals, and ancient objects. That context could affect which material words listeners choose, but none of the plate experiments tests KLAAT artwork or historical associations. Such framing should be documented separately from audio-only judgments, rather than used to retroactively declare an acoustic property “ancient.”

7. TIMBRE IS NOT JUST A SOURCE-LABEL GAME

Lakatos (2000) examined similarity judgments for pitched and percussive instruments, comparing expert musicians and nonmusicians [7]. Across stimulus sets, spectral centroid and rise time emerged as important perceptual dimensions. Musicians and nonmusicians did not significantly differ in their weighting of those dimensions in that study. Listeners also showed some grouping by underlying physical-acoustic source properties, especially for complex percussive timbres.

This result connects the source-size literature to everyday musical hearing. People do not have to know what a resonator is to hear differences in attack and brightness. Yet a bright sound is not always a small sound, a dark sound is not always a large sound, and spectral centroid is not a valid estimator of the size of Ankora. The acoustic dimensions are perceptual cues whose interpretation depends on learned source categories and context.

For Amastacia, a shift in upper harmonics may be heard as a changing voice, object, or space while the nominal drone remains stable. For Ankora, attack shape and decay may influence whether a strike is heard as sharp, hollow, woody, heavy, or distant. The same listener can describe these changes without inferring any literal physical size. A research questionnaire that asks only “How large did it sound?” may miss that richer organization.

8. THE FOUR SIZES KLAAT MUST NOT CONFUSE

SIZE ONE — PHYSICAL SOURCE SIZE. Measurable dimensions of the agave body, frame, hide, bore, and the actual physical configuration of the instruments. These are instrument facts, not listener impressions.

SIZE TWO — INFERRED SOURCE SIZE OR MATERIAL. What the listener thinks could have produced the sound: a large tube, a huge membrane, a hollow chamber, a creature, an unidentified object. This is a perceptual report and may be metaphorical or wrong about physical provenance.

SIZE THREE — APPARENT SOUND-FIELD EXTENT. How wide, enveloping, close, or spatially spread the sound seems. Room reflections, loudspeaker geometry, binaural cues, and recording choices can influence this. Apparent width is not identical to inferred instrument size, and this session does not supply an experimental effect size for KLAAT's spatial image.

SIZE FOUR — DELIVERED MECHANICAL OR ACOUSTIC DOSE. Sound pressure at the ear, exposure duration, and—where physically coupled—vibration at the listener interface. These require calibrated acoustic or mechanical measurements. The phrase “the drum felt enormous” cannot stand in for pascals, decibels, or meters per second squared.

One could add a fifth category: SYMBOLIC SCALE. The work's imagined antiquity, ritual gravity, landscape, or mythic weight. This belongs to KLAAT's art, and it can be deeply real as experience without becoming a claim about anatomy, neuroscience, or a historical lineage. The source-size experiments are not tests of symbolism.

9. WHAT IS ACTUALLY ESTABLISHED

ESTABLISHED IN THE CITED NON-KLAAT EXPERIMENTS: People can discriminate resonance-scale changes in isolated instrumental sounds; pitch/excitation rate and resonance-scale cues both influence perceived size; impact and rolling sounds carry information about source properties; interactions between objects can mislead source attribution; material judgments often succeed at broad categories but fail within them; spectral and temporal cues contribute to timbral similarity.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: The exact discrimination thresholds depend on instrument family, pitch and scale combinations, task and small study samples. The approximately 3–20% resonance-scale thresholds reported by van Dinther and Patterson are not one universal threshold. The 68% instrument-family identification summary applies to their resynthesized stimuli and four-listener matrix. Grassi's plate effect is not a frame-drum effect size. The 70–80% low-pitch size-judgment finding concerns specific speech conditions, not KLAAT.

PLAUSIBLE AND TESTABLE FOR KLAAT: Some listeners may describe Amastacia's sustained harmonic field as a larger-than-visible source; Ankora's attacks may imply weight or material not literally present; room and playback context may modify these judgments; apparent source scale may be correlated with absorption or bodily energy for some listeners but not others. No such correlation has been measured.

SPECULATION: The combination of a sustained, human-breathed resonance and a hand-struck hide might encourage a sense of one immense, partly animate object. The project's archaeosonic imagery might amplify that interpretation. This is an artistic hypothesis, not a demonstrated cognitive mechanism for KLAAT.

UNSUPPORTED: “The low C# makes the listener feel a giant body”; “a huge-sounding drone causes a huge neurological response”; “spectral darkness proves a larger instrument”; “elk hide can be recognized from any recording”; “apparent mass equals measured vibration”; “a perceived ancient source proves inherited ceremonial practice”; or “KLAAT has been shown to create trance by source-size illusion.” None follows.

10. A MEASUREMENT CHAIN FOR A SOUND THAT SEEMS HUGE

An authentic, unedited performance produces an acoustic field. The room and recording/playback chain transform that field before it reaches the ear. The listener hears a spectrum, attacks, decays, and spatial cues, and may infer an object or material. The listener may separately report energy, emotion, absorption, imagery, or desire to move. If the claim concerns bodily vibration or autonomic change, separate physical or physiological measurement is necessary. Do not infer any of those later outcomes from the source-size rating alone.

For future analysis, keep a minimal record of: the exact unprocessed file and segment; listening level and playback system; whether the listener sees the instruments or artwork; free descriptions of imagined source and material; separate ratings of apparent size, spatial extent, bodily salience, energy, and absorption; and, if acoustic analysis is attempted, actual pitch, spectral-envelope/resonance peaks, onset characteristics, decay, and room/microphone limitations. If source-specific features cannot be reliably separated in a mixed recording, say so.

A correlation between low-frequency spectral energy and “large” ratings would not identify the cause. Pitch, loudness, reverberation, prior familiarity, imagery, and instrument recognition may covary. Nor would the absence of a correlation show that the sound lacks artistic scale. A good experiment preserves the possibility that listeners use different metaphors for the same experience.

The open question is whether KLAAT's distinctive sense of scale is predominantly a low-pitch impression, a resonant-envelope cue, a drum-impact inference, an imagined room, a symbolic association, or some combination. The current literature cannot choose among these possibilities for KLAAT. It is also possible that many listeners hear no unusual size at all. That negative outcome must remain acceptable.

12. ONE OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — THE OBJECT YOU HEARD

If an existing, unedited KLAAT excerpt is available, play it once for a few consenting listeners at a comfortable, fixed level, without showing them the instruments or artwork beforehand. First ask only: “What did the sound seem to come from, if anything?” Let them answer freely, including “I don't know” or “just music.” After that, ask separately whether anything seemed large or small, near or distant, physically felt or merely heard, energizing or absorbing. Do not turn their metaphors into measurements, and do not announce a “giant instrument” hypothesis in advance. If they later see Amastacia and Ankora, their changed descriptions are interesting context effects, not an independent second blind trial. Keep the original performance untouched.

This observation is optional. It is not a request to play darker, strike harder, enlarge an instrument, or compose a prescribed ritual. It simply asks what the listener's mind makes of the sound Paul already creates.

THE RETURN

The physical world of KLAAT is small enough to be carried by one performer: breath, agave, hide, hands, and a room. The world a listener hears may be much larger. The research does not authorize us to claim that this enlargement happens, or that it causes trance. It gives us a precise way to ask whether the listener is hearing an object, imagining an object, feeling vibration, or entering an aesthetic world—and to respect those answers as different forms of evidence.

A sound can suggest an enormous presence without pretending to be an enormous machine. KLAAT does not need a scientific score to make that possible. It needs truthful listening, good capture, and room for what emerges.
