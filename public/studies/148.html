THE CLOCKS THAT INVENTED A RHYTHMIC SHIFT

Independent recorder drift, false alignment, and the difference between performed time and measured time

SESSION 148 / 164Pulse & RhythmRESEARCH SESSION

RESEARCH QUESTION When the same spontaneous KLAAT improvisation is captured on a phone and an independent audio recorder, could the two files slowly fall out of alignment even though Paul's hands, breath, Amastacia drone and Ankora strikes have not changed? And could an analyst then mistake an artifact of recording clocks for a changing relationship between pulse and drone?

WHY THIS MATTERS NOW KLAAT is beginning to cross from interpretive research into the possibility of examining real performances. Paul already has short phone recordings and may eventually record simultaneously with an external microphone, second phone or dedicated recorder. Comparing two captures is attractive: one might emphasize Ankora's attack, another Amastacia's harmonics; one might reveal room sound, another close-up articulation. But comparison requires a common timebase. Two devices can record the same five-minute event yet disagree about how many samples represent those five minutes. A single strike aligned near the beginning does not guarantee that a strike near the end remains aligned.

This is not an argument for click tracks, metronomes or regulated improvisation. It is a demand that the instruments, not the measurement apparatus, receive credit for the timing they actually produced.

WHAT A DIGITAL RECORDER CALLS TIME A digital recording is a sequence of sample values associated with a nominal sample rate, commonly 44,100 or 48,000 samples per second. A rate printed in a file header is a declared rate, not an independent measurement of the hardware oscillator. Two recorders both labeled 48 kHz need not have exactly identical physical clocks. Small fractional differences accumulate. A device that records slightly more samples per real-world second than its partner can yield a file that appears longer or shorter when each is played back under the same nominal-rate assumption.

For a constant relative rate difference epsilon measured in parts per million (ppm), accumulated timing discrepancy is approximately:

drift in seconds = elapsed seconds × epsilon / 1,000,000.

At an illustrative 50 ppm relative difference, five minutes gives 300 × 50 / 1,000,000 = 0.015 seconds: 15 milliseconds. Twenty minutes gives 60 milliseconds. These are arithmetic examples, not specifications for Paul's phone or any recorder he owns. Actual drift could be smaller, larger, irregular or effectively removed by a synchronized system. The sign of the error depends on which clock runs faster.

Fifteen milliseconds is not automatically perceptually enormous. It is, however, enough to matter in some transient-alignment and phase-comparison tasks. At a 200 Hz sinusoidal component, one cycle lasts 5 ms; a 15 ms offset spans three cycles. This example illustrates phase ambiguity rather than proving a listener would hear a three-cycle error or that KLAAT has a measured 200 Hz feature. At much lower frequencies, the same delay represents a smaller fraction of a cycle. Phase also depends on microphone distance, reflections, filtering and processing; timebase mismatch is only one contributor.

THE FALSE MUSICAL STORY Imagine two hypothetical files of one uninterrupted KLAAT take. In the first minute, an Ankora strike appears exactly beside a visible change in the drone's amplitude envelope. Four minutes later, the same pair of recordings seems to show the drum attack lagging that envelope by 12 ms. An analyst might describe a progressive loosening of breath–drum coordination, an emerging counter-rhythm, or a subtle tension between the drone and pulse. But if the apparent shift increases approximately linearly with elapsed time and is present across many unrelated sound events, the more economical explanation may be recorder clock drift.

A crucial distinction: genuine expressive timing is local variation within the performance. Clock drift is a changing mapping between the two files' time axes. The latter can be present even when every actual event is unchanged. Conversely, correcting clock drift must not erase genuine performance variation within either original recording.

ESTABLISHED ENGINEERING EVIDENCE Sampling theory requires an accurately interpreted sample interval to reconstruct or compare timed signals. Independent oscillators can have different frequencies; their relative error accumulates as a timing offset. Synchronizing professional digital-audio devices through a shared clock or suitable synchronization architecture addresses this class of problem. These are established properties of sampled systems, not discoveries specific to KLAAT.

The simple ppm equation assumes a stable rate difference. Real devices may show temperature dependence, drift changes, dropped samples, resampling, pauses, or timestamp discontinuities. Video may use variable frame rate; audio inside video containers may be timestamped or resampled differently from a stand-alone WAV. A '48 kHz' label alone is not sufficient evidence of exact temporal agreement.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT EVIDENCE A listener may notice misalignment when two recordings are mixed or compared in certain ways, especially with sharp attacks or comb-filtering from similar microphone signals. Whether an untrained listener notices a particular millisecond offset depends on signal, frequency content, listening setup, level, and task. KLAAT's low drone plus frame-drum attacks make cross-device comparisons analytically interesting, but no perceptual threshold can be assigned to this specific music without listening tests. Likewise, no amount of file drift demonstrates that Paul's physiological timing or a listener's nervous system has changed.

HOW TO TELL DRIFT FROM OTHER PROBLEMS A fixed offset stays roughly constant from beginning to end. It may come from microphone distance, buffering, processing latency or a late recording start. A gradual increase in offset suggests a rate mismatch, although changing processing delay can mimic it. A sudden jump suggests a dropped chunk, edit, pause, discontinuity or timestamp issue. A repeating pattern of offsets may indicate periodic resampling or a flawed matching algorithm. None should be labeled an artistic timing change without checking the underlying files.

The most reliable anchors are distinct acoustic events audible in both recordings: isolated Ankora attacks, abrupt breath articulations or other unique transients. A long nearly periodic drone is a poor sole anchor because a cross-correlation algorithm can match the wrong cycle and report a convincing but incorrect delay. Select at least two anchors widely separated in time, preferably more. Compare their relative offsets. Use a linear fit only if the observed trend supports it; report residual deviations and uncertainty. Preserve raw samples and original timestamps. Any time-stretched or resampled alignment file is a derivative, never the archival master.

ACOUSTIC PROPAGATION IS NOT CLOCK DRIFT Sound takes time to travel through air. Two microphones at different distances from Ankora may record the same strike at different moments. At roughly 343 m/s, a one-meter path difference corresponds to approximately 2.9 ms. This is a rough room-temperature calculation, not a measured KLAAT room condition. If microphone positions remain fixed, this propagation delay is largely a fixed offset, not a steadily growing difference. Reverberation and source directivity can make the best waveform anchor more ambiguous than this simple geometry suggests.

A phone can also apply automatic gain control, noise suppression or codec delay. Such processing may alter the shape of a transient so that peak-to-peak comparison gives the wrong onset. Where possible, compare onset windows rather than a single largest sample; document the algorithm. Do not infer sample-clock accuracy from a visually aligned waveform alone.

FOUR EVIDENCE LEVELS Acoustically measured: two files show a relative offset that changes from, for example, 2 ms to 17 ms over five minutes. That is a file-level observation, conditional on accurate onset matching.

Subjectively reported: Paul or a listener describes a passage as tightening, loosening or gaining momentum. Such reports concern perceived performance, not necessarily the recording clocks.

Neural/physiological: nothing in an audio timestamp comparison measures brain entrainment, heart rate, respiratory coupling or trance.

Causal hypothesis: the increasing cross-file offset is caused by independent recorder clocks. Test by using multiple landmarks, checking container timestamps, comparing shared-clock recordings where possible and looking for a stable linear trend. Other causes remain plausible until excluded.

AN IMPORTANT CONTRADICTION Sometimes two independent devices sound perfectly synchronized during ordinary playback. That does not prove their clocks match closely enough for sample-level analysis. Equally, an offset observed in an editing program does not mean a listener heard mistimed music: each device may reproduce its own recording coherently when played alone. The artifact becomes consequential when comparing or combining independently clocked streams, not automatically during every listening experience.

THE KLAAT-SPECIFIC PAYOFF The goal is to protect the very qualities KLAAT values: breath-led spontaneity, imperfect living interval, and the evolving physical relationship between drone and drum. A poorly aligned pair of recordings can invent a rhythmic development that never occurred, or obscure a subtle real one. Rigorous timing analysis makes the improvisation more legible without asking Paul to make it more predictable.

The same principle extends to later listener studies. If a participant taps along while an experimenter records their taps on a second device, clock synchronization matters before claims are made about anticipation, lag or sensorimotor coupling. A timing discrepancy in the apparatus must not be rebranded as a nervous-system effect.

WHAT WE DO NOT KNOW We have not measured the sample-clock stability of Paul's phone, an external recorder, or any actual KLAAT video. We have not demonstrated cross-device drift in a KLAAT recording. We do not know whether his most important musical events can be reliably located across microphone perspectives. We have not measured listener timing, absorption, activation, trance or neurological responses. The existence of clock drift as an engineering possibility does not establish its presence in this archive.

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATION Established: sampled timebases, oscillator-rate mismatch, ppm drift arithmetic, and the need for synchronization in precise cross-device comparisons. Context dependent: magnitude and audibility of timing mismatch; how codecs, processing and variable-frame-rate containers affect particular files. OPTIONAL OBSERVATION — NO CHANGE TO THE MUSIC On an ordinary improvisation, if two recorders happen to be available, let both run without a click or prescribed structure. Later locate one distinctive Ankora strike near the beginning and another near the end in both original files. Note whether the relative time offset changed. If the strikes are ambiguous, mark the comparison inconclusive. Preserve both untouched files. The observation is about the clocks, not a test of Paul's rhythmic skill.

CLOSING KLAAT does not need to surrender its living time to a machine. It needs only to avoid confusing the machine's time with the musician's. Experience-led creation. Science-informed refinement.
