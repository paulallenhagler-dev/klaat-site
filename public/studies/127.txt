THE EAR IS FAST; THE SCENE IS SLOW
A contradiction in binaural timing, changing sound-source relationships, and the limits of a single auditory clock

Guiding principle: EXPERIENCE-LED CREATION. SCIENCE-INFORMED REFINEMENT.


RESEARCH STATUS AND CLAIM BOUNDARY
This is a synthesis of published human psychoacoustics, auditory modeling, and related experimental work. No KLAAT waveform, room, stereo recording, binaural microphone capture, hearing response, physiological recording, or listener report was analyzed for this study. The published numerical results below belong to external experiments, not to KLAAT. No result establishes that Amastacia or Ankora induces trance, neural entrainment, a specific bodily response, or an altered state. KLAAT's acoustic identity and Paul's spontaneous playing are the starting points, not variables that must be composed to fit a theory.


THE QUESTION


Ankora makes a short, distinct attack inside the much longer acoustic world of Amastacia. To Paul, both sounds may be inseparable parts of one improvised physical act. A listener may nevertheless hear two objects: an ongoing drone and a striking membrane. In a live room those objects can occupy slightly different positions, even if the performer is seated in one place. A recording can preserve or erase some of those differences.


How quickly can the two ears detect a change in the spatial information associated with an acoustic event? And how quickly can the listener reorganize an existing scene when the apparent relationship among sources changes? Those sound like the same question. Experimental research says they are not.


One family of studies finds that human listeners can extract meaningful binaural information from events only a few milliseconds long. Another finds that spatial advantages in masked listening diminish when interaural cues change rapidly, sometimes requiring a model that integrates or stabilizes information over roughly one or two tenths of a second. A 2023 analysis argues that these findings are compatible if rapid encoding is distinguished from slower reconfiguration of an internal representation of the masker.


For KLAAT, this matters because a brief Ankora attack can be registered rapidly without the entire relationship between drone and pulse being perceptually reorganized at the same speed. The result is not a recommendation to slow down, separate the instruments, or invent a spatial composition. It is a better account of what a listener might be processing when authentic KLAAT changes.

2. WHAT THE EARS RECEIVE IS NOT A READY-MADE MAP


Two ears receive different versions of the same physical scene. Differences in arrival time, level, spectral filtering, and coherence can help listeners locate or separate sources. The standard abbreviations are ITD (interaural time difference), ILD (interaural level difference), and IPD (interaural phase difference). IPD and ITD are related, but they are not interchangeable for a complex sound whose phase changes differently across frequency. In a real room, reflections add further copies with different delays and spectra.


A separate question is MASKING. When a target event is difficult to detect because another sound overlaps with it, its detection threshold may rise. Spatial differences can sometimes reduce that masking, either because one ear has a better target-to-masker ratio or because the auditory system uses information from both ears to distinguish the signals. A sound may be audible yet difficult to attribute to a source; detection and source identification are separate outcomes.


KLAAT is a particularly useful conceptual case because its two principal sound-making actions are unlike one another. Amastacia is sustained and spectrally variable: nominal C# does not mean an unchanging single-frequency sine wave. Ankora produces impacts with attack, decay, resonant body, and sometimes overlapping strikes. Neither instrument is a laboratory tone or stationary noise. The listener's auditory system encounters multiple time scales even before rhythm, meaning, and attention enter the picture.


We should distinguish at least five events: a physical strike; the resulting acoustic pressure at each ear; a detectable onset; attribution of that onset to Ankora rather than to breath/noise/room sound; and a conscious impression of a changing KLAAT texture. These may happen close together, but measuring one does not prove the others.


3. THE FAST RESULT: SPATIAL INFORMATION IN ONLY A FEW MILLISECONDS


Reed, Dietz, Josupeit, and van de Par (2016) tested human lateralization of broadband noise that alternated rapidly between different interaural time differences. They reported accurate lateralization from brief noise tokens only 3–7 milliseconds long [1]. That is a remarkable demonstration of sensitivity to fleeting spatial information. It does NOT say that an entire sound scene is rebuilt in 3–7 ms, or that a listener can recognize a new musical source in that interval.


The same study supplies a useful caution about KLAAT's envelope. For wideband stimuli, moving the brief target relative to an imposed amplitude-modulation phase did not change absolute lateralization or detection in the way one might simply predict. For narrowband stimuli, detection was better when the target occurred on the rising part of the amplitude envelope. The auditory readout of rapid binaural cues depended on bandwidth and envelope context. An Ankora transient is broadband to varying degrees; a filtered phone recording may be less so. The laboratory result therefore supports a mechanism worth considering, not a fixed prediction for a drum strike.


Siveke, Ewert, Grothe, and Wiegrebe (2008) combined human psychophysics with physiological evidence in a different rapid-binaural paradigm, using stimuli designed to carry changing interaural timing information [2]. They found faster human binaural performance than older 'sluggish' characterizations would suggest. Importantly, their physiological measurements were not recordings from people listening to KLAAT, and physiological sensitivity to a stimulus feature is not identical to reported musical absorption. The value of the work is to rule out a crude statement such as 'the two ears cannot process rapidly changing timing cues.'


4. THE SLOW RESULT: A MOVING MASKER DOES NOT STAY USEFULLY SEPARATED


Culling and Mansell (2013) tested speech understanding in headphone-simulated spatial scenes with changing interfering noise [3]. A single lateral noise that was square-wave modulated at 10 Hz reduced spatial release from masking under one condition. With two noises at symmetrical positions, alternating their modulation could improve release: the listener could exploit different favorable moments. In a second experiment the researchers varied modulation rates from 1 to 20 Hz and manipulated head-related impulse responses to isolate interaural timing and level cues. Spatial release declined steeply as the changes became faster.


The result is not 'fast changes are always bad.' Even within one paper, alternating maskers could help in one configuration while fast switching limited performance in another. The task was speech intelligibility, with engineered noise and simulated spatial positions. KLAAT's drone and frame drum are not words masked by noises. The durable insight is that the listener's ability to exploit a rapidly changing relationship between target and background is context-dependent.


Hauth and Brand (2018) performed a particularly clean experiment with 10 normal-hearing adults, ages 23–26, using German sentence recognition in speech-shaped noise over headphones [4]. The speech was presented centrally. The interfering noise's interaural phase difference was sinusoidally modulated at frequencies from 0.25 to 64 Hz, deliberately avoiding a simple changing better-ear level advantage. At 0 Hz, the median speech reception threshold was approximately −9.2 dB signal-to-noise ratio. At the slowest modulation, 0.25 Hz, it improved to approximately −12.3 dB. As the interaural phase changed faster, performance deteriorated; above about 4 Hz, the median threshold plateaued near −10.3 dB. The reported benefit relative to the diotic condition was about 2–3 dB at the slowest rate, falling to roughly 1–1.5 dB at the faster rates.


Those are measurements of the signal-to-noise ratio needed to understand 50% of test words, not perceived loudness increases, spatial widths, or musical pleasure. A 3-dB threshold advantage is not a 3-dB physical increase in Amastacia or Ankora. The slow 0.25-Hz condition also had high variability because its four-second modulation cycle could be sampled differently across a roughly two-second sentence depending on the randomly chosen initial phase. The sample was small and experienced in psychoacoustic testing. These qualifications matter more than a seductive single number.


5. WHY THE 200-MILLISECOND NUMBER IS EASY TO MISUSE


Hauth and Brand modeled their results with a short-term binaural speech-intelligibility framework. Their ordinary short-time analysis operated in 23.2-ms frames. That short analysis alone could not reproduce the observed decline in masking release as interaural phase changes became faster. They therefore introduced an additional window for stabilizing estimates of the masker-related binaural parameters; a window around 200 ms gave their best fit. Windows above roughly 100 ms also captured the broad decline, and fit differences above that were small relative to measurement variability [4].


It would be wrong to turn '200 ms' into a KLAAT prescription. It is not the time it takes the brain to hear an Ankora strike, not a biological metronome, not a preferred pulse interval, not a threshold for trance, and not proof that attacks closer than 200 ms become neurologically ineffective. It is a task- and model-dependent parameter describing one kind of scene updating during speech-in-noise recognition.


For comparison only, the provisional 137 BPM anchor corresponds to about 438 ms per beat, while an attack density of 280 events per minute corresponds to about 214 ms per event on average. The numerical proximity between 214 ms and a model's 200-ms window is NOT evidence of resonance, entrainment, or an optimal performance speed. One number describes an observed musical-event rate in a particular recording; the other is a fitted parameter in a very different psychoacoustic task. Treating the match as a discovered law would be precisely the kind of attractive but unsupported claim this research program is designed to avoid.


6. THE 2023 RESOLUTION: FAST ENCODING, SLOWER RECONFIGURATION


Eurich and Dietz (2023) explicitly revisited the apparent contradiction between fast binaural detection and sluggish performance when a masker changes [5]. They argued that a single, long binaural integration window cannot account for experiments demonstrating detection of rapid interaural oscillations. Their model instead allows fast encoding of interaural phase differences while updating the internal statistical representation of the masker more slowly. That two-process account could describe results from both the 'fast' and 'sluggish' experimental families.


This is a valuable conceptual advance, but its status must be reported accurately. The 2023 paper is a computational/model-based synthesis and comparison with experimental paradigms; its model fit does not constitute a direct measurement of a discrete 'scene-update neuron' in the human brain. Competing implementations may also explain portions of the data. The point is not that a universal two-clock brain mechanism has been proven. The point is that fast feature extraction and slower scene-reference updating are separable explanatory functions, and that the evidence does not support describing the entire binaural system with one sluggish speed.


Imagine hearing a very brief click from one side while a fan hums steadily elsewhere. Your ears can register the click's timing information quickly. That does not mean your brain must abandon its ongoing representation of the fan and immediately reconstruct the entire auditory scene. An internal estimate of the background may persist while the new event is evaluated against it. This analogy clarifies the hypothesis; it is not a KLAAT experiment.


7. WHAT THIS MIGHT EXPLAIN ABOUT AUTHENTIC AMASTACIA AND ANKORA


KLAAT contains a durable background and intermittent events, but the distinction is not absolute. Amastacia's drone can become rough, pulsed, breathy, vowel-like, or noisy. Ankora can strike once, accelerate, cluster, or decay into near silence. In a continuous improvisation, the listener may maintain an impression of the ongoing drone while rapidly registering a drum attack that momentarily dominates the sound field.


A plausible interpretation is that a relatively stable acoustic reference can help the listener recognize deviations without the whole scene needing to be reconstructed after each strike. That is a TESTABLE idea, not an established effect of KLAAT. If the source relationships remain physically stationary, a model of rapidly changing interaural phase may not be the primary mechanism at all; spectral separation, onset salience, temporal masking, source familiarity, and attention could matter more. A frame-drum impact does not automatically shift the didgeridoo's spatial position, and a faster pulse does not necessarily imply a faster change in binaural cues.


There is also an important asymmetry between performance and replay. Paul's live listening includes bone and tissue conduction, near-field instrument geometry, room reflections, body movement, and knowledge of his own actions. A single phone microphone may collapse those relationships into mono or processed stereo. A pair of speakers can reproduce some spatial cues while introducing its own cross-talk and room reflections. Headphones can provide channel differences but cannot recreate an authentic head-related field unless the capture/rendering supports it. These distinctions extend Sessions 119 and 121 rather than replacing them.


The aesthetic implication is modest and significant: KLAAT's apparent continuity and its interruptions can be perceived together without being reducible to one clock. That helps explain why 'a drone plus a drum' is a poor summary of the perceptual possibilities. It does not demonstrate a trance mechanism or dictate what Paul should play next.


8. DETECTION, SEGREGATION, AND ACTIVATION MUST NOT BE COLLAPSED


A person can detect an Ankora strike without accurately judging its position. They can localize it without assigning it to the correct instrument. They can identify the instrument without feeling energized. They can feel energized without being able to describe the acoustic source. They can enter a subjective state of absorption without any evidence of neural phase locking to the pulse. Each statement requires a different observation.


Acoustic measurement: microphone channels, source positions, level, spectrum, attack envelope, room response, and whether the recording contains actual left-right differences.


Perceptual measurement: target detection, event counting, perceived position, source attribution, masking thresholds, and confidence. These require human judgments and appropriately controlled comparisons.


Subjective experience: activation, desire to move, bodily sensation, absorption, irritation, and remembered texture. These require listener reports; they do not emerge from an FFT or interaural cross-correlation alone.


Physiology: heart rate, breathing, skin conductance, EEG, and movement are separate endpoints requiring calibrated instruments, a design that addresses confounds, and careful interpretation. Nothing in the cited binaural experiments measures KLAAT-specific physiology or establishes a therapeutic effect.


The central research hazard is to observe a rapid onset, attach a '200-ms auditory clock' to it, and leap from there to an explanation of trance. Every step in that chain would need its own evidence.


9. THREE COMPETING EXPLANATIONS FOR A MORE DISTINCT ANKORA ATTACK


Suppose an actual listener reports that the drum suddenly 'steps out of' the drone in one excerpt. At least three explanations are possible. First, the acoustic mixture may have changed: Ankora was struck harder, the drum moved, Amastacia's harmonics shifted, or a phone compressor altered the balance. Second, the mixture may be acoustically similar but the listener's attention or source grouping may have changed. Third, a different reproduction path may have made spatial or spectral cues more usable. These are not mutually exclusive.


The new two-timescale account adds a fourth, narrower hypothesis: rapid acoustic evidence might be registered against a background representation that changes more slowly. To distinguish it, one would need an experiment manipulating temporal changes in spatial cues while holding other variables appropriately constant. Simply comparing an early and late KLAAT excerpt cannot identify that mechanism because tempo, density, timbre, level, expectation, and recording artifacts can all change at once.


Even a listener who prefers the 'more separated' version has not demonstrated a superior trance state. Preference, event intelligibility, and absorption can diverge. KLAAT's energizing everyday-listening identity makes this especially important: what feels exciting while walking, what feels absorbing in a quiet room, and what is easy to parse on a phone speaker need not be identical.


10. THE EVIDENCE LADDER


ESTABLISHED IN CONTROLLED NON-KLAAT EXPERIMENTS: Human listeners can use very brief interaural information in some tasks, including lateralization of 3–7-ms noise tokens [1]. Dynamic spatial-masker configurations influence speech recognition; in one 10-person study, slow IPD modulation produced more spatial benefit than faster modulation, with specific thresholds reported above [3,4].


MODEL-SUPPORTED BUT CONTEXT-DEPENDENT: Separating rapid binaural encoding from slower updating of masker statistics reconciles different experimental paradigms in a 2023 model [5]. The model's internal components are not direct observations of a universal neural architecture, and 200 ms is not a transferable constant for KLAAT.


PLAUSIBLE, TESTABLE KLAAT HYPOTHESIS: A listener may register brief Ankora transients rapidly while maintaining a more stable impression of Amastacia as an ongoing background. Natural spatial differences could contribute if actually present and reproduced. Spectral/onset mechanisms and attention are competing explanations.


SPECULATION: This combination of stability and rapid change might contribute to the feeling of a continuous field animated by discrete events, or to certain listeners' subjective absorption. The direction and size of any effect are unknown.


UNSUPPORTED: KLAAT causes a measured binaural scene-reset; a 200-ms 'brain clock' dictates an optimal pulse; 137 BPM or 280 attacks/min creates a spatial-neural resonance; faster attacks necessarily overwhelm the auditory system; stereo width guarantees stronger activation; any listener has entered trance, experienced physiological entrainment, or received therapeutic benefit from this mechanism.


11. WHAT WOULD COUNT AS REAL PROGRESS


The next serious evidence would not be another diagram of the ear. It would be source provenance and actual listener reports linked to authentic KLAAT sound. Is a recording truly mono or stereo? Are left-right differences stable, natural, and reproducible? Does an Ankora event remain easy to identify when the didgeridoo changes timbre? Do listeners describe two sources or one integrated mass? Does their account change with ordinary speaker versus headphone listening, without conflating level and device quality? If a difference appears, does it survive a repeat on another day or with another listener?


Only after those basics are documented would a controlled spatial-cue manipulation be justified. Such a manipulation would be an analytical copy of an existing improvisation, not a rule for future playing. It should preserve an unedited master and label any derivative clearly. A negative result would be useful: it might show that KLAAT's perceived two-source identity is dominated by attack, timbre, or rhythm rather than binaural dynamics.


12. CONCLUSION — TWO SPEEDS, NOT TWO COMMANDMENTS


A good explanation of KLAAT has to accommodate the listener who hears a single physical world and the listener who notices two independent sound-makers moving within it. The binaural literature gives a precise warning against treating auditory perception as a single instantaneous operation. The ears can extract fleeting timing information while the listener's representation of an ongoing masker or scene changes on a different, often slower timescale. That is a defensible research insight. It is not a performance instruction.


The principle remains intact: Paul's improvisation supplies the phenomenon. Science can refine our descriptions, expose confounds, suggest better recording practice, and design honest tests. It does not get to decide where Ankora must strike, how long Amastacia must drone, or when a listener is supposed to feel transformed.


OPTIONAL KLAAT OBSERVATION — KRX-127: THE BACKGROUND THAT SURVIVES THE STRIKE


On one existing, unedited 30–60-second KLAAT recording, listen once at a comfortable level without looking at a waveform. Afterward, note in a single sentence whether Amastacia felt like one continuing background through Ankora's attacks, or whether particular attacks made the whole sonic scene seem to change. If the recording is known to be mono, say so; do not infer spatial processing from the impression. This is a private phenomenological observation, not a neural test, not a demand to edit or replay multiple versions, and not a reason to change the next improvisation.
