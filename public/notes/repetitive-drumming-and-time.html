Why Repetitive Drumming Can Change How Time Feels

The clock stays fixed. Listening does not.

KLAAT RESEARCH  ·  OCTOBER 19, 2026  ·  A PUBLIC RESEARCH NOTE

One pulse, several clocks

Strike a frame drum at a steady rate and something curious happens. The performer supplies a sequence of events, but the listener supplies an organization. Some people follow every strike; others group the same pattern into a slower beat, or hear accents as the beginning of a larger phrase. The physical event rate and the perceived clock are not identical.

In KLAAT, Ankora is the pulse source. An important reference recording was played at approximately 137 beats per minute, but that figure is a description of one performance—not a prescribed tempo or a proven biological frequency. Different listeners may find different levels of the same rhythm compelling.

Elapsed time versus experienced time

Researchers distinguish several questions that ordinary conversation folds into “time felt strange.” How long did the event last? How long did someone estimate that it lasted? Did it feel fast or slow while it was happening? How many distinct sections can they remember afterward?

Those answers need not agree. Attention directed toward the passage of time differs from attention absorbed in a musical environment. A listener might spend fifteen minutes following the sound without checking the clock, then remember many distinct moments when asked afterward. That possibility is intriguing, but it is not the same as demonstrating a trance state.

Repetition builds a place to stand

A stable rhythmic pattern gives the auditory system something to expect. Once that expectation develops, tiny departures can become conspicuous: an accent shifts, a subdivision thickens, a strike does not arrive. In long-form music, those moments may function as landmarks, separating the experience into sections that no one planned in advance.

KLAAT calls these possibilities listener-defined chapters. An improvised performance can feel structured to its audience even when it was not composed around a fixed map. The research question is whether independent listeners identify similar transitions, rather than assuming they all heard the performer's imagined structure.

What remains to be tested

The research behind KLAAT supports a modest interpretation: temporal attention, meter, and remembered event structure can influence duration judgments in particular circumstances. It does not establish that any special drum tempo reliably alters consciousness.

A stronger KLAAT study would use authentic recordings, ask people about time before revealing the research questions, compare their estimates with the actual duration, and note where they independently place boundaries. That kind of evidence could tell us whether the experience travels beyond the person performing it.
