PROLOGUE / THE SECOND INTERVAL

The first interval exists between two actions: a sound that remains and a sound that interrupts.

But there is another interval. It exists between what the performer does and what another person experiences. Between the physical event and the remembered event. Between a recorded sound and the same recording heard again.

The first Atlas considered the architecture of an acoustic ritual. This second inquiry follows that architecture beyond the instruments. A performance does not possess a single experience. It creates the conditions from which different experiences may emerge.

The sound has a source. Its meaning has no single location.

BOOK II / PART I

THE BODY THAT MAKES

KLAAT / BOOK II / LEAF I

THE DIVIDED BODY

Two acoustic actions can be sustained by one body without becoming one physical task.

The Drone depends upon a continuous negotiation among airflow, pressure, embouchure, and the changing shape of the vocal tract. The Pulse depends upon another sequence: movement, impact, release, and preparation for the next contact. These systems share posture, muscular support, attention, and the limited resources of a living performer.

A respiratory transition may alter the circumstances in which the hand moves. A demanding passage of percussion can change the body that sustains the Drone. These relationships need not be consciously arranged to become part of the performance. Coordination can develop until formerly separate movements become available as one fluent action, yet the mechanisms remain capable of interfering with each other.

What is heard as one music is produced by several systems with different forms of resistance. The unity of KLAAT is not the erasure of those differences. It is their continued negotiation.

The body is not merely the operator of two instruments. It is the interval in which they learn to coexist.

THE SOUND HEARD FROM WITHIN

The performer occupies an acoustic position that no audience member can reproduce exactly.

Airborne sound enters the ears, but the performer also receives contact vibration, pressure through the mouth, resistance in the hand, and knowledge of a gesture before it becomes audible. The body experiences the work while producing it. The listener receives another sensory arrangement, made from sound arriving at a particular position in a particular room.

A drum strike may feel decisive beneath the hand and less prominent several meters away. The Drone may remain a continuous physical task for its maker while reaching the listener as a moving distribution of harmonics. The performer remembers a difficult transition the listener did not detect. The listener may hear a clarity or balance unavailable to the person standing inside the work.

These are not competing accounts of an identical sensation. They are differently situated encounters with the same event. Even the performer's absorbed state cannot establish the listener's state.

An acoustic ritual can join people in a room without making their experiences interchangeable.

THE LABOR THAT DISAPPEARS

A practiced movement may appear effortless because its coordination has become familiar. Breath continues. The hand returns. The performance develops without every action needing an explicit verbal instruction.

Fluency does not eliminate labor. The body continues to regulate pressure, execute strikes, redistribute effort, and respond to small failures. An action may become easier to organize without becoming physically costless. In a long improvisation, felt effort, measurable loss of capacity, audible change, and physical discomfort need not occur at the same moment.

A passage can feel demanding while remaining technically stable. The performer can feel absorbed while muscular or respiratory demands accumulate. Conversely, a reduction in accuracy may appear before fatigue is consciously acknowledged.

KLAAT's duration is therefore not empty time filled with repeated sound. It is an extended negotiation between available technique, physical limits, and the desire to remain present inside the work.

The labor may become less visible. It does not cease to exist.

THE UNINTENDED EVENT

A breath transition roughens. A strike arrives with unfamiliar weight. The body produces something other than what was intended.

In a scored composition, an error may be recognized by comparison with a fixed sequence. In spontaneous performance, that reference is less secure. The performer can identify a departure from immediate intention; the recording can preserve an acoustic difference; the listener can report that a sound stood out. None of these observations settles the others.

A disturbance may remain a disturbance. It may be corrected before becoming consequential. Or it may suggest another texture, a new interval, a different density of sound. There is no reason to glorify every accident as art, nor to classify every rough edge as a failure.

Improvisation leaves the event undecided long enough for continuation to give it meaning.

What did the body mean to do? What did the instrument actually sound? What happened next? These are three different questions.

THE ART OF CONTINUATION

An unexpected sound arrives. The next action determines whether it becomes a passing interruption, a corrected gesture, or the beginning of another relationship.

The performer may return to the earlier pulse. Repetition may give the disturbance a recognizable shape. The sound may be abandoned, leaving the original architecture intact. Or the music may follow the new possibility away from a direction that had seemed certain only moments before.

These responses are not identical. Correction restores an intention. Repetition can convert an incident into a pattern. Departure allows a consequence to become material.

Yet continuation is never arbitrary. Every choice is made inside a field already conditioned by the Drone, the returning strike, the room, and the body's remaining capacity. The present has limits established by what preceded it.

KLAAT does not need to know its destination before arriving there. It must only remain answerable to the event taking place.

THE MIND THAT RECEIVES

KLAAT / BOOK II / LEAF VI

ATTENTION AS AN INSTRUMENT

Two sounds may occupy one acoustic field while attention gives them different prominence.

A listener follows the drum until the Pulse seems to organize everything around it. Attention turns toward the continuous Drone. What had served as a background opens into a changing arrangement of harmonics, pressure, and articulation. No instrument has changed its action, but the perceptual foreground has moved.

This is one consequence of working with sparse means. There may be only two sources, yet multiple ways to organize the relationship between them. A listener can hear independent events, an apparent composite rhythm, or a single broad texture whose interior becomes visible only through sustained attention.

Attention is not the hand that struck the drum, nor the breath that sustained the tone. It does not rewrite the acoustic signal. It selects and shapes which relationships become available in the encounter.

KLAAT has no single permanent perceptual foreground. The apparent arrangement can move inside the listener while the sound remains where it is.

THE SOUND THAT DID NOT CHANGE

The same recording is played twice.

On the first hearing, its repetitions are unfamiliar. The listener may not know which variations matter, when an accent might return, or whether a long passage contains an identifiable direction. On a later hearing, some uncertainty has diminished. A once-unexpected transition can be anticipated. A faint articulation may emerge from beneath what previously sounded undivided.

Nothing in the stored signal has changed.

Familiarity can make difficult sound more accessible. It can also remove surprise, reduce interest, or sharpen irritation. There is no universal movement from unfamiliarity into pleasure, nor from repeated exposure into absorption. The same listener may become more involved, less involved, or simply involved in another way.

A recording can be replayed precisely. Its original encounter cannot. Repetition reproduces the sound while altering the history of the person who hears it.

THE RHYTHM NO ONE PLAYED

An acoustic attack does not dictate a single internal rhythm.

The drum produces strikes. The Drone contributes fluctuations, articulations, and changes in spectral weight. Between their audible events, a listener may perceive subdivisions, groupings, and larger recurring shapes that neither instrument explicitly marks.

The recorded events can be counted. Their intervals can be measured. A modulation spectrum can describe fluctuations in acoustic energy. None of these quantities, by itself, identifies the beat or meter a person experiences.

One listener may organize a sequence around every strike; another may hear alternating accents as a slower recurrence. A change of attention can expose a new grouping even when the recording remains identical.

The performer gives time a series of physical events. The listening mind constructs a relationship among them. The perceived rhythm is real as an experience without requiring that every part of it be directly played.

STILLNESS UNDER PRESSURE

Stillness is often imagined as quiet, softness, and reduced motion.

But an absorbed state need not be built from gentle sound. A demanding repetitive field can occupy attention while leaving little space for competing thought. Its pressure may feel activating rather than soothing, and the intensity may be part of the reason a listener remains engaged.

Activation, absorption, enjoyment, movement, and relaxation are different dimensions. A person may feel energized but physically still. Another may move with the pulse without experiencing inward absorption. A third may find the same passage oppressive or exhausting. None of these responses can be inferred from the acoustic waveform alone.

KLAAT pursues a paradox: a condition of altered stillness reached through persistent force rather than escape from sensation. This is an artistic pursuit, not a demonstrated universal effect.

The pressure rises. What the listener does with it remains open.

THE LISTENER ARRIVES BEFORE THE SOUND

No one comes to listening without a history.

The room has a character before an instrument begins. A listener may have seen the diagrams, heard the word ritual, observed the performer preparing, or been told to expect trance. Such context may influence attention and anticipation before the first audible event.

A bodily response measured after the music begins need not have begun because of the music. Its initiating cause may be a preceding cue, an expectation, a memory, or a combination. A report after listening may also reflect the language used to introduce the work.

This does not make ritual context false. It means that hearing KLAAT cold and encountering it through a deliberate introduction are different conditions. Both may have artistic value; they cannot be treated as scientifically identical.

The listener does not arrive at the beginning of the recording. The listener arrives carrying expectations that are already in motion.

THE MANY LISTENING MINDS

A shared acoustic event does not guarantee a shared account of what occurred.

One listener follows the beat. Another hears the changing edge of the Drone. Another is drawn toward the abrasive friction between them. Someone else hears little beyond a difficult repetition. Preference, familiarity, attention, listening position, physical state, and expectation can alter the encounter.

An average response can conceal this variation. Even when a group reports a common tendency, individual listeners may reach it by different paths or not reach it at all.

An artistic work can be coherent without producing one predictable state in every person who encounters it. Its identity belongs to its practice and form; its reception belongs also to the listener.

The ritual establishes conditions. It does not own their outcome.

THE IDENTITY OF THE EVENT

KLAAT / BOOK II / LEAF XII

THE SIGNATURE BENEATH THE MATERIAL

An instrument carries an acoustic character made from dimensions, material, resistance, and resonance. Change the bore or membrane and the resulting sound cannot remain identical.

But an artistic identity may extend beyond the particular objects used to produce it. The performer returns to certain kinds of decisions: when to remain, when to disturb, how to allow a roughness to develop, when to release pressure, and how to respond when the intended sound is not the sound that arrives.

Those tendencies might survive a change of instruments. Whether unfamiliar listeners could recognize the same musical hand across different acoustic rigs is an open research question, not a finding.

The possibility nevertheless changes how KLAAT can be understood. Its identity may not be entirely contained in the voice of the agave or the hide. It may also exist in the manner in which a body places continuity and interruption in relation.

An instrument has a voice. A performer develops a way of speaking.

THE ROOM HAS MANY CENTERS

A performance takes place in one room. It does not sound the same everywhere within it.

Direct sound and reflections combine differently as distance and position change. Low-frequency pressure may accumulate in one region and diminish in another. The balance between sustained tone and struck membrane can shift without any change in the instruments.

The person beside the performer, the person near a wall, and the person across the room occupy different acoustic positions. None hears a complete description of what all the others receive.

This makes the room less like a container than a distribution of possible encounters. A microphone placed at one point captures one perspective on that distribution, not the room in its entirety.

The room is an instrument only in the sense that it changes what reaches each listener. It does not promise one universal place from which the work can be perfectly heard.

THE INCOMPLETE RECORD

A recording survives after an event has ended. It can preserve a remarkable amount of acoustic detail, and it permits the same passage to be examined again.

What it preserves is a signal received by a particular microphone and shaped by a particular capture path. The original event also included direct contact vibration, the position of the performer, the distribution of sound through the room, and the sensory access of people occupying different places.

Microphone placement, automatic gain adjustment, clipping, and speech-oriented processing can change the recorded balance of Drone and Pulse. A faint attack may be missed. A sustained tone may be attenuated. Neither possibility should be asserted about a specific recording without checking how it was made.

The record is neither false nor complete. It is evidence of an event that occupied more physical and perceptual space than one stored signal can contain.

The event ends. Its surviving trace remains partial.

THE SECOND INSTRUMENT OF PLAYBACK

A stored sound must pass through another system to be heard again.

Speakers and headphones have their own frequency responses. Rooms contribute new reflections. Stereo channels may be summed, processed, delayed, or filtered. A device incapable of reproducing the lowest part of the Drone may deliver a much thinner encounter than the original room did.

The source file has not changed. The signal arriving at the ear may have.

This is why the recorded performance, the delivered sound, and the listener's account are separate things. A statement that a passage sounded heavy, distant, sharp, or enveloping may describe the original music, the capture, the playback system, the listening environment, or several at once.

KLAAT can retain its artistic identity through translation without promising identical sensation across every device. Playback is not a neutral window. It participates in the encounter.

THE IMAGE THAT HEARD THE SOUND

A visible gesture can change how an acoustic event is understood.

Seeing a hand approach the drum may strengthen the impression of an attack. The effort of a body sustaining the Drone may suggest intensity or strain that cannot be read directly from the sound. A listener watching video receives more than the audio track contains.

The image can enrich the event. It can also make it harder to tell which judgments arose from hearing and which from seeing. An audio-only encounter and an audiovisual encounter are not equivalent forms of evidence.

KLAAT's diagrams introduce another form of visibility. Their lines and nodes describe relationships: continuity, arrival, density, separation, return. They are not pictures of invisible forces proven to act upon every listener. They are a visual grammar through which an artistic structure can be examined.

A plate can map an intention. It cannot substitute for the sound.

THE OPEN INQUIRY

KLAAT / BOOK II / LEAF XVII

THE NUMBER AND THE EXPERIENCE

An interval can be measured. A recorded attack can be located. The energy of a frequency band can be estimated, and a rhythmic fluctuation can be described in mathematical terms.

These measurements matter because they discipline attention to what is actually present in the record. But the number and the experience answer different questions.

A modulation peak does not establish the beat a listener hears. A strong bass component does not prove bodily vibration. A reported trance-like experience does not identify a neural mechanism. A change in physiological activity does not, by itself, identify the acoustic event that caused it.

The distinction does not diminish an unusual experience. It prevents the experience from being assigned an explanation that the available evidence cannot support.

For KLAAT, research is not an authority over improvisation. It is a means of learning how to ask more exact questions of what the music has already done.

THE FIELD REMAINS OPEN

A long inquiry can make an artistic practice more precise without bringing it to an ending.

The accumulated research has examined vibration, rhythmic organization, breath, expectation, attention, performer effort, room acoustics, capture, playback, and the uncertainty of human reports. Some familiar explanations have become less certain. Some questions have become more exact. Others have divided into several distinct problems that demand different kinds of evidence.

This is not a failure of understanding. It is the shape of a practice that refuses to confuse conviction with measurement.

The music still begins with a body and two acoustic actions. The performer does not know exactly where the improvisation will lead. The listener arrives with a history the performer cannot control. The room and the receiving equipment alter what is heard.

The work does not become complete when every mechanism has been named. Another performance can begin. Another listener can arrive. Another relationship can emerge.

The Drone is sustained again. The Pulse returns. What happens between them remains undecided.
